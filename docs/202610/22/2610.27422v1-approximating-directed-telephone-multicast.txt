# ABSTRACT 

In the telephone model a message starts at a source vertex, and in every round each informed vertex may call one neighbour. Telephone multicast asks for a schedule that informs a given set of k

terminals in the fewest rounds. On directed graphs, the best known ratio for this problem was ˜O(√k),due to Elkin and Kortsarz and to Hathcock, Kortsarz and Ravi. For every integer ℓ ≥ 2, we give a deterministic combinatorial algorithm with running time nO(ℓ)

that computes a schedule of length O(ℓ3 log k) · (OPT + k1/ℓ ), where n is the number of vertices and OPT the optimum. Since OPT ≥ log 2(k + 1) , this gives ratio O(kϵ) in time nO(1 /ϵ ) for every fixed ϵ > 0, and ratio O(log 4 k) in quasi-polynomial time. The method rests on jump trees with additive vertex loads , a height reduction that bounds the load of every vertex, and a recursive greedy algorithm with an exponential potential. We also show that directed multicast is a special case of the degree-bounded directed Steiner tree problem on a time-expanded digraph. With known algorithms for that problem this gives randomized ratios O(log 2 n) in time nO(log n) and O(nϵ) in time nO(1 /ϵ ),which, unlike ours, grow with n.On the hardness side, directed multicast stays (1 − ϵ) ln k-hard even when OPT = O(log k), the regime in which the previous algorithms were weakest. arXiv:2610.27422v1 [cs.DS] 20 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

In the telephone model a message spreads through a network in synchronous rounds, and in every round each node that knows it can pass it on to one neighbour. How fast can it reach all nodes, or a given set of nodes? These are the telephone broadcast and multicast problems, classical problems of information dissemination [HHL88]. On undirected graphs the best approximation ratio is O(log k/ log log k) for multicast to k terminals, hence O(log n/ log log n) for broadcast, against a lower bound of 3 − ϵ. On directed graphs, which model links that work in one direction, the gaps have been much wider: for multicast the best ratio was about √k in the worst case, against a lower bound of ln k, and for broadcast it is O(log n), against a lower bound of Ω( √log n) unless NP ⊆ DTIME (nO(log n)). This paper narrows the multicast gap: in polynomial time the ratio drops to O(kϵ), and the lower bound ln k holds even on instances with a small optimum, where the previous algorithms were weakest. 

The model. Let G = ( V, E ) be a directed graph with n = |V | vertices and a source s ∈ V . Communication proceeds in synchronous rounds 1, 2, . . . . At the start only s is informed . In each round, every informed vertex may call at most one of its out-neighbours; a vertex that is called becomes informed at the end of the round and may make calls from the next round on. A schedule lists the calls. In directed telephone multicast (DTM ) we are also given a set K ⊆ V \ { s}

of k terminals , all reachable from s, and we want a schedule that informs every terminal; the other vertices may be used as relays or left uninformed. The objective is the number of rounds until the last terminal is informed, and OPT 

denotes its minimum. Directed telephone broadcast (DTB ) is the case K = V \ { s}. The undirected problems are the special cases in which G is symmetric. In the k-multicast problem the input contains t ≥ k terminals, and any k of them must be informed. 

The k-multicast problem. Hathcock, Kortsarz and Ravi [HKR26] studied k-multicast as a step toward closing the gap between multicast on undirected and on directed graphs. In approximability, undirected k-multicast lies between the two. A ratio ρ for undirected k-multicast is also one for undirected multicast, the case t = k. Conversely, as they observe using a reduction of Kortsarz and Nutov [KN22] for degree-bounded trees, a ratio ρ for directed multicast gives ratio ρ · polylog( k) for undirected k-multicast. Directed k-multicast contains all these problems. The problem also arises in keeping replicated data consistent [HKR26]: when an update must reach copies of a database at any k of t

candidate sites, choosing the k sites that can be updated fastest is k-multicast. Telephone broadcast is NP-hard and solvable in linear time on trees [SCH81]. It is NP-hard already on cactus graphs of pathwidth 2, and constant-factor approximations exist for cactus graphs and for graphs of bounded pathwidth [AKSJS25]. Its approximability on general graphs has been studied for three decades.

1.1 Technical overview 

We now sketch the main ideas, in the order in which the paper develops them. 

Loads instead of degrees. A schedule is an out-arborescence together with an order of the calls at every vertex (Section 2). An arborescence of out-degree d and height h that contains the terminals can be scheduled within 

d log 2 k/ log 2 d + h rounds (Lemma 2.2). So it suffices to find such an arborescence with small out-degree and height. The out-degree is a maximum over vertices, which is hard to control directly. We relax the arborescence to a jump tree :a tree of depth H = 2 ℓ − 1 whose edges carry jumps , walks in G of at most D arcs. The load of a vertex is the number of jumps that leave it. It bounds the out-degree of the union of the jumps and, unlike the out-degree, it is a sum. A jump tree with maximum load Λ ≥ k1/ℓ gives a schedule of length at most ℓΛ + HD .

Height reduction for every vertex. The arborescence of an optimal schedule has out-degree and height at most 

OPT . Lemma 3.4 turns it into a jump tree of depth 2ℓ − 1 in which every vertex has load O(ℓ(OPT + k1/ℓ )) . Level by level, a vertex becomes a hub once ρ = ⌈k1/ℓ ⌉ terminals or hubs of the previous level have been passed up to it, and the source is always a hub. So after ℓ − 1 levels at most ρ + 1 hubs remain, and a vertex that is not a hub is passed by fewer than ρ items per level. The height reduction of Zelikovsky [Zel97], which [CCC +99] uses and Helvig, Robins and Zelikovsky [HRZ01] corrected, bounds the total cost; here the bound holds for every vertex. 

Finding a jump tree. Unfolding G into a tree U of depth H with nO(ℓ) nodes turns the search for a jump tree into a group Steiner tree problem on a tree, with the maximum load as objective. We use a variant of the recursive greedy algorithm of [CCC +99] and of Chekuri, Even and Kortsarz [CEK06]: a candidate at a node is a jump to a child together with any prefix of the list of structures (partial jump trees) that one recursive call at the child returns. So no demands are guessed for the recursive calls, and no single cutoff of their lists is chosen in advance. Every candidate is charged the increase of the potential P 

> v∈V

(eL(v)/Γ − 1) of the loads L, as in online load balancing [AAF +97]. A direct analysis loses a factor ℓ + log k more than our bound. Two observations remove it. In the prefix of a recursive call that the analysis uses, only the structures before the last one raise the cost of later ones, and they cover few terminals. At the top, comparing each iteration with the current potential rather than the final one moves the harmonic sum of greedy covering into the potential. The loads end up O(ℓ log k) times those of the height-reduced optimal jump tree, which gives Theorem 3.1. 

Degree-bounded Steiner trees. Expanding G over T rounds, with a node for every vertex and round and a node for every possible call, turns a schedule into an out-arborescence of the expanded digraph in which every vertex node has at most two children and every call node one (Definition 3.11). Conversely, an out-arborescence in which every call node has at most β children can be replayed as a schedule of length at most βT (Theorem 3.12). So a bicriteria algorithm for degree-bounded directed Steiner tree yields an algorithm for DTM , and the one of [GKL +22] gives a randomized ratio 

O(log 2 n) in quasi-polynomial time. 

Hardness. The lower bounds start from Set Cover. In Fraigniaud’s gadget [Fra01] the source calls set vertices, and each set vertex informs its elements through a tree of logarithmic height. A cover of size c gives a schedule of length 

c + O(log m), and in NO instances every cover is (1 − ϵ) ln m times larger. There the optimum is at least c, because the source makes one call per round. In our hub gadget the source first informs c/ℓ hubs, each of which calls ℓ set vertices. This keeps OPT = O(log k) and the gap (1 − ϵ) ln k (Theorem 4.2). 

Limitations. For fixed ℓ the ratio O(ℓ3(log k + k1/ℓ )) is polynomial in k. A polylogarithmic ratio needs ℓ =Ω(log k/ log log k), and hence time nΩ(log k/ log log k), as for directed Steiner tree, and whether polynomial time suffices is open (Section 5). For broadcast, k = n − 1, and our bounds are weaker than the O(log n) of Elkin 

4and Kortsarz [EK05]. Our hardness result for multicast is within the logarithmic regime: it does not exclude a polylogarithmic ratio in polynomial time. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

This section fixes notation and collects the facts that later sections use: how schedules correspond to out-arborescences, two ways to schedule a given arborescence, and the form of the hardness of Set Cover from which our reductions start. 

Notation. Throughout, G = ( V, E ) is a digraph with n vertices and set of arcs E(G) = E, s is the source and K

the set of k terminals; for broadcast K = V \ { s}. For undirected graphs every edge is read as two opposite arcs. The distance dist( u, v ) is the length of a shortest directed path. An out-arborescence rooted at r is a subdigraph that contains, for each of its vertices v, a unique directed path from r to v. Its height is the largest number of arcs on such a path, and its out-degree is the largest number of children of a vertex. For a vertex v of an arborescence, d(v) is its number of children. All logarithms without a base are natural or to base 2 where it does not matter. 

Schedules and arborescences. In a schedule, call the vertex that informs v the parent of v. The arcs from parents to their children form an out-arborescence rooted at s that contains K, and the schedule is determined, up to useless calls, by this arborescence and by the order in which every vertex calls its children. Conversely, every out-arborescence T

rooted at s with an order of the children of each vertex defines a schedule: every vertex calls its children in the given order, starting in the round after it is informed. For a fixed T , an optimal order can be computed in polynomial time by the tree algorithm of Slater, Cockayne and Hedetniemi [SCH81]: process T bottom-up and let every vertex call first the child whose subtree needs the most rounds. The difficulty therefore lies in choosing T (Figure 1). We use the following basic facts.

Set Cover. An instance consists of a universe of m elements and sets S1, . . . , S N ; a cover is a family of sets whose union is the universe. All our reductions start from the following form of the hardness of Set Cover. Feige [Fei98] proved it unless NP ⊆ DTIME (nO(log log n)), and the reduction of Moshkovitz [Mos15] with the parallel repetition theorem of Dinur and Steurer [DS14] makes it NP-hard. This reduction goes through Label Cover; the cover in the completeness case has one set for every vertex on one side of the Label Cover graph, and the number of sets, the number of elements and the size of this cover are all polynomially related to the size of the Label Cover instance. 

Theorem 2.3 ([Mos15, DS14]) . For every ϵ > 0 there is a constant C such that the following is NP-hard. Given a Set Cover instance with m elements and N ≤ mC sets, and an integer c ≥ m1/C , distinguish the case that a cover of size 

c exists from the case that every cover has more than (1 − ϵ)c ln m sets. 

# 3 Directed multicast: ratio k1/ℓ in time nO(ℓ)

This section presents our algorithm for directed multicast. Its guarantee is the following. 

Theorem 3.1. For every integer ℓ ≥ 2 there is a deterministic algorithm with running time nO(ℓ) that returns a DTM 

schedule of length O(ℓ3 log k) · (OPT + k1/ℓ ). The same holds for directed k-multicast, with OPT its optimum. 

The choice of ℓ trades the running time against the ratio: 

Corollary 3.2. Since OPT ≥ log 2(k + 1) , the ratio is O(ℓ3(log k + k1/ℓ )) . In particular, deterministically: (a) for every fixed ϵ > 0, ratio O(kϵ) in time nO(1 /ϵ ), which is polynomial; (b) with ℓ = ⌈log 2 k⌉, ratio O(log 4 k) in time nO(log k); with ℓ = ⌈log k/ log log k⌉, ratio O(log 4 k/ (log log k)3)

in time nO(log k/ log log k);(c) directed k-multicast, and hence undirected k-multicast, has ratio O(kϵ) in time nO(1 /ϵ ) and ratio O(log 4 k)

in time nO(log k).

The ratios depend on k only; n enters only the running time. The proof uses height reduction as in Zelikovsky [Zel97] and [CCC +99], an unfolding into a tree, and a variant of the recursive greedy algorithm of [CCC +99] and of Chekuri, Even and Kortsarz [CEK06], combined with the exponential potential of online load balancing [AAF +97]. Three points are new. We bound loads , the number of times jumps leave a vertex of G, instead of out-degrees; loads are additive, so greedy covering applies. Our height reduction bounds the load of every vertex of G, not only the total cost. And our analysis of the greedy algorithm loses only a factor O(ℓ log k) in the loads, whereas a straightforward analysis of greedy covering with an exponential potential loses an extra factor ℓ + log k; Section 3.6 explains how our analysis avoids it. Section 3.1 gives an overview of the algorithm and the role of its parameters. Sections 3.2–3.4 define it precisely, in pseudocode as Algorithms 1 and 2, and Sections 3.5 and 3.6 analyse it. 

73.1 Overview of the algorithm 

Before the formal definitions, we describe what the algorithm does and why each of its ingredients is needed. 

What we look for. An optimal schedule informs the terminals along an out-arborescence whose height and out-degree are at most OPT . Conversely, an arborescence of height h and out-degree d that contains the terminals can be scheduled within d log 2 k/ log 2 d + h rounds (Lemma 2.2). So it suffices to find an arborescence with small height and small out-degree. The difficulty is the out-degree: it is a maximum over the vertices of G, and greedy covering algorithms control sums, not maxima. 

Jump trees, and the parameters H, D and Λ. We therefore look for a jump tree : a tree of small depth H whose edges are jumps , walks in G of at most D arcs. The load of a vertex of G counts the jumps that leave it. Loads add up over jumps, so a covering algorithm can control them, and the out-degree of the union of the jumps is at most the maximum load Λ. This union has height at most HD , so the jump tree yields a schedule of length about ℓΛ + HD 

(Lemma 3.6). The jump length D stands for the height of an optimal arborescence: we guess D = T for T = 1 , 2, . . . ,and the analysis uses the guess T = OPT . Guesses T ≥ k are not needed, since for OPT ≥ k a shortest-path schedule is already a 2-approximation (Lemma 3.7). 

The parameter ℓ. The height reduction (Lemma 3.4) shows that the optimal arborescence can be cut into a jump tree of depth H = 2 ℓ − 1 with loads at most about ℓ(OPT + k1/ℓ ). It cuts in ℓ levels, and each level divides the number of terminals by ρ = ⌈k1/ℓ ⌉, which costs an additive ρ in the loads. A larger ℓ makes this additive term smaller, but the search more expensive: jump trees of depth H live in an unfolding of G, a tree with nO(ℓ) nodes, in which finding a jump tree becomes a group Steiner tree problem on a tree. This is where the running time nO(ℓ) comes from. 

The potential and its scale Γ. To keep the maximum load small, every choice is charged the increase of the potential

The recursive greedy algorithm. The jump tree is built top down on the unfolding. A call at a node σ receives a demand , the number of terminals it should cover, and the set of terminals not yet covered. It repeatedly adds the

The top call covers all k terminals. 

Where the factors come from. The final bound O(ℓ3 log k)(OPT + k1/ℓ ) collects four factors: ℓ in the loads of the height-reduced optimal jump tree, H = 2 ℓ − 1 in the scale Γ, log k from greedy covering (the potential grows by a polynomial in k), and ℓ when the jump tree is turned into a schedule. 

3.2 Jump trees 

In this subsection we define jump trees, the structures that the algorithm searches for instead of schedules, and the procedure that turns a jump tree into a schedule. Fix an integer D ≥ 1, which bounds the number of arcs of every jump. 

Definition 3.3. A jump tree of depth H is a rooted tree J of depth at most H whose nodes are labelled by vertices of 

G; a vertex of G may label several nodes. The root is labelled s, every terminal labels at least one node, and every edge of J from a node labelled u to a child labelled w carries a jump , which is a walk in G from u to w with at most D arcs (the empty walk if w = u). Loads are defined on the vertices of G, not on the nodes of J: the load LJ (v) of a vertex 

v ∈ V is the number of pairs (jump of J, arc of that jump that leaves v), that is, the number of times the jumps on all edges of J leave v. Finally, Λ( J) = max v∈V LJ (v).If two children of a node have the same label, we may keep one of them, with its jump, and move the subtree of the other below it. This does not increase loads or depth. So we may assume that siblings have distinct labels. Figure 4 shows an example.

3.3 The unfolding 

To search for a jump tree of depth H, we unfold G into a tree whose nodes are the possible sequences of labels on a path from the root. Let H = 2 ℓ − 1. The unfolding U = UH,D is the rooted tree whose nodes are the sequences σ = ( w0, . . . , w j ) with 

w0 = s, 0 ≤ j ≤ H and dist( wi−1, w i) ≤ D for all i (we allow wi = wi−1). The parent of σ is obtained by dropping 

wj , and the label of σ is λ(σ) = wj . It has at most 2nH nodes. A jump tree of depth H whose siblings have distinct labels is, as a labelled tree, a subtree of U containing the root, together with a jump for every non-root node. Finding a jump tree is therefore a group Steiner tree problem on U: choose a subtree containing the root and, for every terminal t,a node of the group {σ : λ(σ) = t}. Instead of a cost, the objective is the maximum load (Figure 5). Appending to a node labelled t a chain of nodes labelled t, with empty jumps, changes neither the loads nor the depth. So we may assume that every terminal labels a node at depth exactly H, that is, a leaf of U, and we let only leaves cover terminals.

3.4 The algorithm 

We now present the algorithm. A recursive greedy procedure builds a jump tree on the unfolding (Algorithm 1), and an outer loop tries the parameters and turns the jump trees into schedules (Algorithm 2). We first define the partial jump trees that the procedure builds and the potential that guides it. 

Structures. A structure rooted at a node σ of U is a finite rooted tree with root σ whose nodes are nodes of U (a node of U may occur several times); each non-root node is a child in U of its parent and carries a jump from the label of its parent to its own label: a path with at most D arcs, or the empty path if the labels agree. The main algorithm. The function M ULTICAST (Algorithm 2) solves both problems. It receives the instance with its set K of terminals, the number k of terminals to inform, and the integer ℓ ≥ 2; for DTM k = |K|, and for k-multicast

Finally it returns the best schedule. Larger values of T are not needed: when OPT ≥ k, the first schedule is already a 2-approximation (Lemma 3.7).

In this subsection we prove Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.2. We bound the length of the two kinds of schedules that Algorithm 2 compares, show that one iteration of the recursive greedy algorithm chooses a cheap structure (Lemma 3.9) and that the top call finds a jump tree with small loads (Lemma 3.10), and combine these bounds. As for the recursive greedy algorithm of [CCC +99], which Chekuri, Even and Kortsarz [CEK06] analyse for group Steiner tree on trees, a candidate of a node of height h has density within a factor O(h) of the optimum (Lemma 3.9). The objective, the maximum load, is a maximum over the vertices of G rather than a sum, which is why a candidate costs the increase of an exponential potential, as in online load balancing [AAF +97]. The loads end up at most O(Γ log k),and two observations allow the scale Γ = Θ( HΛ) . In the prefix of a recursive call that the analysis uses, the structures before the last one cover only a 1/h fraction of what the optimum covers there, and spend only O(1) times its cost; only they raise the cost of a later structure. At the top, comparing every iteration with the current potential instead of the final one turns the harmonic sum of greedy covering into a factor kO(1) inside the potential, which costs only O(log k)

in the loads.

3.7 A second route: degree-bounded Steiner trees 

This subsection presents a second algorithm for DTM , by a reduction to a Steiner tree problem with degree bounds. Its ratio depends on n rather than on k, so it complements Theorem 3.1. In the degree-bounded directed Steiner tree problem (DB-DST) the input is a digraph with arc costs c ≥ 0, a root, a set of k terminals and a bound dv ≥ 0 for every vertex v. A solution is an out-arborescence rooted at the root that contains all terminals and in which every vertex v has at most dv children; its cost, the sum of the costs of its arcs, is to be minimized. An (α, β )-bicriteria algorithm returns, whenever a solution exists, an out-arborescence rooted at the root that contains all terminals, has cost (for a randomized algorithm, expected cost) at most α times the optimum and gives every vertex v at most βd v children. Guo, Kortsarz, Laekhanukit, Li, Vaz and Xian [GKL +22] give a randomized 

(O(log n log k), O (log 2 n)) -bicriteria algorithm with running time nO(log n), where n is the number of vertices. We show that DTM is the special case of DB-DST with zero costs on a time-expanded digraph, in which a violation of the degree bounds by a factor β costs at most a factor β in the number of rounds. 

Overview. The idea is to make time explicit. For a guess T of the optimum, the time-expanded digraph has a copy of every vertex for every round, and a node for every call that a vertex can make in a round. A schedule then becomes an out-arborescence in which every round of every vertex either waits or makes one call, which are degree bounds 2 and 1,and the costs play no role. So a DB-DST algorithm can search for the schedule. It may exceed the degree bounds by a factor β, that is, ask a vertex for β calls in one round; the procedure C ONVERT spreads these calls over consecutive rounds, which stretches every informing time by the factor β and no more.

# 4 Hardness of directed multicast 

This section proves our lower bound: directed multicast stays (1 − ϵ) ln k-hard even when OPT = O(log k) (Theo-rem 4.2). The reduction starts from Set Cover, through the gadgets below. 

4.1 Gadgets 

In this subsection we describe the gadgets from which our reduction is built, and bound the optimum of the hub gadget (Lemma 4.1). A gadget lets the source choose sets, and every chosen set then informs its elements. All gadgets use out-trees in which every vertex has at most two children. In such a tree, if every vertex calls its children in the two rounds after it is informed, a vertex at depth d is informed within 2d rounds after the root; a tree with at most m leaves can be chosen with height ⌈log 2 m⌉.Both gadgets use set trees . For a set Sj , its set tree is an out-tree rooted at a vertex xj , whose leaves are the terminals of the elements of Sj , whose other vertices are new, and in which every vertex has at most two children. Its height is at most ⌈log 2 m⌉ + 1 , so it is informed within 2⌈log 2 m⌉ + 2 rounds after xj . Element terminals have no out-arcs. A terminal can be informed only through the set tree of a set that contains it. 

Fraigniaud’s gadget [Fra01] (Figure 11) has a source s, a vertex xj with the arc (s, x j ) for every set, and the set trees. Fraigniaud states his reduction for another communication model, in which a call may follow a path and simultaneous calls use vertex-disjoint paths, and under the assumption NP ̸ ⊆ DTIME (nO(log log n)). He uses binomial trees as set trees and attaches about log m/ϵ copies of the digraph to the source, which makes the additive O(log m) negligible; one copy suffices here, since c ≥ m1/C in Theorem 2.3. His schedules for the YES case use only single arcs, and every schedule of the telephone model is one of his model, so his argument applies verbatim. The source calls one set vertex per round, and the set vertices informed by round T must form a cover. Hence 

OPT ≤ c + 2 ⌈log 2 m⌉ + 2 if a cover of size c exists, and OPT ≥ c′ if every cover has at least c′ sets. With Theorem 2.3 this gives (1 − ϵ) ln k-hardness of DTM , by combining Fraigniaud’s reduction with the NP-hardness of Set Cover. These instances have a large optimum: in the NO case OPT > (1 − ϵ)c ln m ≥ m1/C . The hub gadget (Figure 12) spreads the calls over many vertices and so brings OPT down to O(log k).In Fraigniaud’s gadget the source calls the set vertices itself, so the optimum is at least the size of a cover, which is polynomial in m. Our hub gadget delegates these calls to many hubs, which makes the optimum logarithmic and keeps the gap. # 5 Open problems 

We close with the main questions that remain open. • Directed multicast. In polynomial time the ratio is now O(kϵ), against the lower bound (1 − ϵ) ln k. This is the situation of directed Steiner tree. Is there a polylogarithmic ratio in polynomial time? By Corollary 3.2(b), any NP-hardness of a ratio ω(log 4 k), under polynomial reductions, would put NP into quasi-polynomial time. In quasi-polynomial time the ratio is O(log 2 n) (Corollary 3.13). By Theorem 3.12, a degree violation 

O(log n) for degree-bounded directed Steiner tree would give DTM ratio O(log n), within a constant of the lower bound when k ≥ nΩ(1) ; the algorithm of [GKL +22] achieves degree violation O(log n) for its special case, the degree-bounded group Steiner tree problem on trees. • Directed broadcast. The ratio O(log n) against Ω( √log n) of Elkin and Kortsarz [EK05] is unchanged; for 

k = n − 1 our bounds are weaker.

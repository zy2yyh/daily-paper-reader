# BIER-DC: Stateless Multicast for In-Orbit Space Data Centers in LEO Constellations 

Mostafa Abdollahi, Wenjun Yang, Kousar Malekinasab, and Jianping Pan 

University of Victoria, BC, Canada Email: {abdolahi68, wenjunyang, kovvy, pan }@uvic.ca 

Abstract —Emerging data-intensive space applications generate large volumes of data that must be processed, stored, replicated, and delivered efficiently. However, relying on terrestrial cloud regions can expose satellite data to high delivery latency and loss of timeliness due to intermittent satellite-to-ground visibility and limited access to ground infrastructure. Space Data Cen-ters (SDCs), which organize collaborative satellites into logical compute and storage clusters, offer a promising approach for in-orbit data management and service continuity. In this paper, we first evaluate how satellite clustering with different sizes affects satellite visibility and gateway outage. We then propose BIER-DC, a stateless one-to-many communication protocol tailored for satellite-based SDCs. Unlike terrestrial multicast protocols designed for stable data center fabrics, BIER-DC decouples inter-SDC forwarding from intra-SDC delivery and encodes compact cluster-level forwarding information in packet headers. Comparative evaluations against BIER, BIER-TE, and Yeti show that BIER-DC significantly reduces packet-header length and byte-hop overhead, improving effective goodput and scalability for next-generation LEO space data centers. 

Index Terms —Space data center, multicasting, LEO satellite networks. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Modern data-intensive Earth-observation (EO) missions, in-cluding high-resolution optical, hyperspectral, and SAR sens-ing, can generate terabyte-scale daily data depending on the payload and observation mode. Recent estimates suggest that global EO collections are growing by roughly 100 PB per year and may exceed 1400 PB by 2030 [1]. Yet data backhaul is limited by satellite-to-ground visibility, ground infrastructure, and variable downlink capacity. After downlink, data may still traverse long terrestrial paths from gateways to remote cloud or data center regions, adding latency and routing inefficiency. This bottleneck is acute in traditional EO architectures, where ground segments may capture as little as 2% of available hyperspectral observations [2]. Although broadband mega-constellations show that dense gateway deployments improve ground access, bent-pipe and terrestrial-cloud models still constrain timely processing and dissemination [3]. Onboard filtering and in-space computing can reduce this dependency. Prior work shows that, for a 1,000-satellite constellation, downlinking only useful data reduces required ground-station sites from 112 to 6, about a 95% reduction [3]. To address the limits of terrestrial-cloud and bent-pipe architectures, companies and startups are exploring space-based data centers with high-capability satellites and orbital platforms. Starcloud has launched a satellite with data-center-class GPU compute, Google’s Project Suncatcher studies TPU-equipped solar-powered satellites with optical links for in-space AI, and Lonestar Data Holdings has demonstrated space-based storage for disaster recovery and long-term resilience [4]–[7]. These efforts move computation and storage closer to space-generated data. However, space data centers differ from terrestrial ones, which rely on fixed racks, servers, top-of-rack and aggregation switches, and spine or leaf-spine fabrics [8], [9]. In space, each satellite acts as a rack-like unit with processing, memory, storage, VM or container hosting, and packet forwarding, but without a stable switch-centric fabric. As a result, existing data center communication protocols be-come inefficient. Terrestrial replication, cache updates, service synchronization, and multicast often rely on switch-resident forwarding state across hierarchical switches [10], [11]. In space, there are no dedicated top-of-rack or spine switches, and connectivity depends on resource-constrained satellites and dynamic inter-satellite links. Maintaining per-flow, per-group, or per-replica state across many satellites can therefore consume valuable onboard memory, processing, and control bandwidth. Space data centers require one-to-many communication for replication, fleet-wide management, and distributed learning. As in terrestrial systems that replicate data across data cen-ters and racks for availability [12], replication is critical in space to tolerate radiation effects, solar events, and hardware anomalies. Multicast supports fleet-wide firmware, software, configuration, and security updates [13], as well as satellite federated learning, where intra-orbit ISLs with partial in-network aggregation achieve a 29 × training speed-up and an 8 × reduction in parameter-server traffic [14]. However, existing protocols scale poorly in orbit. IP multicast requires per-flow state and frequent control updates [15], [16], while BIER, BIER-TE, and Yeti incur headers that grow with receivers, forwarding adjacencies, or network structure [17]– [19]. Terrestrial schemes such as Elmo assume stable Clos topologies and programmable switches [10], [20], [21], which do not match dynamic ISL networks. Thus, space data centers need a lightweight stateless multicast design whose header overhead is decoupled from constellation scale. To address the limitations of existing multicast schemes in orbital environments, we carry out our study in three steps. Second, we design BIER-DC for stateless inter-SDC mul-ticast. This allows us to measure header overhead, byte-hop overhead, scalability, and the impact of header growth on payload capacity, packet deliv-ery, and goodput. Compared with existing multicast schemes, SDC-level encoding keeps headers compact, preserves more payload space, improves goodput, and maintains stateless one-to-many forwarding . Cloud access is geographically uneven. Terrestrial cloud data centers can expose users to long access latency because intercontinental traffic largely depends on submarine fiber-optic cables, which carry over 99% of international data traffic [22]. To quantify this limitation, we use the public cloud–edge latency dataset of Charyyev et al. [23], which reports RTT measurements from RIPE Atlas probes to cloud locations. For each probe, we compute the nearest-cloud latency as the minimum RTT to any measured cloud data center and group the results by source region. As shown in Fig. 1, Europe and North America achieve the lowest access latency, while South America and especially Africa experience much higher RTTs. This result shows that access to terrestrial cloud data centers remains geographically uneven even under an optimistic nearest-cloud assignment. This motivates SDCs, which enable satellite-generated data to be processed and disseminated cooperatively in orbit, closer to its source and with reduced dependence on geographically uneven terrestrial cloud access. 

Single satellites provide only short service opportuni-ties. Emerging space applications such as Earth observation and in-space AI generate large data volumes, including the 

∼100 TB/day produced by NASA’s SWOT and NISAR mis-sions [24]. However, downlink remains a major bottleneck: prior work reports that ground infrastructure can capture only about 2% of available observations [2]. A single satellite is also not a practical service unit because its compute, storage, and connectivity are available only intermittently as it moves along its orbit. To illustrate this limitation, over representative equatorial, mid-latitude, and polar H3 cells [25], together with Starlink gateways and OneWeb Satellite Network Portals (SNPs) [26], shown in Fig. 2, we conduct a two-line element (TLE)-driven analysis. Fig. 3 shows that an individual satellite remains over a target H3 cell for only a few minutes, with typical median dwell times around 3–6 minutes across OneWeb and Starlink shells. Such short service windows are insufficient for persistent workloads including multi-stage processing, AI inference pipelines, caching, and replication, because unfinished tasks must be checkpointed, migrated, or delayed until another satellite becomes available.

Solution methodology and scale selection. Our fixed-K

Therefore, we use a scalable heuristic to construct compact and connected SDC clusters. A. BIER-DC Overview 

We present BIER-DC, a stateless multicast architecture for in-orbit space data centers. As shown in Fig. 5, BIER-DC organizes satellites into logical space data center clusters, called SDCs, and separates multicast forwarding into two lev-els: inter-SDC delivery across clusters and intra-SDC delivery inside each cluster. 

1) Design principles: The design of BIER-DC follows three primary goals. First, compact multicast headers. Since satel-lite links have limited bandwidth and energy budget, BIER-DC should avoid per-receiver or per-VM bit positions in the inter-SDC header. Second, scalable stateless forwarding. 

Multicast forwarding should not require per-flow forwarding state at every satellite, especially as the number of destination satellites, VMs, containers, and multicast groups increases. 

Third, hierarchical control. The control plane should match the clustered structure of space data centers: SDC controllers coordinate cluster-level forwarding, while satellite controllers handle local resource management and final service delivery. 

2) Key ideas of BIER-DC: BIER-DC adopts three key ideas to realize these goals: (i) cluster-level addressing. Satellites are grouped into SDCs, denoted as SDC 1 to SDC m, and each SDC is assigned a unique Cluster ID . The Cluster ID is used in control messages, forwarding entries, and BIER-DC packet headers. We use m to denote the number of SDCs and 

n to denote the number of satellites per SDC. This abstraction allows BIER-DC to describe multicast delivery at the SDC level rather than at the individual satellite, VM, or container level, thereby reducing header growth and limiting forwarding-state complexity as the number of service endpoints increases. 

(ii) border-satellite-based inter-SDC forwarding. Instead of encoding every destination satellite or VM in the packet header, BIER-DC encodes compact forwarding entries that identify the destination SDC and the selected border satellite. This allows the source SDC to express the inter-SDC multicast tree using a small number of cluster-level entries. (iii) local header translation at the destination SDC. When a packet reaches a border satellite of a destination SDC, the inter-SDC BIER-DC header is replaced by a local intra-SDC header. The destination SDC then delivers the packet to the selected satellite, VM, container, or data object using local forwarding decisions. 

3) System overview and workflow: The BIER-DC architec-ture consists of a data plane and a control plane . In the data plane, satellites inside each SDC are connected through intra-cluster ISLs, while neighboring SDCs are connected through inter-cluster ISLs. Each satellite may host local computation and storage resources, represented as VMs or containers, enabling in-orbit services such as data replication, caching, processing, and multicast delivery. Each satellite has a local satellite controller, which abstracts func-tions already present in modern onboard systems [28], [29]. BIER-DC extends this role to networking and service man-agement: the satellite controller monitors neighboring ISLs, applies forwarding rules, tracks local resources, manages VM/container placement when onboard edge computing is available, and performs final delivery to the selected VM, container, or data object. Each SDC is managed by an SDC controller that maintains control sessions with the satellite controllers in its cluster. The SDC controller keeps cluster-level state, including the Cluster ID, SDC membership, intra-cluster topology, regis-tered border satellites, aggregate per-satellite capacity, and forwarding entries for intra-SDC and inter-SDC delivery. This design mirrors cloud and edge control planes, where a node-level agent manages local workloads and reports status to a higher-level controller. For example, Kubernetes uses kubelet as the node agent, and KubeEdge extends Kubernetes-native orchestration to edge hosts, including a cloud-native satellite case study [30]–[33]. Accordingly, BIER-DC keeps only an aggregate view at the SDC controller, while fine-grained placement and final delivery decisions remain with satellite controllers. 

Border satellites are the managed entry and exit points of each SDC. They are edge satellites that maintain ISL connec-tions to neighboring SDCs. Each border satellite registers with its local SDC controller, which records its satellite ID, bound-ary face, and reachable neighboring SDCs. Only registered border satellites are used for inter-SDC forwarding; inter-SDC edges incident to non-registered satellites are excluded from the forwarding graph. On egress, a border satellite forwards cloned packets toward the selected neighboring SDC. On ingress, it hands incoming packets to the local intra-SDC de-livery process. If multiple border satellites can reach the same neighboring SDC, BIER-DC selects the lowest-cost candidate. SDC members, including border satellites, are selected from the same or adjacent orbital planes and move in the same direction, so their relative positions in the topology remain approximately stable over extended periods. This stability allows registered border satellites to remain active without fre-quent reselection. Moreover, predictable orbital motion enables inter-SDC border connectivity and scheduled handovers to be precomputed for upcoming topology snapshots. 

B. BIER-DC Header and Addressing 

BIER-DC first requires identifying where a multicast packet should be delivered and how it should cross the space data center topology. Unlike conventional satellite-level multicast encoding, which represents individual satellites or egress nodes in the packet header, BIER-DC uses a cluster-level addressing model. Each SDC is assigned a unique Cluster ID , denoted by CID k, which serves as the basic inter-SDC forwarding identifier. Each satellite also has a local Satellite ID , which is used for intra-SDC delivery, border-satellite registration, and ingress-satellite resolution. Services hosted inside an SDC, such as VMs, containers, storage replicas, or application instances, are identified by a local service or VM identifier. This two-level addressing model keeps inter-SDC forwarding compact while still allowing precise delivery inside each destination SDC. 

Addressing scheme. BIER-DC follows a four-step process to address destinations. Step-I: assigning SDC identifiers. 

Each SDC is assigned a unique cluster identifier and packets are forwarded across the SDC-level topology using these identifiers, instead of carrying a flat satellite-level multicast bitstring. 

Step-II: identifying border satellites. Each SDC maintains a local list of border satellites that connect the SDC to neighboring SDCs. These satellites act as ingress or egress points for inter-SDC forwarding and can be represented by compact local indexes. 

Step-III: identifying internal destinations. Inside each SDC, satellites and hosted services are assigned local identifiers. Therefore, a complete service address can be written as 

[SDC , Border , Satellite , VM] , where the SDC and border fields guide inter-SDC forwarding, while the satellite and VM fields are used for local delivery after the packet reaches the destination SDC. 

Step-IV: maintaining the address mapping. The SDC con-troller maintains the mapping between destination services, internal satellites, and selected border satellites. When the topology or SDC membership changes, these mappings are updated and forwarding assignments are re-evaluated over the updated topology snapshot. Packing compressed addresses into packets. To indicate multicast forwarding, the source SDC packs a compressed destination representation into the BIER-DC header. The destination path encoding is the main scalability feature of BIER-DC. Let m be the number of SDCs. C. Forwarding Mechanism 

BIER-DC forwards multicast packets at two levels: inter-SDC forwarding between space data centers and intra-SDC delivery inside destination SDCs. Inter-SDC forwarding is performed by border satellites, which process the compact path encoding carried in the BIER-DC header. Each remaining destination is associated with an SDC-level path and a selected ingress border satellite for each downstream SDC. When a packet reaches a border satellite, the satellite determines whether the current SDC contains local destinations and whether the packet must continue toward one or more adjacent SDCs. Algorithm 1 gives the border-node forwarding procedure. The algorithm takes as input a packet p with BIER-DC header 

H, the current SDC c, the ingress border satellite bin , and the local SDC controller Γc. It first extracts the set of unresolved destinations Dloc that belong to the current SDC and groups the remaining destinations by their next adjacent SDC into 

Gdown . This grouping allows BIER-DC to send only one packet copy for destinations that share the same next SDC and to replicate packets only when the downstream paths diverge. 

Case 1: Local delivery inside the current SDC. If the current SDC contains one or more destinations, the ingress border satellite queries the local SDC controller to resolve the corresponding destination satellites, services, or VMs. The controller then constructs an intra-SDC header rooted at bin .This local header replaces the inter-SDC BIER-DC header only for the packet copy delivered inside the current SDC. Therefore, local delivery can be handled using compact intra-SDC forwarding information without exposing satellite-level destinations in the inter-SDC header. 

Case 1.1: A single local destination satellite. If the local destination set contains only one destination satellite, no local multicast branching is needed. The border satellite creates a local packet copy, replaces its BIER-DC header with the intra-SDC header, and forwards the packet along the selected local path toward that destination satellite. 

Case 1.2: Multiple local destination satellites. If the current SDC contains multiple local destination satellites, the controller identifies the required local branches from the ingress border satellite. The border satellite then creates one packet copy per local branch and attaches the corresponding intra-SDC header to each copy. Packets are replicated only at the required branching points, which avoids carrying a flat satellite-level bitstring in the inter-SDC header. 

Case 2: Inter-SDC forwarding to downstream adjacent SDCs. After local destinations are processed, the border satellite forwards the packet toward unresolved destinations in downstream SDCs. For each group in Gdown , the border satellite selects the egress border satellite toward the next adjacent SDC and forwards the packet to it using the shortest path inside the current SDC. The egress border satellite then transmits the packet over the inter-SDC link to the selected ingress border satellite in the next SDC. Before forwarding, the packet header is updated to keep only the destinations belonging to that downstream branch, the current SDC entry is consumed, and the TTL is decremented. If the packet has no local destinations and no downstream SDCs, it is dropped. This design separates inter-SDC forwarding from intra-SDC delivery. The inter-SDC header only carries compact SDC-level path information, while detailed satellite-level delivery is resolved locally inside each destination SDC. By Lemma 1, BIER-DC preserves shortest-path routing because it does not recompute independent local routes be-tween SDC borders. Instead, each border-to-border segment is extracted from the original shortest-path tree computed for the current topology snapshot. Therefore, the selected SDC border satellites appear in the same order as they do on the original SPT path from the source ingress satellite to the destination. Concatenating these extracted segments reconstructs the same end-to-end shortest route. Hence, BIER-DC represents the original SPT result at the SDC-border level without loss of routing optimality. 

Handling Failures in BIER-DC. BIER-DC handles satel-lite and ISL failures through snapshot-based recomputation and localized recovery. Because the packet header encodes SDC-border-level decisions derived from the current shortest-path tree, a failed satellite or inter-SDC link may invalidate one or more border-to-border segments. Upon detection, the affected satellite or SDC controller updates its connectivity view, reports the change to the SDC-level control plane, and recomputes the affected SPT-derived segments for the next topology snapshot. During this transition, packets with stale border-level information are either locally detoured toward the same next border satellite if an alternative path exists, or dropped and recovered by upper-layer reliability or source-side retransmission. Because the LEO topology evolves over time, the assignment should be re-evaluated over the updated topology snapshot. V. E VALUATION 

Evaluations in this section focus on answering the following three questions related to BIER-DC. Q1: Can BIER-DC achieve high multicast goodput for data delivery compared with existing multicast forwarding approaches? Q2: Can BIER-DC maintain compact packet headers as the number of VMs or services inside each SDC increases? Q3: How does BIER-DC scale to larger space data center deployments with an increasing number of SDCs? 

Prototype and testbed setup. We evaluate BIER-DC using NetSatBench [34], a container-based LEO emulator that instantiates satellites as independent Docker containers and connects them according to time-varying constellation snap-shots. The snapshots are generated using StarPerf [35], which provides satellite positions, ISL topology, and propagation de-lays for the target constellation. In our prototype, each satellite container additionally runs a lightweight BIER-DC runtime agent that parses BIER-DC headers and forwards multicast packets either to the next hop or to a local VM/service. A centralized controller computes forwarding rules based on the source, destinations, SDC membership, and inter-SDC paths, and installs them through the emulator control channel. For each experiment, a UDP sender in the source container segments the input file according to the available payload after subtracting the protocol header size, while destination receivers record delivery statistics. By varying the number of VMs per satellite and the number of SDCs, we measure header size, raw payload size, packet count, and goodput.

Stateless multicast: BIER [17] encodes the multicast re-ceiver set as a packet-header bitstring, where each bit identi-fies an egress router. Routers use per-interface bitmasks to replicate and forward packets along shortest paths. BIER-TE [18] extends this model by assigning bits to adjacen-cies, enabling traffic-engineered multicast trees, while BIER-FRR [38] adds precomputed backup entries for local failure recovery. Yeti [19] is also stateless, using specialized labels, such as local interface IDs and interface bitmaps, in a dynamic stack that shrinks along the path. Such fine-grained encoding can still incur high header overhead in SDCs with many VMs, containers, and replicas. BIER-Star [39] improves scalability for large LEO systems by using a compact satellite-network abstraction, but it targets satellite access multicast rather than multicast among distributed computing and storage resources inside space data centers. 

Cloud/data center multicast: Elmo [10] and Orca [11] are multicast systems for terrestrial cloud/data center networks. Elmo uses a logically centralized controller to encode mul-ticast forwarding rules in packet headers and exploits the symmetry and short paths of multi-rooted Clos fabrics. Orca targets Clos/leaf-spine data centers, using rack-level server agents and maintaining small exception state at selected spine switches. In SDCs, where each satellite acts as both a rack-like compute unit hosting multiple VMs/containers and a forwarding node connected to neighboring satellites through dynamic ISLs, directly applying Orca would require agent functionality to scale with the number of satellites and would rely on a fixed, port-based leaf/spine/core hierarchy that does not match this distributed satellite architecture. Thus, Orca is not directly suitable for space data centers. BIER-DC differs from conventional BIER and hierarchical data center multicast by separating inter-SDC forwarding from intra-SDC delivery. It aggregates multiple service destinations into compact SDC–border-satellite entries and performs local header translation at destination SDCs, keeping satellite- and VM-level information out of the inter-SDC header while preserving stateless forwarding. This design avoids the fixed Clos-fabric assumptions of Elmo and Orca and is tailored to multicast among distributed compute and storage resources in dynamic LEO SDCs. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented BIER-DC, a compact stateless mul-ticast design for space data centers formed by cooperative LEO satellite clusters. By encoding forwarding at the SDC level through destination-SDC and ingress-border identifiers, rather than individual satellites, VMs, or adjacencies, BIER-DC substantially reduces header overhead and preserves more packet capacity for application payload. Our analysis and NetSatBench-based EO image transfer emulations show that BIER-DC scales more efficiently and achieves higher goodput than BIER, BIER-TE, and Yeti as multicast scope and service density increase. Overall, BIER-DC provides a scalable mul-ticast mechanism for in-orbit data replication, caching, and processing across distributed SDCs. Future work will extend BIER-DC with dynamic failure recovery, traffic-aware desti-nation grouping, and re-evaluation of forwarding assignments over updated topology snapshots. REFERENCES [1] R.

## ABSTRACT 

Network simulation plays an important role in HPC/AI system research by enabling exploration of network architectures, communication strategies, and system configurations at scales that may be impractical to study experimentally. Existing approaches to network simulator calibration and validation lack a principled methodology that combines parameter alignment, constrained infer-ence, and independent validation for collective-centric HPC/AI networks. We present CALIPER, a methodology for systematic calibration and validation of HPC/AI network simulators. CALIPER distinguishes directly observable system parameters from auxiliary parameters which are configured via calibration and constrains the latter to feasible domains consistent with the target system. We evaluate CALIPER’s hierarchical, scale-aware calibration and its effects on parameter identifiability and generalization using collective communication benchmarks on a reference system with up to 64-nodes at the Jülich Supercomputing Centre. Validation results show that the calibrated parameter configurations identified by CALIPER predict system performance with less than 5% mean errors across scale-up and scale-out network domains. CALIPER demonstrates that hierarchical, con-strained calibration can produce simulator configurations whose predictive accuracy persists beyond the observations used for fitting.

## 1 Introduction 

The continued scaling of artificial intelligence (AI) and high-performance computing (HPC) workloads is transforming network performance from a secondary consideration into a first-order system-design constraint [1–3]. Contemporary systems distribute computation and model state across large numbers of compute nodes, making application performance dependent on the interplay between computation and communication. Collective communication primitives [4] are widely used to exchange data among compute nodes in workloads such as distributed training, model serving, and other communication-intensive applications. Consequently, network design is a key consideration in large-scale systems and must be evaluated under realistic workloads and configurations. However, direct hardware experimentation alone cannot support this evaluation. Representative systems are costly to construct and operate whereas available production installations may be unavailable for controlled experiments. Therefore, simulation provides a practical means to evaluate network architectures including implementation of collective algorithms, communication policies, etc. [5–8]. It also provides repeatable and configurable experimental environments in which individual design variables can be isolated [9, 10]. Because measurements on real HPC systems consume scarce compute and network resources, one of the requirements to have an effective methodology to configure and evaluate a network simulator is that it must be based on a deliberately selected, compact benchmark set. Modern HPC/AI simulation studies increasingly report close correspondence with real systems, which establishes that accurate and scalable prediction is achievable [11–16]. However, the methodological path from system measurement to calibrated configuration is not described consistently across the literature [17, 18]. Calibration determines parameter values that reduce misalignment between simulation and selected reference measurements, whereas validation evaluates whether the resulting model provides an acceptable level of accuracy for its intended use and domain of applicability. This distinction is consequential: alignment with the measurements used for calibration demonstrates fit to the calibration data, but does not establish predictive validity. A specific instance of parameters of the simulator may achieve respectable accuracy for one benchmark while failing for a different workload or system scale. Calibration accuracy must consequently be evaluated along several generalization dimensions: unseen message sizes, held-out collectives, alternative workload configurations, and larger system scales [19, 20]. Simulator parameters can be broadly classified into two categories namely, direct parameters that are set based on the target hardware information (e.g. the number of compute nodes) and auxiliary parameters cannot be directly associated with target hardware or are specific to the simulator abstraction (e.g. link latency). During calibration, direct parameters 

must not be tuned to reduce simulation error, as doing so would effectively model a non-existent system. Auxiliary parameters may be tuned during the calibration process and therefore define the calibration search space. Furthermore, the calibrated simulator must be explicitly validated under conditions systematically excluded from calibration, such as different collectives, message sizes, or system scales to assess its predictive capability on unseen workload scenarios. Existing approaches (Refer Section 6) to tune and evaluate network simulators typically do not jointly address simulator independence, generalization, constrained parameter inference, and system validation as shown in Table 1. In this paper, we introduce CALIPER methodology for calibrating network simulators for large scale HPC/AI systems. Specifically, this paper makes the following contributions. • We propose CALIPER, a methodology that combines explicit parameter classification, bounded optimization, and independent validation to calibrate and evaluate network simulators for large scale AI/HPC systems. • We apply CALIPER for calibrating the SST simulator [5] to simulate state-of-the-art network configura-tions (with up-to 64 GPUs) from Jülich Supercomputing Centre. • We show that CALIPER’s hierarchical, scale-aware calibration produces simulator configurations that achieve less than 5% mean completion-time error validated across the evaluated scale-up and scale-out network domains. The reported results demonstrate that employing the proposed CALIPER methodology enables a simulator configuration to retain its accuracy beyond the calibration conditions across collective measurements spanning message sizes, communicator scales, and physical placements. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides the necessary background. Section 3 presents the CALIPER model and its design. Section 4 presents the experimental setup and evaluation results. Section 5 discusses the potential extensions of CALIPER that can be undertaken in future work. ## 2 Background 

Contemporary standalone network simulators as well as distributed AI system simulators compare predicted communi-cation or application performance with measurements from accelerator clusters and supercomputing systems, in some cases at large system scales [9, 11–16, 21–26]. These studies show that useful predictive accuracy can be achieved using a variety of modeling strategies at different abstraction levels, from analytical models to cycle-accurate simulation, each offering a distinct balance between execution speed, scalability, and fidelity of network representation. Table 2 summarizes the taxonomy of simulator types and highlights their respective trade-offs. Analytical models [11–13, 16, 21, 26–28] prioritize speed and scalability at the expense of modelling granularity. Flow/event-based simulators [6, 9, 14, 15, 22–25, 29, 30] explicitly model communication operations and resource sharing while abstracting some packet- or cycle-level behaviour. Cycle-accurate network simulators [5, 7, 8] model router pipelines, buffers, arbitration, links, and flit movement at or near individual clock cycles. However, abstraction level alone does not determine predictive accuracy. A more detailed simulator can represent mechanisms that a coarser model omits, but its predictions remain meaningful only if the simulated workload and model parameters correspond to the target system. Conversely, a compact model can provide accurate predictions within an operating regime in which its assumptions and parameters are appropriate. Predictive accuracy is therefore not an intrinsic property of a simulator category but depends on the calibration and validation of a specific model for its target system and workload. Validation is increasingly reported across both distributed AI simulators [11–16, 26–28, 30–32] and standalone network simulators [5–9, 21–25, 29]. However, calibration procedures are often only partially described or omitted altogether. This lack of information limits the ability to assess, reproduce, and transfer simulation models across system configurations, motivating the need for more systematic calibration methodologies. There also exists considerable diversity in how calibration is performed [13–16, 24, 26, 31]. Consequently, reported prediction accuracy on a selected validation set does not necessarily imply that the calibrated model will retain its predictive fidelity when applied to different calibration parameters, workloads or system configurations. The resulting gap is therefore not an absence of calibration or validation, but rather the fragmented methodological treatment of how calibration should be designed, executed, and evaluated so that the resulting model is both accurate and demonstrably transferable beyond the observations used to fit it. A fundamental limitation of calibration based solely on quality of fit is that different parameter configurations can explain the same finite set of measurements, and low fitting error does not imply that the inferred parameters capture reusable system behavior. Automated search can make this process faster and reduce the effort of manual tuning [17], but optimization alone cannot determine whether a low-error configuration is physically meaningful or whether its apparent accuracy results from compensating errors. This makes simulator configuration consequential. Finally, some real-system behavior may not be modeled by the simulator at all. The methodological issue is therefore not whether simulation parameters can be adjusted to improve error; 1 with measurements. The harder question is how to determine which 

quantities should be adjusted, under what constraints , and what evidence is sufficient to show that the resulting configuration remains predictive beyond the measurements used to obtain it. CALIPER addresses this gap by separating three sources of simulator discrepancy that tuning would otherwise conflate. First, directly observable properties and execution choices are aligned between the target system and the simulation. Second, only explicitly uncertain auxiliary parameters are exposed to calibration. Third, the resulting configuration is frozen and evaluated on structured, held-out workloads [19] and system scales. Residual discrepancies that persist after these stages are not automatically absorbed through further tuning; instead, they provide evidence of workload-representation, implementation, or model fidelity limitations. This separation makes calibration interpretable while providing stronger confidence that the calibrated simulator can generalize beyond calibrated dataset. 

## 3 CALIPER 

In this section, we introduce the CALIPER methodology, shown in Figure 1. CALIPER partitions the simulator configuration into three parameter classes. Direct parameters (DP) 1 , correspond to observable or specified properties of the target system 1 , such as GPU count, link bandwidth, topology, etc. and are aligned with the hardware configuration rather than optimized. Auxiliary parameters (AP) 2 , are simulator-specific, non-directly measurable parameters whose values are inferred through calibration to reproduce target-system behavior. Auxiliary parameters lack one-to-one correspondence with the target system or exist primarily within the simulator abstraction; these constitute the calibration search space. System characteristics that are not represented in the simulator, i.e., parameters unmodeled in simulation 3 , are classified as model gaps and require refinement of the underlying model or implementation rather than parameter adjustment. CALIPER executes equivalent collective communication benchmarks 4 on the target system and simulation frame-work 5 . The Simulation Execution Engine 6 provides a simulator-agnostic interface for configuring and executing the target simulation framework and comparing its outputs against hardware measurements partitioned into calibration 

Dcal and validation Dval datasets. During calibration, direct parameters remain fixed at their hardware-aligned values, while the CALIPER optimizer 7 iteratively updates the auxiliary parameters based on the loss function 8 between simulated results and Dcal . The resulting calibrated output 9 combines the fixed direct parameters with the optimized auxiliary parameters.

3.5 Experiment Representation 

Caliper represents each measured experiment as 

ri = ( xi, q i, y i), (15) where xi describes the logical experiment, yi is the measured performance, and qi describes how the experiment was realized on the target system. The distinction between xi and qi is important. A logical workload does not always determine a unique execution. Software layers can select different algorithms, decompose work differently, or expose different levels of parallelism. Two executions can therefore have the same high-level workload xi while producing different low-level behavior. CALIPER records the execution information required to reproduce the relevant behavior in simulation. We denote this execution information by qi.The exact contents of qi depend on the simulator and workload. CALIPER does not impose a specific representation.

4.1 Target Hardware Platforms 

We assessed CALIPER’s measurement and calibration workflow on GPU-accelerated JUWELS Booster system at the Jülich Supercomputing Centre [34], as shown in Figure 2. We refer to the two JUWELS Booster topological placement allocations as Placement A and Placement B. At 64 GPUs, as depicted in Figure 2b, Placement A crosses two adjacent Dragonfly+ groups, whereas Placement B remains within one group. The scale-up parameter and scale-out is calibrated using Placement A. Placement B is used only for scale-out validation. 

4.2 Benchmarks and Ground-truth Measurements 

We use NCCL Ring/Simple collective communication benchmark executions with captured channel assignments [35]. The evaluated collective operations are AllGather, AllReduce, and ReduceScatter. All four GPUs of one node participate in the scale-up experiments. The scale-out experiments use 8, 16, 32, or 64 GPUs. The four-GPU executions use 24 NCCL channels. Executions at larger scales use eight channels. For each collective and GPU count, the dataset contains 1,024 operation sizes between 0.5 MiB and 512 MiB. Our primary evaluation metric is collective completion time, which is defined as the average in-place operation time reported by nccl-tests over 20 timed iterations following one warm-up iteration. We also track algorithmic bandwidth as defined in Equation 4. Algorithmic bandwidth is derived from completion time and is used to explain the shape of the performance curves. 

4.3 Simulation Execution Engine Instantiation 4.3.1 Simulation Framework 

We modeled the Dragonfly+ topology of the JUWELS Booster network in SST/Merlin [34, 36]. The model preserves cell hierarchy, local and global links, link capacities, and measured node placement. The default ASTRA-sim collective implementation derives a communication pattern from the collective type and communicator size. This is not sufficient when a measured NCCL execution uses a specific communication plan. We therefore modified astrasim to enable it to read the captured NCCL log (i.e., selected algorithm, channels, chunking, GPU-to-NIC placement) and to approximately reproduce the corresponding communication plan. The simulations run on a server equipped with two Intel Xeon E5-2699 v3 processors (36 physical cores, 72 hardware threads) running Ubuntu 22.04. The optimizer processes are batched sequentially. We parallelize the execution of simulator runs across 72 processes, which results in each iteration taking a median 84.5s of wall-clock time. 4.3.2 Tracked Simulator Output Metrics 

The simulator output primarily tracked is the completion time of the simulated collective. This is used to determine the loss compared to the ground truth from real hardware measurement, as described in subsection 4.2. For each simulation, the simulator reproduces the recorded GPU placement, GPU-to-NIC affinity, collective algorithm, protocol, channel count, channel order, and message partitioning. This prevents calibration from compensating for differences between the measured NCCL execution and the simulated collective communication benchmarks. 

4.3.3 Benchmark Partitioning for Calibration and Validation 

Each collective communication benchmark contains 1,024 operation sizes. During calibration, 389 distinct sizes are utilized while the remaining 635 sizes are used for validation, as shown in Table 4. For scale-up (i.e. n = 4 ) stage of calibration, we use only a subset of the four-GPU AllReduce benchmarks targetting Placement A topology, whereas the rest of the collectives (AllGather and ReduceScatter) are entirely used for validation. For scale-out calibration (i.e. n > = 8 ), we retain the fixed scale-up configuration and use only a subset of the 64-GPU AllReduce measurements from Placement A. The resulting configuration is validated in three ways. We first evaluate the remaining 64-GPU operation sizes from Placement A. We then evaluate the complete 8, 16, and 32-GPU AllReduce benchmarks targeting Placement A to test generalization across communicator size. Finally, we validate the complete 8, 16, 32, and 64-GPU AllReduce benchmarks targeting Placement B to test generalization across physical allocation, since Placement B topology was not used during calibration.

## 5 Discussion 

In this section, we discuss the directions that can be taken to extend CALIPER methodology and to adopt it to other scenarios than those presented in the previous section. 

5.1 Setting up Calibration Budgets and Stopping Criteria 

In the reported set of experiments, we setup a fixed iteration budget specified at the start of each calibration run. This approach could be extended to a combination of a maximum resource budget (expressed in number of iterations, simulator execution runs, or wall-clock time), with an early stopping criterion (in terms of target RMSE) tracking calibration progress. Also, if the RMSE does not change across a set of multiple consecutive iterations (without reaching 13 CALIPER: HPC/AI Network Simulator Calibration A P REPRINT 

the target RMSE), it may be used as a stopping criteria, indicating errors in the simulator which cannot be fixed by calibrating the simulator parameters. 

5.2 Adapting to different network simulator 

CALIPER separates its calibration logic from simulator-specific input files, commands, and output formats through the simulator interface, as shown in the Fig. 1. This freedom allows a new network simulator to be calibrated using CALIPER by means of an adapter specific to that simulator to attach the simulation execution engine and report the simulator outputs in the requried format. 

5.3 From Collectives to End-to-End Workloads 

Distributed ML training and inference serving simulators rely on network simulators for forecasting performance of AI workloads [13, 14, 16, 27, 28, 30, 32]. These simulators deal with a large variety of parameters to model including distribution strategy of data, computation time, dependencies between phases, concurrent communication, and overlap between computation and communication. By using the CALIPER methodology, these simulators can pre-calibrate their network simulators to represent target hardware systems faithfully, thereby enabling more reliable performance prediction of ML workloads. 

## 6 Related Work 

High-fidelity simulation studies commonly assess accuracy by comparing simulated and observed performance under a selected set of conditions. However, a close fit alone is insufficient evidence that a simulator represents the target system correctly: errors in workload mapping, uncertain hardware parameters, and omissions in the model can compensate for each other and yield accurate predictions for incorrect underlying reasons. Prior work has developed principles for simulation credibility and validation [10, 19, 20, 39], techniques for simulator calibration [17, 18, 40–42], and increasingly sophisticated network and distributed-AI simulators [13–16]. However, these strands do not consistently distinguish three conceptually different activities: aligning simulator inputs with directly measurable system properties and inferring auxiliary parameters that account for unresolved or abstracted behavior. CALIPER makes this separation explicit and uses it to structure the calibration and validation of AI/HPC network simulators. 

Simulation credibility, verification, and validation. Simulation credibility requires more than a close match between simulated and observed results for a limited set of experiments. Verification and validation distinguish correct implementation from whether a simulator, its assumptions, and its inputs adequately represent the target system [19, 20]. In network simulation, concerns about credibility also include the quality of experimental methodology and statistical analysis [39], while attempts to invalidate a model can expose flaws that remain hidden when evaluation reports only successful cases [10]. CALIPER translates these general principles into a concrete workflow for AI/HPC network simulators: it separates hardware alignment, parameter inference, and model diagnosis, then uses structured validation on withheld workloads and scales to test whether a calibrated configuration is credible beyond the observations used for fitting. 

Automated calibration. Automated calibration reduces the effort of manually selecting simulator parameters by searching for configurations that minimize the difference between simulated and measured behavior [17]. This is valuable because calibration becomes increasingly difficult as the number of parameters and their interactions grow [43]. Techniques such as Bayesian optimization have been used to tune HPC network models across message regimes [41, 42], while other work combines calibration with network model optimization and evaluates configurations across message lengths and router models [18]. However, automatic calibration has also shown that an inaccurate baseline can substantially distort prediction quality [40]. CALIPER differs by treating optimization as one stage of a broader method. It separates parameters that should be measured directly from those that must be inferred, constrains inferred values using system knowledge, and uses independent validation to determine whether residual error comes from parameter choices, workload mapping, or missing model behavior. 

Simulation accuracy and transferability. Simulation accuracy depends on both the chosen network abstraction and the communication conditions being modeled. Flow level models can support faster simulation than packet level models, but their accuracy varies across operating regimes and may become unacceptable when their abstraction omits relevant behavior [44]. Incorporating network topology and contention can improve predictive accuracy across real application benchmarks [45], while broad evaluations of distributed system simulators show that accuracy and scalability must be assessed across various different workloads and platforms rather than assumed from a single setting [9, 46]. CALIPER addresses transferability by comparing cross collective, and scale generalizing calibration strategies, then evaluating each resulting configuration on workloads and scales that were not used during fitting. 14 CALIPER: HPC/AI Network Simulator Calibration A P REPRINT 

## 7 Conclusion 

We presented CALIPER, a principled methodology for calibrating AI/HPC network simulators that moves beyond fitting simulation outputs to hardware measurements. CALIPER explicitly separates direct parameter alignment, auxiliary-parameter inference, and independent validation, preventing optimization from compensating for known system properties, workload mismatches, or model-form limitations. Our evaluation demonstrates the importance of identifiability-aware calibration, where network domains are calibrated hierarchically and calibration experiments are selected to sufficiently expose the parameters being inferred. By calibrating the simulator configuration and evaluating it across held-out message sizes, system scales, and physical placements, CALIPER distinguishes goodness-of-fit from predictive validity. More broadly, our results show that simulator credibility cannot be established by low calibration error alone: it requires evidence that the inferred configuration captures system behavior that persists beyond the observations used for fitting. CALIPER provides a systematic foundation for obtaining such evidence and for making simulator calibration more interpretable and scientifically defensible.

Abstract —Irregular workloads, such as those stemming from Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, frequently induce severe incast congestion in large-scale parallel environments. Existing fabric-level congestion control mechanisms rely on reactive, link-level rate limiting that de-grades overall throughput. While receiver-driven, credit-based flow control offers a proactive alternative, as process counts scale memory slots per rank fall below usable thresholds, rendering traditional rank-based accounting untenable. To address this challenge, we introduce MailRail, an out-of-order, asynchronous messaging framework, along with a novel user-space, node-based credit flow control system. By shifting credit accounting from individual processes to shared per-node pools, our proactive, fabric-agnostic approach maintains sufficient credit availability even as process density grows, enabling processes to dynamically utilize freed credits without waiting for receiver-side redistri-bution. Experimental evaluations demonstrate that node-based flow control achieves up to a 1132 × speedup over unconstrained execution at an adversarial incast ratio of 4096:1 while adding minimal overhead on uniform workloads. 

Index Terms —flow control, asynchronous communication, in-cast communication 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Both Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models generate highly irregular communi-cation patterns that are inherently prone to inducing incast congestion, a severe bottleneck where many sender processes simultaneously flood a small set of receiving victim processes [1]–[3]. GNNs use real-world datasets for training, which typ-ically exhibit power-law degree distributions [4], [5]. During training, aggregating features from hub nodes causes fan-in traffic unless mitigation techniques are used. In MoE models, used to make AI inference more scalable, tokens are routed to a subset of experts. Some experts prove more popular than others, creating many-to-one traffic spikes at GPUs hosting those experts during All-to-All collectives [1]. As one of the most destructive forms of network con-gestion in distributed architectures, incast congestion causes severe communication stalls, leaving high-speed compute and memory resources underutilized and significantly degrading overall system throughput to near zero [6], [7]. In lossless network fabrics, link-level flow control prevents packet loss by backpressuring senders when receiver buffers saturate. During incast communication, the victims’ buffers quickly saturate, forcing upstream senders to stall. Congestion spreading en-sues, causing severe performance degradation. Thus, conges-tion control represents a critical challenge in high-performance computing (HPC). Current incast mitigation strategies focus on hardware-level solutions that are reactive instead of proactive. These include sender rate limiting, which has been shown to degrade effective throughput rates [1], [6]. To overcome these limitations, end-to-end, receiver-driven, credit-based flow control offers a proactive alternative by tying transmission credits directly to pre-allocated buffer memory at the receiver, bounding in-flight traffic before congestion occurs [8], [9]. However, as supercomputer core counts and process densities scale, traditional rank-based credit accounting faces severe memory constraints: dividing receiver memory among thousands of sending processes reduces available credits to less than one buffer slot per rank, rendering rank-based accounting untenable. While traditional rank-based flow control manages credits on a per-process (MPI rank) basis, node-based flow control aggregates credit accounting into a shared, per-node pool accessed dynamically by all local ranks on the same physical host. By shifting credit accounting from individual processes to shared per-node pools, local sending ranks can dynami-cally access a collective quota of credits, maintaining high throughput and credit availability even when receiver memory slots fall below one per rank. Although receiver-side credit redistribution strategies attempt to adapt to shifting process demands [6], [10]–[12], they remain inherently reactive by relying on the receiver to detect communication bottlenecks and adjust allocations across the network. In contrast, sender-side node-based credit pooling naturally reallocates available credits instantaneously as local processes complete their trans-fers. In this work, we introduce MailRail, an asynchronous mes-saging framework designed for high-performance out-of-order communication. Within MailRail, we propose two end-to-end, user-space, credit-based flow control mechanisms to address incast congestion in a fabric-agnostic manner: rank-based flow control, which manages credits per process, and node-based flow control, which aggregates credits into host-level shared pools. We evaluate MailRail across synthetic benchmarks and real-world application traces, including an adversarial incast benchmark, a uniform-traffic histogram workload, and trace replays from YGM, LLNL’s asynchronous distributed active message runtime [13]. Experimental results show that node-based flow control consistently outperforms traditional rank-based and unconstrained execution, achieving up to a 1132 ×

speedup at an adversarial incast ratio of 4096:1, sustaining

B ACKGROUND 

Incast communication, where many processes send to a few victims, rapidly saturates receiver buffer space at victim processes, causing severe network backpressure, buffer bloat, and throughput collapse that hinders system scalability. Prior mitigation approaches predominantly rely on hardware-level or sender-side rate-limiting mechanisms to manage these bot-tlenecks, including the following: 

A. Network Fabric Strategies 1) Ethernet with RoCEv2: RoCEv2 enforces losslessness using Priority-based Flow Control (PFC) at the link level and regulates send rate via Data Center Quantized Con-gestion Notification (DCQCN). When incast communication saturates switch buffers, DCQCN relies on feedback loops using Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN) and Congestion Notification Packets (CNP) to throttle senders. These feedback loops take multiple round-trip times (RTT) to take effect. This delayed reaction causes throughput oscillations, buffer overruns, PFC pause storms, and Head-of-Line (HoL) blocking across the fabric [14]–[16]. 

2) Receiver-Driven Congestion Control for InfiniBand: To prevent packet loss, InfiniBand (IB) utilizes hop-by-hop credit-based flow control. However, fan-in incast rapidly depletes switch credits, causing backpressure to cascade upstream and freeze even uncongested flows. Receiver-driven paradigms resolve this by granting transmission windows and credit tokens from the receiving host [6]. 

3) Ultra Ethernet Consortium: The Ultra Ethernet Trans-port (UET) implements Receiver Credit-Based Congestion Control (RCCC) to eliminate last-hop incast. The receiving host tracks active senders and allocates bandwidth using microsecond time slots via ACK CC control packets [10]. 

4) Omni-Path Express (OPX): Omni-Path Express (OPX) mitigates receiver incast using in-fabric telemetry and flit-level credits rather than the link pauses used by PFC. When switch buffers near the receiving host fill up, switches issue backward notifications to throttle the sending hosts, causing the congestion for that target endpoint; the sender is free to continue sending to uncongested recipients because only the traffic sent to the congested endpoint is throttled. Per-virtual-lane credit accounting caps ingress data volume to prevent buffer overruns [14]. 

B. Endpoint Software Strategies 1) MPI Protocols: Message Passing Interface (MPI) is a library specification widely used for communication in HPC systems. MPI utilizes protocols that require acknowledgment handshakes to limit sender data transmission. This includes the Rendezvous protocol, which is triggered when a message exceeds a given size, and synchronous sends (e.g. MPI Ssend and MPI Issend), which wait for a receipt acknowledgment before the communication can finish. Both of these techniques introduce additional RTT latency, though MPI Issend can overlap this delay with computation [17], [18]. 

2) Flow Control for GASNet-EX: GASNet-EX is a commu-nication library for the network middleware layer designed for Active Messaging (AM) and Remote Memory Access (RMA). As part of the library, GASNet-EX uses flow control that ties the number of credits each sending process has to available buffer space on the receiving process using a dynamic credit allocation scheme that initially underallocates credits, then reallocates surplus credits to processes that have previously stalled due to credit starvation when sending AM Requests [12]. 

C. Motivation 

While irregular workloads achieve higher throughput us-ing asynchronous execution, small asynchronous messages can cause congestion, buffer overflows, and out-of-memory (OOM) errors. Examined congestion control strategies fail to prevent this without negatively impacting effective through-put: sender rate limiting experiences delayed responses and prolonged recovery times, while software protocols requiring handshakes or dynamic receiver-side adjustments create RTT delays. Our proposed node-based flow control overcomes these limits by proactively shaping traffic flow at the user-space level while naturally allocating credits to active sending processes. This enables consistent throughput without triggering switch backpressure regardless of the underlying network fabric. III. M AIL RAIL INTERFACE

To deliver high throughput without incurring memory copy overhead, the MailRail API centers on these core principles: zero-copy, sender-side backpressure, and user-driven progress. 

A. Buffer Reservation and Sender-side Backpressure 

To achieve zero-copy performance, MailRail requires applications to request a send buffer via mailrail reserve send buffer prior to populating payload data. Crucially, buffer allocation failure is not an error condition; rather, it serves as MailRail’s primary sender-side backpressure mechanism (see Fig. 2). When internal buffers are saturated, the call returns a null buffer and immediately yields execution back to the caller. Applications handle saturation through cooperative polling, interleaving reservation attempts with calls to mailrail receive. This ensures a sender stalled by backpressure continues to drain its incoming queues, avoiding distributed deadlocks. Once populated, the message is relayed via mailrail send. This operation is nonblocking and fire-and-forget. Depending on the implementation, a message is not necessarily sent immediately when this function is called. The API allows for sends to be deferred in a deferred send queue. When implemented, this would enable the application to continue execution without waiting for a send to complete, which is in keeping with our asynchronous model.

MailRail’s simplicity allows for various implementation strategies and design decisions. This enables experimentation over a wide variety of communication paradigms. Here we discuss details of a MailRail implementation centered on MPI, including node- and rank-based flow control. We first discuss general implementation decisions, then the specifics of each flow control mechanism. 

A. MailRail over MPI 

We implement MailRail using MPI. Because we focus on asynchronous communication, we use the nonblocking functions MPI Isend and MPI Irecv for passing messages. Receivers process messages in the order they arrive without regard to the source or the message tag. To avoid malloc overhead, MailRail reuses memory through dedicated send and receive buffer pools. When the application requests a send buffer, MailRail pulls the buffer from the send queue. Once MPI Test confirms completion, MailRail recycles the buffer back to the send pool. Similarly, when the application posts a receive request, MailRail draws a buffer from the receive pool and releases the buffer when directed by the application. Environment variables configure the datagram size, pool sizes, and maximum outstanding receive requests. 

B. Flow Control 

Our MailRail implementation provides three flow control modes: unconstrained execution, rank-based flow control, and node-based flow control. In both node- and rank-based mech-anisms, MailRail ties credits directly to available slots in the receive buffer pool. Because these credits reflect real receiver-side capacity, senders only transmit when space is available, preventing incoming messages from overwhelming receive buffers even in incast scenarios. An environment variable configures the credit-return threshold. To prevent message communication from interfering with returning credits, MailRail duplicates the MPI communicator to create a dedicated credit communicator. MailRail returns credits using zero-payload messages, encoding the credit count directly in the message tag. This approach avoids buffer allocation and memory access during credit communication. Both flow control mechanisms require a deferred send queue. When the application calls mailrail send, if the sender lacks sufficient credits for the destination process, MailRail places the send buffer into the deferred send queue and immediately returns control to the calling application. This design allows the application to continue executing without blocking on send credits. Whenever an application invokes mailrail send while messages remain in the deferred send queue, MailRail attempts to drain the queue by transmitting one message to each destination that now has available credits. MailRail then appends the new message to the queue, preserv-ing FIFO ordering to prevent newer messages from bypassing older messages destined for the same target. The maximum number of outstanding send buffers (those in use by the application as well as those enqueued in the deferred send queue) is the number of slots in the send buffer pool. When all slots are in use, attempts to reserve buffers will result in backpressure until user-driven progress drains messages from the deferred send queue. 

1) Rank-Based Flow Control: In rank-based flow control, each sending rank begins with the same static number of credits. Credits are tied to the number of slots set aside for that sender in each receiver’s receive buffer. The number of credits per sender-receiver pair is: Credits per pair = Total receive buffer size per receive rank Datagram size × Total ranks Rank-based flow control tracks credits symmetrically on both sender and receiver ranks. Senders maintain a credit count for each target destination, decrementing credits upon each transmission and sending only when credits are available. Conversely, receivers track consumed credits per sending rank, incrementing the count as they process and release incoming buffers. Once a sender’s consumed credits reach the configured threshold, the receiver returns them via the dedicated credit communicator. MailRail also supports piggybacking credits in rank-based flow control. When transmitting a payload message, the sender checks for pending credit returns owed to that target rank. If credits exist, MailRail encodes the count directly into the message tag. Upon receiving any payload message, the receiver inspects the tag; if non-zero, it adds the tag value back to its send credits for that rank. An environment variable enables this piggybacking feature. 

C. Node-Based Flow Control 

In node-based flow control, MailRail tracks credits per node rather than per rank. The tracking mechanism mirrors rank-based flow control, using shared sender- and receiver-side credit counts per node. Before transmitting, a sender verifies that its local node has available credits, then decrements the shared node-level count. Upon receiving a message, the target process, on behalf of the receiving node, increments the credits consumed for the sending node. Once the consumed count reaches the return threshold, the receiving process transmits a credit-return message to the sending process, which then updates its node’s sending credits. Because this design shifts tracking from rank pairs to node pairs, the credit allocation formula becomes: Credits per pair = Total receive buffer size per receive rank Datagram size × Total nodes There are three major advantages to tracking credits on a per-node basis rather than per-rank: reduced memory over-head, access to a large pool of shared credits, and a reduction in credit return message traffic. With rank-based flow control, the memory overhead for credit tracking is: 

2 × (Ranks per node × Node count )2 × Bit width Because our node-based mechanism tracks credits in shared memory, updating adjacent credit entries on the same cache line can trigger false sharing and costly cache evictions. To prevent this performance degradation, we pad each credit entry to 128 bytes, isolating every credit entry onto its own cache line. Consequently, node-based flow control uses 

2 × (Node count )2 × 128 Bytes to track credits. On 112-core processors, this design allows node-based flow control to use 425 × to 850 × less memory (depending on whether the bit width is 4 or 8 bytes) than rank-based flow control to track the same number of effective credits. Furthermore, node-based flow control enables receiver buffer pools smaller than one slot per rank without starving senders of credits or oversubscribing receiver memory. Under node-based flow control’s shared credit model, active sending processes gain access to a larger pool of credits as neighboring processes on the same node finish their trans-missions. Theoretically, this dynamic pooling is expected to shorten the latency tail relative to static rank-based flow control, without requiring receiver-side intervention. Node-based flow control substantially reduces network over-head from credit-return messages compared to rank-based schemes. Because credit returns trigger when a threshold ratio of consumed-to-total credits is reached, aggregating credits at the node level increases the credit pool capacity by a factor of 

k, where k is the number of ranks per node. This larger credit pool scales the credit-return threshold accordingly, offering a potential k-fold reduction in inter-node credit update mes-sages, depending on traffic distribution. In addition, intra-node credit updates bypass the network entirely, resolving locally via low-overhead shared memory. Together, these mechanisms reduce fine-grained control traffic, reducing the risk of fabric congestion caused by frequent control packets. V. E XPERIMENTATION 

A. Hardware and Software 

Unless otherwise specified, all experiments are run on Dane, a supercomputer housed at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL). Dane contains 1,544 nodes, with each node having two 56-core, 2 GHz Xeon Platinum 8480+ (Sapphire Rapids) central processing units (CPUs) running the TOSS4 operating system. Nodes are connected via Cornelis Networks Omni-Path Architecture (OPA), using Performance Scaled Messaging 2 (PSM2) as the low-level communications library. The MPI library used is OpenMPI 4.1.2, and the compiler is GCC 10.3.1 using the default optimization levels. Also unless otherwise noted, we use 4 KiB datagrams and 64 ranks per node in our experiments. We assign sending ranks 8 credits per receiving rank when using rank-based flow control. For node-based flow control, all ranks on sending nodes share 512 credits per receiving node. We conduct all rank-based flow control experiments using credit piggyback-ing. We set the credit return threshold to 2, meaning receiving processes return credits to senders after half of the senders’ credits are consumed. This reduces the number of credit return messages sent while combating credit starvation. 

B. Adversarial 

We introduce an adversarial benchmark to study how incast communication affects performance. This benchmark repre-sents an extreme example of incast communication, focusing entirely on communication and severely imbalanced work-loads. The benchmark accepts four parameters: the number of victim processes, the datagram size, the number of messages sent per process, and the number of trials. It randomly selects victim processes from the process pool, after which each process randomly generates its message sending order. Pro-cesses aggressively receive communication before their first sent message, while waiting for available send buffers, and after each sent message. This benchmark performs no compute, calling mailrail send immediately after a successful buffer reservation. Victim pro-cesses do not send messages to themselves. Only the com-munication portion of the benchmark is timed, and we find goodput (the useful, application-level data transferred over a network, measured in bits per second) by dividing the total aggregate bits received by all victims by the overall runtime of the trial [19]. All goodput numbers reported for node- and rank-based flow control schemes are based on this calculation method. Because incast communication degrades performance so severely, we add an option to capture sub-second execution statistics for granular insight into communication behavior. These metrics include elapsed time, sent message count, number of bytes sent, received message count, number of bytes received, completion percentages for sends and receives, the maximum time between mailrail send calls on the sender side, and the maximum time between mailrail receive calls on the receiver side. We print metrics per rank to avoid impacting runtime measurements. Note that because our benchmark collects these metrics at the application layer, it reports mes-sages issued to mailrail send; under active flow control, these messages may still reside in MailRail’s deferred send queue. This is why we use number of bytes received when reporting goodput for unconstrained execution.

2) Incast Degree Sensitivity: To evaluate flow control per-formance across the spectrum from severe incast to uniform traffic for pure communication, we use the adversarial bench-mark to perform a victim sweep (see Fig. 5). We hold the system scale constant at 4096 ranks and fix the aggregate data volume at 512 GiB. We define the victim sweep by varying the number of receiving (victim) ranks in power-of-two increments from 1 to 4096, transitioning from maximum incast severity (1 victim process) to a uniform distribution (4096 victim processes). Consequently, workload per victim rank doubles as the incast ratio increases. Among the evaluated methods, node-based flow control demonstrates the greatest resilience to increasingly severe incast ratios while performing at least as well as unconstrained execution under uniform communication. Beginning at an incast ratio of 8:1, unconstrained execution exhibits approxi-mately cubic runtime growth with respect to workload size: doubling the workload increases runtime by approximately 8×. In contrast, node- and rank-based flow control maintain approximately constant runtimes until an incast ratio of 64:1. At that point, node- and rank-based flow control both begin to see linear runtime growth with respect to workload, with node-based flow control growing at a slower constant factor than rank-based. At incast ratios of 4096:1 and 2048:1, node-based flow control achieves a 3.7 × speedup over rank-based flow control. Between 512 and 128 victim processes, node- and rank-based flow control exhibit significant performance speedups over unconstrained execution. At 512 victim processes (an incast ratio of 8:1), node- and rank-based flow control achieve a speedup of 15.8 × and 10.8 × over baseline respectively. This rises to 89 × and 56 × over baseline for 256 victims. At 128 victims (an incast ratio of 32:1) node- and rank-based flow control exhibit a 512 × and 391 × speedup, respectively. PSM2 timeout errors occur at 64 victims; therefore, we are unable to collect data on unconstrained execution beyond 128 victims. Node-based, rank-based, and unconstrained execution ex-hibit similar performance for more uniform communication (victim counts 1024, 2048, and 4096). Even for these, how-ever, node-based flow control demonstrates a 1.06 × to 1.58 ×

speedup compared to the unconstrained baseline. Rank-based flow control actually performs best in more uniform commu-nication, with a speedup over baseline of between 1.47 × to 1.66 ×, with the uniform case being the highest speedup.

Potential reasons for rank-based flow control’s poor perfor-mance at 14,336 ranks include receiver buffer underutilization and control message traffic. At this scale, Reddit sort traces have 17.6 million sender-receiver pairings with zero commu-nication, representing 8.6% of all pairs. Because rank-based flow control allocates credits to every sender-receiver pair in advance, pairs with no communication retain credits tied to receive-buffer capacity that active rank pairs cannot use. This can lead to unnecessary credit starvation in active senders that would benefit from access to these unused receive buffers. Node-based flow control naturally shares credits across pro-cesses and therefore avoids this limitation. Another factor contributing to node-based flow control’s superior performance over rank-based flow control may be its credit-return strategy; while rank-based flow control averages between 17.8 million and 26.4 million credit return messages per trial at 14,336 ranks, node-based flow control averages between 116,922 and 435,055, reducing network congestion caused by control messages.

Node-based flow control delivers substantial gains on the evaluated adversarial incast workloads and incurs minimal overhead, or provides modest improvements, on the evaluated uniform and trace-replay workloads. Node-based flow control delivers substantial performance gains on the evaluated adversarial incast workloads and incurs minimal overhead, or provides modest improvements, on the evaluated uniform and real-world communication patterns, as demonstrated through our evaluation of MailRail on synthetic benchmarks and YGM application traces. By avoiding the static credit-allocation constraints of rank-based flow control and regulating message injection relative to unconstrained execution, node-based flow control delivers up to a 1132 ×

speedup over unconstrained execution at an adversarial incast ratio of 4096:1, sustains goodput even under extreme 4096:1 incast scenarios, and exhibits linear runtime scaling as victim workloads increase.

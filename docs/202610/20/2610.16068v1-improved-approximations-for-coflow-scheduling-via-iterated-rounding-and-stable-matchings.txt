# Improved Approximations for Coflow Scheduling via Iterated Rounding and Stable Matchings 

Mikhail Cherniavskii, Arseni Fedorovich, Mikhail Koshelev, Andrey Kupavskii, Stanislav Moiseev, Fedor Ozhegov, Alexander Shekhovtsov 

Abstract 

We study Coflow Scheduling, introduced by Chowdhury, Zhong, and Stoica (2014). We give deterministic polynomial-time algorithms achieving a 2.77 -approximation without release dates and a 3.17 -approximation with integer release dates. These improve the 3.42 and 4.36 

guarantees of Rohwedder and Schnaars (2025). Our approach combines two ideas: (1) a novel iterated rounding scheme, and (2) Galvin’s list edge coloring method, which is based on the Gale–Shapley stable matching algorithm. 

# 1 Introduction 

Coflow scheduling, introduced by Chowdhury, Zhong, and Stoica [7], models collections of data transfers with a common completion requirement. Examples motivating the coflow abstraction in-clude MapReduce shuffles [7], broadcast and aggregation stages in Spark [6], and message exchanges within a Pregel superstep [16, 6]. A coflow completes only when all its transfers have finished, so op-timizing individual transfer need not optimize the completion of the corresponding communication stage. The Varys system demonstrated that coordinating transfers at the coflow level can improve completion times compared with per-flow mechanisms [7]. We study the offline, discrete unit-transfer model: input and output ports form the two sides of a bipartite multigraph, each unit transfer is an edge, and each time slot processes a matching. The edges are partitioned into coflows with positive weights, and the objective is the weighted sum of their completion times. The release-date variant additionally requires each edge to be scheduled after the release date of its coflow. A concurrent-open-shop relaxation treats each port as a machine and each coflow as a job. The job’s processing requirement on a machine is its total load at that port. These workloads can be processed independently, dropping the requirement that a transfer use its input and output simultaneously [13]. For concurrent open shop without release dates, Mastrolilli et al. [17] give a primal–dual 2-approximation. Concurrent open shop also embeds as a special case of coflow scheduling using disjoint input–output port pairs. Thus the (2 − ε)-inapproximability of Sachdeva and Saket [22], obtained through structural hardness for hypergraph vertex cover, applies to coflows as well: for every fixed ε > 0, a (2 − ε)-approximation would imply P = N P . Qiu, Stein, and Zhong [18] gave the first constant-factor approximation algorithms using interval-indexed LP rounding, geometric batching, and matching decompositions. Khuller and Purohit [13] instead obtain a coflow ordering from a concurrent-open-shop relaxation and schedule geometric batches in that order. They give deterministic factors 8 and 12 without and with release dates, respectively, and a randomized (3+2 √2) -approximation without release dates. Shafiee and Ghaderi [23] combine an LP with ordering variables and randomly shifted geometric batching to obtain randomized factors

Shafiee and Ghaderi [24] obtain the same factors with a strengthened ordering-variable LP followed by preemptive list scheduling. Sincronia [1] connects approximation guarantees to system design: primal–dual coflow ordering combined with work-conserving, preemptive, order-preserving flow scheduling gives a factor 4 when all coflows arrive at time zero. 1.1 Our Contribution 

We give improved approximation algorithms for both variants of Coflow Scheduling. Our algorithms use an iterated rounding procedure and, for release dates, a scheduling construction based on stable matchings. 

Theorem 1 (Without release dates) . There is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm achieving a 58 /21 ( < 2.77) -approximation for Coflow Scheduling without release dates.

Iterated rounding Our rounding procedure improves over the scheme of Rohwedder and Schnaars [19], in which they used the result of Beck and Fiala [4] to bound the error in terms of column spar-sity of the matrix. Our method builds on ideas from iterative rounding techniques (see Lau, Ravi, and Singh [15]). Our algorithm selects a subset of tight capacity constraints and walks orthogonal to them to round the fractional solution. To perform this walk, we need to show that at every step the number of fractional variables is more than the number of selected rows. By doing a careful summation of capacity and assignment constraints we show that the number of fractional variables is at least the number of selected rows. Release-aware scheduling through stable matchings For release dates, we use list-coloring algorithms after running our iterated rounding. Our idea to use list-coloring comes from the fact that for each edge e, we have a list of timeslots Le = {re + 1 , r e + 2 , . . . , r e + de} in which it can be scheduled ( re is a release date of edge e, de is how many slots we allow for this edge), and the list-coloring permits to assign an edge while preserving release and capacity constraints. The list-coloring method that we use is based on Galvin’s method [10], which is implemented through Gale–Shapley stable matchings [9]. For the beautiful connection between stable matchings and list coloring, see its application to the Dinitz problem in Proofs from THE BOOK [3, Chapter 38] and the extensions of Galvin’s method by Borodin, Kostochka, and Woodall [5]. Moreover, we introduce an extension of greedy algorithm that achieves better guarantees than the standard greedy algorithm. Our extended algorithm uses a novel idea that we can reverse the priority of edges to balance out demand in a prefix of timeslots. This idea is of independent interest as it can be applied to other scheduling problems. 2.3 Known graph results 

In this section, we provide necessary background definitions and results about graphs and matchings that we will use in our algorithms. We say an edge coloring is proper if two different edges sharing a vertex have different colors. Consider timeslots 1, 2, 3, . . . as different colors. In this view, a proper edge coloring partitions the edges into matchings and therefore gives a schedule when release constraints are absent (color corresponds to timeslot in which the edge is scheduled). We use the following classical theorem. 5Lemma 2.1 (Kőnig [14]) . Every bipartite multigraph F admits a proper edge coloring with ∆( F )

colors, and such a coloring can be found in polynomial time. Equivalently, its edges can be scheduled in ∆( F ) slots. 

Our algorithm for instances with release dates uses stable matchings and the Gale–Shapley theorem. We recall the relevant definitions and theorem below. For each vertex v ∈ V , fix a strict total order ≻v on its incident edges δ(v), where e ≻v f means that v prefers e to f . A matching 

F ⊆ E is stable if, for every edge e ∈ E \ F , there exist an endpoint v of e and an edge f ∈ F ∩ δ(v)

such that f ≻v e.

Theorem 3 (Gale–Shapley [9]) . Every finite bipartite multigraph equipped with strict total orders on the incident edges at each vertex admits a stable matching. Such a matching can be found in polynomial time.

Abstract 

Long-context training of large language and multimodal models couples expres-sive structured attention masks (document, prefix-LM, share-question, sliding-window, block-sparse) with multi-node context parallelism, exposing the mask as a dominant bottleneck. The column-wise sparse mask of FlashMask com-presses such masks from O(n2) to O(n) via four query-axis boundary vectors and covers a broad family of structured patterns. Implemented for Ampere GPUs, FlashMask does not exploit FlashAttention-3’s TMA/WGMMA warp-specialized pipeline on Hopper GPUs, and offers no distributed support. We observe that the column-wise sparse mask is indexed by key positions but stores query-axis interval boundaries. Under any multi-chunk context-parallel assignment, this property reduces per-rank mask localization to a provably zero-communication clip–shift– max on the boundary vectors, directly compatible with the single-GPU kernel. Building on this property, FlashMask-3 introduces a mask-aware Hopper kernel with a three-role warp specialization atop the FlashAttention-3 TMA/WGMMA pipeline, together with a mask-aware distributed extension: zero-communication mask localization, a sparsity-aware load balancer (HCC-IPO), and a mask-aware compute-communication overlap with mask-driven communication pruning, whose forward all-gather additionally adopts topology-aware hierarchical routing. On a single H100 GPU across 12 structured masks at context lengths from 4K to 128K, FlashMask-3 improves achieved TFLOPs over FlashMask, FlexAttention, and MagiAttention by 45% to 141%, 34% to 71%, and 2% to 44%, respectively. Under context-parallel degrees from 4 to 32 with 8K sequence per device, it attains 31% to 75% compute-communication overlap efficiency and improves throughput over Megatron-Core CP (ring), Megatron-Core CP (all-gather) and MagiAttention by 

1.80 × to 3.71 ×, 1.71 × to 2.92 × and 1.09 × to 1.46 × respectively. In end-to-end training of a 118B-A14B MoE model at a 128K context on 128 H100 GPUs, it raises throughput by 7.3% to 11.0% over a Dual Chunk all-gather baseline, with attention forward+backward 24.5% to 44.9% faster. https://github.com/PaddlePaddle/FlashMask .

1 Introduction 

Long-context training of large language and multimodal models increasingly relies on structured attention masks, including document masks [Dao et al., 2022], prefix-LM masks [Wang et al., 2026], share-question masks [Deng et al., 2025], sliding-window masks [Beltagy et al., 2020], and block-sparse patterns [Child et al., 2019, Yuan et al., 2025, Lu et al., 2025], to encode training-data semantics

within a single packed batch. As context grows toward hundreds of thousands of tokens and training scales to multi-node context parallelism [Liu et al., 2023, Jacobs et al., 2023, Narayanan et al., 2021], masks become a first-class bottleneck: mask materialization, mask-aware kernel scheduling, and mask-induced load imbalance [Jiang et al., 2025, Tao and Huang, 2025] come to dominate step time. Programmable attention compilers [He et al., 2024] handle arbitrary masks but trail FlashAttention-3 (FA3) [Shah et al., 2024] in Hopper throughput; FA3 itself first-classes only causal, full, and varlen-document masks. The column-wise sparse representation of FlashMask [Wang et al., 2024] fills the expressiveness gap by encoding masks through four O(n) boundary vectors, covering 12+ structured patterns within an exact tiled kernel [Dao et al., 2022, Dao, 2023]. However, FlashMask was implemented on Ampere GPUs and predates FA3: it exploits neither Hopper’s TMA/WGMMA warp-specialized pipeline [Shah et al., 2024] nor any form of distributed context parallelism [Liu et al., 2023, Brandon et al., 2023, Narayanan et al., 2021]. We observe a structural property that resolves the distributed gap. Because the column-wise sparse mask is indexed by key positions but stores query-axis intervals [Wang et al., 2024], per-rank mask localization under any multi-chunk CP assignment reduces to an elementwise clip–shift–max on the boundary vectors, requiring no cross-rank communication and producing a rank-local tensor directly compatible with the single-GPU kernel (Theorem 1). Building on this property, we present FlashMask-3, 3 which organizes mask-awareness along two axes: (i) a mask-aware Hopper kernel with three-role warp specialization integrating column-wise sparse scanning into the FA3 TMA/WGMMA pipeline (§3.1); and (ii) a mask-aware distributed extension combining zero-communication mask localization (§3.2), a sparsity-aware hierarchical load balancer(§3.3), and a mask-aware compute-communication overlap [Chang et al., 2024, NVIDIA, 2023] with topology-aware hierarchical routing and mask-driven communication pruning (§3.4). Our main contributions are as follows: • Mask-aware Hopper kernel. A three-role warp specialization (KV Index Generator, KV Loader, MMA Consumer) integrates column-wise sparse mask processing into the asynchronous TMA/WGMMA pipeline at near-zero overhead, complemented by a preemptive persistent-tile scheduler that restores SM-level balance under irregular masks. • Mask-aware distributed extension. A zero-communication clip–shift–max mask localiza-tion with provable correctness; a sparsity-aware hierarchical load balancer (HCC-IPO); and a mask-aware compute-communication overlap with mask-driven communication pruning, whose forward all-gather uses topology-aware hierarchical routing and whose backward reduce-scatter is overlapped with attention recomputation through bitmask-AMO (Atomic Memory Operations) synchronization. • Empirical evaluation. On a single H100 GPU across 12 structured masks at context lengths from 4K to 128K, FlashMask-3 improves achieved TFLOPs over FlashMask, FlexAttention, and MagiAttention by 45% to 141%, 34% to 71%, and 2% to 44%, respectively. Under context-parallel degrees from 4 to 32 with 8K sequence per device, it attains 31% to 75% compute-communication overlap efficiency and improves throughput over Megatron-Core CP (ring), Megatron-Core CP (all-gather) and MagiAttention by 1.80 × to 3.71 ×, 1.71 × to 2.92 × and 1.09 × to 1.46 ×

respectively. In end-to-end training of a 118B-A14B MoE model at a 128K context on 128 H100 GPUs (CP degree 16), our distributed extension raises the training throughput by 7.3% to 11.0% over the same system using Dual Chunk balancing and all-gather communication. 

2 Background 

2.1 FlashAttention-3 on Hopper GPUs 

The FlashAttention family [Dao et al., 2022, Rabe and Staats, 2021, Milakov and Gimelshein, 2018, Dao, 2023] computes exact attention O = softmax( QK ⊤/√d)V without materializing the n × n

score matrix, through tiling and online softmax. FlashAttention-3 [Shah et al., 2024] targets Hopper GPUs by adding asynchronous TMA loads, WGMMA instructions, and warp-specialized producer– consumer pipelines: a producer warp group issues TMA loads while consumer warp groups execute WGMMA, coordinated through a small set of cross-warp barriers. FlashMask-3 builds on this 

> 3The name aligns with FlashAttention-3, on which our Hopper kernel is built, mirroring how FlashMask [Wang et al., 2024] built on FlashAttention-2 [Dao, 2023].

2asynchronous backbone, inserting mask processing as an additional warp-specialized stage rather than inline branches in the main compute loop (§3.1). 

2.2 Column-wise sparse mask representation 

FlashMask [Wang et al., 2024] encodes structured attention masks from the perspective of key tokens. The score matrix is split along the diagonal into lower-triangular and upper-triangular regions, and the mask at each key j is encoded by four boundary vectors LTS , LTE , UTS , UTE ∈ Zn

> ≥0

, with the masked query rows at key j given by [LTS j , LTE j ) ∪ [UTS j , UTE j ). The tensor is indexed by key positions but stores query-axis values , a structural asymmetry that we exploit for zero-communication distributed mask localization in §3.2. Inside a tiled kernel, blockwise max/min along the key axis classifies each query–key tile as fully-masked, partially-masked, or unmasked, supporting tile skipping (§3.1) and the cost model in §3.3. Two alternative routes target masks beyond the FlashAttention-native subset. FlexAttention [He et al., 2024] JIT-compiles an arbitrary user-defined mask_mod function via a Triton-based DSL [Tillet et al., 2019]; the compiled kernel handles arbitrary mask logic but follows a code path separate from FA3 and incurs per-mask compilation cost. MagiAttention [Tao and Huang, 2025] introduces an AttnSlice formulation paired with a dedicated Flex-Flash-Attention (FFA) kernel that natively supports arbitrary mask types; its distributed extension materializes per-rank mask structure via slice-cutting operations. In contrast, FlashMask-3 retains the O(n) column-wise sparse representation and reduces per-rank mask localization to a clip–shift–max on the boundary vectors at zero cross-rank communication (§3.2), with no per-rank slice materialization or kernel recompilation. 

2.3 Context parallelism and load balancing 

Context parallelism (CP) shards the query sequence across P ranks; each rank computes attention for its local queries against the global K, V stream exchanged across ranks. Ring Attention [Liu et al., 2023] circulates K, V around a device ring overlapped with blockwise attention; Striped Attention [Brandon et al., 2023] interleaves tokens for causal-load balance; DeepSpeed Ulysses [Ja-cobs et al., 2023] converts sequence parallelism into head parallelism via all-to-all; Megatron CP [Narayanan et al., 2021] pairs early and late query chunks ( dual chunking ) for balanced causal workload and obtains global K, V through a forward all-gather and a backward reduce-scatter. Under expressive masks, the monotonic-load assumption underlying dual chunking breaks, coupling two further challenges: balancing mask-induced sparse compute across ranks, and overlapping forward all-gather and backward reduce-scatter with mask-aware computation, both addressed in §3. 

2.4 Compute–communication overlap 

Compute–communication overlap operates at two levels. At the kernel-stream level, FLUX [Chang et al., 2024] fuses all-gather and reduce-scatter into GEMM tiles via signal-based dependencies, achieving near-complete overlap for linear layers; Ring Attention [Liu et al., 2023] overlaps K, V 

circulation with blockwise attention via dual CUDA streams. Extending tile-level fusion to attention is non-trivial because online softmax requires per-tile rescaling that degrades throughput upon splitting. At the topology level, NVSHMEM [NVIDIA, 2023] exposes GPU-initiated one-sided put/get over symmetric memory, enabling fine-grained overlap without CPU staging. These mechanisms form the substrate on which §3.4 builds a mask-aware overlap that prunes communication of fully-masked KV chunks and tracks per-segment readiness via bitmask-AMO synchronization. A comprehensive discussion of additional related work is provided in Appendix B.

3.1 Hopper-optimized mask-aware kernel 

On Hopper, FA3 overlaps TMA-issued KV loads with WGMMA score and online softmax via two warp-group roles [Shah et al., 2024]; inserting column-wise sparse mask scanning into either role would stall the TMA queue or introduce a barrier inside the WGMMA critical path. We observe that FA3’s Producer warp group dedicates 128 threads to TMA issue while a single thread suffices to launch each op, leaving 96 threads idle every iteration; FlashMask-3 reclaims these threads as a third warp-specialized role, the KV Index Generator , which scans the column-wise sparse boundaries described in §2.2 and emits a queue of valid blocks classified as fully-masked, partially-masked, or unmasked via the blockwise max/min of {LTS , LTE , UTS , UTE }. The KV Loader consumes the queue and issues TMA loads only for listed blocks; the MMA Consumer runs the WGMMA score path, applying a row-interval mask only on partially-masked blocks (Figure 2). The same classification feeds host-side preprocessing in §3.3 and communication-side filtering in §3.4 on independent code paths. Beyond warp specialization, irregular masks make per-Q-block valid-K-block counts vary by an order of magnitude, leaving SMs that own long-tail Q-blocks straggling under static tile-to-SM dispatch. 3.3 Hierarchical Sparsity-Aware Compute-Communication Balancing 

Achieving efficient distributed attention requires jointly optimizing both compute and communication loads, especially under structured sparsity. While balancing per-rank compute loads is critical to avoid stragglers, disregarding the communication topology can easily lead to excessive inter-node key-value (KV) traffic and increased overall latency. However, exact joint optimization is intractable due to the combinatorial search space and sparsity-induced irregularities. To address this challenge, we propose a Hierarchical Compute-Communication Iterative Pairwise Optimal (HCC-IPO) scheduler, which alternates between: (i) inter-rank compute balancing (assigning chunks to ranks to minimize per-rank compute imbalance under strict cardinality constraints), (ii) inter-node communication reduction (iteratively swapping chunk assignments between nodes to minimize the maximum inter-node KV traffic while maintaining compute balance within an ϵ-tolerance), and (iii) intra-node compute rebalancing (restoring compute load balance within each node, with node assignments fixed). 3.4 Mask-aware hierarchical compute-communication overlap 

CP introduces overlap in two aspects: (i) the all-gather (AG) of K, V in the forward pass (and back-ward recomputation), and (ii) the reduce-scatter (RS) of dK, dV in the backward pass. FlashMask-3 hides both costs behind sparse attention computation via three mechanisms: (1) a sparse-mask-aware NVSHMEM symmetric substrate; (2) a hierarchical (intra-/inter-node) AG scheme decoupled from the attention kernel; and (3) a multi-stage backward pipeline interleaving AG, attention, and RS. Communication kernels occupy only 4–8 SMs on high-priority non-blocking streams. The overlap requires fewer than 20 lines of minimally invasive modification to the attention kernel. Detailed protocols are in Appendix C.4 and C.5; Figure 4 provides a timeline summary. 

Sparse-mask-aware NVSHMEM symmetric substrate. A single Send/Recv buffer (SR buffer), sized for the complete post-AG K, V tensors, is allocated in NVSHMEM symmetric memory. Any device can access any remote SR-buffer slot via one-sided RDMA get /put , and the attention kernel reads K, V directly from this buffer without intermediate copies. The only unavoidable data movement is a one-time copy of the local K, V shard into the SR buffer; the same buffer is reused for backward recomputation at no extra cost. Before each communication launch, a lightweight sparsity-check kernel scans the column-partitioned sparse boundaries (§3.2) to produce a binary 

copy_chunk_mask marking entirely masked-out KV chunks. The communication kernel skips these chunks, eliminating ≈50% of AG/RS traffic in typical causal document and prefix-LM document masks in our experiments. 

Decoupled hierarchical all-gather. The AG kernel runs on a high-priority stream while the attention kernel performs a single pass over the gathered K, V , without partitioning K, V across communication steps, thereby avoiding the inter-partition online-softmax LSE correction overhead. The two kernels synchronize via a monotonically increasing write-pointer frontier ; the KV Loader (§3.1) polls this pointer before each TMA load. 4 Experiments 

In this section, we evaluate FlashMask-3 through a comprehensive set of experiments designed to assess single-GPU kernel efficiency (§4.1), distributed scalability (§4.2), and end-to-end training throughput on a production-scale MoE workload (§4.3), followed by component-level ablations (§4.4). All experiments are conducted on machines equipped with NVIDIA H100 80GB SXM5 GPUs interconnected via NVLink within each node and RoCE across nodes, with 8 GPUs per node and Intel Xeon Platinum 8468V CPUs (48 cores). We use CUDA 12.4, CUTLASS (commit afa177 ) [Thakkar et al., 2023] and NVSHMEM 3.2.5 throughout. Baseline configurations are as follows: MagiAttention (commit d97b72 ), FlexAttention (PyTorch 2.8.0), and Megatron 2.8.0. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments use a head dimension of d = 128 and the BF16 data type. Detailed experimental configurations are provided in Appendix D.1.1. Speed measurements are repeated; error bars in the figures and ± values in the tables denote ±1 standard deviation.

4.3 End-to-End Training Throughput 

The preceding sections measure attention in isolation; we now report throughput for full training steps. We train a 118B-A14B MoE model at a 128K context on 128 H100 GPUs (16 nodes) with CP degree 16, a per-device local sequence length of Slocal =8 K tokens, Hq =64 query heads, Hkv =8 KV heads, and a document mask. The baseline is FlashMask-3 with Dual Chunk balancing and all-gather communication, i.e. with the balancer of §3.3 and the overlap of §3.4 disabled. Because the share of step time spent in attention depends on mask sparsity, we repeat the comparison on three datasets with average mask sparsity 70%, 80%, and 86%. Table 1 reports gains of 7.3% / 7.7% / 11.0% in end-to-end throughput with both components enabled, at 70% / 80% / 86% sparsity, against attention forward+backward speedups of 24.5% / 42.1% / 44.9% (at 70% sparsity, 10900.5 ms per step down to 8757.6 ms). The end-to-end gain is smaller because attention accounts for only 43.5% / 25.8% / 24.4% of forward+backward compute time in the full configuration, and the end-to-end measurement also covers gradient synchronization and the optimizer update, which our method does not modify. Each component helps on its own, contributing 2.8% / 4.4% / 5.7% (balancing) and 5.4% / 4.2% / 5.9% (overlap), and chunk-skip pruning is worth a further 2.0% / 2.9% / 1.5%. Hierarchical routing is required for the two to compose at this cross-node scale: with it disabled, balancing plus overlap falls to −1.7% / −1.5% / +1.9%, below overlap alone, because the balancer spreads chunks across nodes and that traffic is no longer confined to congruence groups (§3.4). The default of 4 backward pipeline stages follows the rule of §3.4 at P =16 : halving it to 2 costs up to 3.6%, while raising it to 8 yields no significant further gain. FlashMask-3 computes exact attention: mask localization matches the global mask (Theorem 1), and the balancer, overlap, and pruning change only how work is placed, not what is computed. Convergence and downstream quality are therefore unaffected; Appendix D.3 compares the loss of an otherwise identical run without context parallelism.

We compare three tile-scheduling strategies on a single H100 GPU: (i) a static scheduler that assigns a fixed tile grid, (ii) a single-tile scheduler that dispatches one work tile at a time, and (iii) the proposed PPT scheduler. As shown in Figure 7(a), across 12 mask configurations at sequence lengths of 8K, 32K, and 128K, PPT consistently outperforms the static scheduler by up to 12% , demonstrating that preemptive, persistent tile execution effectively amortizes scheduling overhead and improves streaming multiprocessor (SM) utilization.

As shown in Figure 8, FlashMask-3 attains an overlap efficiency ranging from 0.31 to 0.75, validating the effectiveness of our mask-aware hierarchical compute-communication. 

5 Conclusion 

FlashMask-3 establishes that the column-wise sparse mask representation, originally a single-GPU memory optimization, also serves as the right interface for distributing expressive masks at zero communication on Hopper-class hardware. The method preserves one column-wise sparse mask abstraction and one FA3-derived kernel across single-GPU and multi-node settings, with mask-awareness propagated through Hopper kernel scheduling, hierarchical load balancing, and compute-communication overlap. Empirical evaluation across 12 structured masks at context lengths up to 128K on a single H100 GPU and context parallelism up to P =32 yields consistent improvements in single-GPU TFLOPs, compute-communication overlap, and distributed throughput over representa-tive single-GPU and distributed mask baselines. A Limitations and Broader Impact 

Limitations. The method covers the column-wise sparse mask family of Wang et al. [2024]; masks whose per-column masked rows do not decompose into two intervals require representation extensions. The Hopper kernel exploits TMA/WGMMA and assumes Hopper-class architectures; porting to other architectures requires re-targeting the warp specialization. The hierarchical overlap of §3.4 assumes per-node 8-GPU NVLink islands with same-rank IB topology; clusters with different topologies need a different inter-node mapping. Hierarchical routing is currently applied only to the all-gather 12 operation; extending it to the backward reduce-scatter is left as future work. Empirical claims rely on production-scale long-context settings; smaller contexts may not amortize the scheduler overhead. Finally, our benchmarks cover a representative but not exhaustive set of QKV tensor configurations; a systematic sweep over batch sizes and query/key-value head ratios beyond those reported in §4.2 and §4.3 is left to future work. 

Broader impact. By improving the throughput and energy efficiency of long-context training, FlashMask-3 lowers the cost barrier for academic and open-source long-context multimodal research and reduces the carbon footprint of large-scale training. The same efficiency gains can also lower the cost of training capability-amplifying models, which is an externality common to research on large-scale training infrastructure. 

B Extended Related Work 

IO-aware exact attention. FlashAttention [Dao et al., 2022] introduced tiling and online soft-max [Milakov and Gimelshein, 2018, Rabe and Staats, 2021] to compute exact attention in O(N )

memory without materializing the N ×N score matrix. FlashAttention-2 [Dao, 2023] swapped the loop order and optimized warp-level work partitioning, reaching 50–73% of A100 peak throughput. FlashAttention-3 [Shah et al., 2024] further added Hopper-native TMA/WGMMA pipelining and FP8 support, achieving 740 TFLOPs/s on H100. Block-Sparse FlashAttention [Dao et al., 2022] augments the tiled kernel with an O(N 2/B 2) binary block mask for tile skipping; FlashMask’s column-wise sparse representation achieves O(N ) storage with token-level granularity. 

Compiler-driven attention. FlexAttention [He et al., 2024] JIT-compiles arbitrary mask_mod 

functions into Triton [Tillet et al., 2019] kernels, offering maximal flexibility at the cost of per-mask compilation overhead and a code path separate from FA3’s Hopper-native pipeline. Under distributed CP, each rank independently reconstructs the local block mask at O(QK/B 2) cost. FlashMask-3 avoids both issues through a fixed kernel binary and zero-communication mask localization. 

Learnable block-sparse attention. NSA [Yuan et al., 2025] combines compression, top-k block selection, and sliding-window branches in an end-to-end differentiable architecture that is hardware-aligned and natively trainable from pretraining. MoBA [Lu et al., 2025] routes each query to its top-k

KV blocks via MoE-style gating, enabling seamless switching with full attention. Both learn which 

tokens to attend to and require architectural modification. FlashMask-3 solves a complementary problem: efficiently computing a pre-specified structural mask with no model changes required. 

Structured mask representations. MagiAttention [Tao and Huang, 2025] represents masks as AttnSlice triples (Qrange , K range , MaskType ), cut along Q/K axes for distribution; this requires a dedicated FFA kernel with per-slice atomics. FlashMask [Wang et al., 2024] uses column-wise sparse boundary vectors at O(N ) storage, covering 12+ structured mask patterns. FlashMask-3 extends this to distributed settings through an elementwise clip–shift–max transform that preserves the single-GPU kernel interface at zero cross-rank communication. 

Context parallelism. Ring Attention [Liu et al., 2023] circulates KV on a device ring with dual-stream overlap, achieving ∼50% GPU utilization under causal masks due to inherent load imbal-ance [Li et al., 2023]. Striped Attention [Brandon et al., 2023] restores causal balance via round-robin token assignment (1.45–1.65 × over Ring) but supports only causal masks. DeepSpeed Ulysses [Ja-cobs et al., 2023] converts to head parallelism via all-to-all, natively supporting arbitrary masks but requiring Nheads mod P = 0 . USP [Fang and Zhao, 2024] unifies Ulysses (intra-node) with Ring (inter-node). Megatron CP [Narayanan et al., 2021] employs dual chunking for causal balance; under irregular masks such as shared-question (RLHF) or multi-document masks, its linear-cost assumption fails and per-rank utilization degrades significantly. 

Load balancing under irregular masks. DCP [Jiang et al., 2025] (SOSP 2025) models CP scheduling as hypergraph partitioning over block-level workloads, achieving <5% imbalance at the cost of a heavyweight solver. MagiAttention [Tao and Huang, 2025] uses chunk-level LPT-greedy assignment [Graham, 1969] based on AttnSlice areas. FlashMask-3 derives per-chunk valid-block counts directly from the column-wise boundary vectors and solves the scheduling subproblem with provable optimality guarantees (§3.3), avoiding external solver overhead. 13 Compute–communication overlap. Ring Attention [Liu et al., 2023] relies on dual CUDA streams, which suffer SM contention when large attention kernels starve communication of resources. FLUX [Chang et al., 2024] demonstrates that kernel-level tile fusion eliminates this contention for GEMMs, but online softmax prevents straightforward extension to attention. MagiAttention uses DeepEP-based [Zhao et al., 2025] GroupCast/GroupReduce with stream-level multi-stage overlap. FlashMask-3 uses NVSHMEM [NVIDIA, 2023] GPU-initiated one-sided communication with zero-copy symmetric buffers shared with the attention kernel, achieving high overlap without perturbing the WGMMA critical path. Mesh-Attention [Chen et al., 2025] reduces communication volume from O(N d ) to O(N d/ √P ) via a 2D assignment matrix, a topological optimization orthogonal and composable with FlashMask-3’s latency-focused NVSHMEM overlap.

Discussion. The cardinality-constrained min-max P -way partitioning problem (2) is NP-hard in general, and an exact solver would require Branch-and-Bound over the full P N assignment space. IPO deliberately restricts each iteration to 2- or 3-rank sub-problems whose exact solutions are tractable (via MitM or BnB with tight pruning), trading global exhaustiveness for polynomial per-iteration cost. This local-search structure cannot theoretically guarantee convergence to the global optimum in all cases. In practice, however, the LB certificate (Theorem 2(b)) provides a sufficient condition that is verifiable in O(N ) time: whenever IPO attains Φ = LB , global optimality is certified without requiring full enumeration. C.4 Forward all-gather protocol and pseudocode 

This appendix formalizes the forward AG overlap protocol of §3.4. 

C.4.1 Write-pointer synchronization 

The AG communication kernel runs Ncta CTAs on a high-priority stream, dynamically claiming work units (each covering R KV rows) via an atomic counter. A monotonically increasing write pointer 

(wptr ) signals progress to the attention kernel: all rows at offsets < wptr are guaranteed globally visible. 

Producer (AG kernel). Each CTA publishes its latest completed work ID to a per-CTA slot. Warp 0 reads all Ncta slots via __reduce_min_sync , yielding a conservative contiguous frontier wmin . A 

__threadfence then ensures KV store visibility before atomicMax(wptr, wmin × R) advances the pointer. 

Consumer (attention kernel). The KV Loader iterates blocks right-to-left. For block n, it computes target (n) = b (Stotal −Slocal ) + ( Stotal −nB N −Slocal ). Blocks within the local shard (Stotal −nB N −Slocal < 0) skip the wait; remote blocks spin on wptr via ld.volatile.global with a cached last-seen value. 

C.4.2 Deadlock prevention 

Two mechanisms prevent the attention grid from starving the AG kernel of SM resources. Since the attention kernel depends on wptr , the AG kernels must be scheduled ahead of the computation kernels to guarantee forward progress: (1) each AG CTA sets a bit in a GPU-memory coordinator word upon physical scheduling; a sentinel kernel on the compute stream spin-waits until all bits are set before the attention grid launches; (2) the attention tile scheduler is capped at num_sm −sm_margin 

tiles, reserving capacity for the AG kernel. Yet in practice, we find that the sentinel stream coordinator kernel alone is sufficient to ensure correct SM scheduling, while enabling sm_margin may negatively impact computation performance. sm_margin is therefore set to 0 throughout the experiments.

C.5 Backward multi-stage reduce-scatter overlap 

This appendix formalizes the backward RS overlap protocol of §3.4. 

C.5.1 Per-chunk synchronization 

The forward AG uses a contiguous write-pointer frontier wptr because the attention kernel scans KV blocks sequentially. The backward kernel is column-parallel : each CTA owns one KV chunk and iterates over all Q blocks independently, requiring no cross-chunk ordering. The communication kernel therefore replaces the write pointer with per-chunk work_done flags: after fetching chunk 

c, the CTA issues __threadfence (ensuring data visibility) then sets work_done[ c] = 1. The backward compute CTA polls this flag directly before loading its chunk. 

C.5.2 Bitmask-AMO reduce-scatter protocol 

RS is realized as a sparse all-to-all via NVSHMEM one-sided put . Each device simultaneously acts as a producer (sending dK/ dV to chunk owners) and a consumer (receiving and reducing contributions from other ranks). 

Protocol.

D.5 Scheduling cost and overlap robustness 

Both the per-chunk weights wi (§C.2) and the HCC-IPO assignment operate on the four boundary vectors rather than on a dense mask, so their cost grows linearly with the sequence length and not with the quadratic tile count. Their latency is also not on the critical path. For a fixed set of weights the assignment is deterministic, so we compute it ahead of time and overlap it with the compute and communication of the previous iteration, and reuse it across layers through a cache keyed on the mask pattern. The cost is not negligible in absolute terms, which is why it is prefetched rather than run inline. Table 8 reports the standalone scheduling latency at P =32 with chunk size 2048 next to the attention time under the same configuration: 23 to 44 ms of scheduling against 35 to 67 ms of attention, the same order of magnitude. Table 9 measures the same preprocessing inside the 128-GPU configuration of §4.3, in a separate run, where it accounts for 0.60% to 0.75% of the step. Prefetching therefore keeps the scheduling latency out of the end-to-end time. Computing the assignment offline has two consequences. First, for a model whose layers use different mask types we do not switch the assignment per layer. We score each mask type, take a weighted sum over the proportion of those types in the stack, and solve HCC-IPO once on that aggregate profile. When the mask type changes often from layer to layer, a single static assignment fits each individual layer less well, and re-solving at that frequency would add latency that prefetching cannot hide.

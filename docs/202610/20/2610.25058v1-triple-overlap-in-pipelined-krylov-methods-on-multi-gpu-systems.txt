Abstract —We introduce a novel triple-overlap implementa-tion of the pipelined preconditioned conjugate gradient (Pipe-PCG) and pipelined stabilized biconjugate gradient (Pipe-BiCGStab) solvers, methods widely used in scientific applica-tions. By leveraging a collective communication library, our ap-proach extends conventional computation–communication over-lap to concurrently execute collective operations, irregular P2P communication, and compute kernels, enabling compu-tation–communication–communication overlap across multiple GPUs. We evaluate the design on the MareNostrum 5 (H100) and Leonardo (A100) supercomputers, comparing against PETSc and the state-of-the-art two-stream NCCL implementation using SuiteSparse matrices and three massive instances scaled up to 512 GPUs. The three-stream design outperforms the two-stream baseline on every instance, with the margin widening from 2–6% at 4 GPUs to 18–47% at 64 GPUs. At 64 GPUs, the geometric-mean speedup over the two-stream baseline is 1.33× on MareNostrum 5 and 1.23× on Leonardo for Pipe-PCG, and reaches 1.30× for Pipe-BiCGStab. In large scale experiments, we observed a speedup up to 35% in Pipe-PCG and up to 33% in Pipe-BiCGStab compared to two-stream design. For PETSc, the margin is even wider, up to 3.27x speed-up in both Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BiCGStab. Related code for the proposed method is available online: https://github.com/ParCoreLab/aCG 

Index Terms —Parallel processing, pipeline processing, iterative methods, sparse matrices, graphics processing units, parallel algorithms 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Overlapping communication with computation is a well-established approach to reducing communication cost. A com-mon example is the use of non-blocking send and/or receive 

operations to initiate individual message transfers, typically between neighboring processes to exchange halo data [1], [2]. When these messages make progress in the background, com-putation can proceed concurrently. This strategy is effective in both multi-CPU and multi-GPU settings. On CPUs, message progression relies on dedicated communication threads [3] or advanced MPI implementations [4]. On GPUs, overlap is typically realized with two concurrent streams, one for communication and one for computation [5], [6]. The conjugate gradient (CG) method [7], its preconditioned (PCG) variants and pipelined stabilized biconjugate gradi-ent (Pipe-BiCGStab) methods are canonical Krylov subspace solvers for sparse linear systems, which makes them a central building block in large-scale scientific applications. Prior work on communication overlap in CG has largely treated its two communication types separately: global collective communi-cation needed for parallelizing dot products and point-to-point (P2P) halo exchange in distributed sparse matrix-vector multi-plications (SpMV). The literature on pipelined Krylov methods addresses the former to lower the global synchronization overhead, by overlapping non-blocking reductions with the SpMV and preconditioner operations, and this idea is further extended through deeper pipelines or fewer reductions per iteration [8]–[11]. The pipelined CG algorithm first presented in [8] is theoretically capable of overlapping both collective and P2P communication with computation. The authors of that work, however, focused only on hiding collective operations. A separate line of work targets overlapping halo/P2P data exchanges with computation in iterative solvers and multigrid methods, primarily through scheduling and implementation techniques, such as specialized OpenMP threads [12], [13]. On GPUs, recent efforts improve the communication path through multi-GPU SpMV overlap, persistent kernels, and CPU- or GPU-initiated communication mechanisms [5], [14]– [16]. However, none of these works jointly schedule collective communication, halo exchange, and computation as concurrent components of a single design. In this work, we show that pipelined Krylov subspace solvers such as Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BiCGStab offer richer overlap opportunities than previously exploited. Beyond the traditional communication-computation overlap, they naturally allow communication-communication overlap, where collec-tive reductions and P2P halo exchanges proceed concurrently alongside computation. To exploit this, we propose a novel 

three-stream implementation of Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BiCGStab that overlaps three components: collective reduction, halo exchange, and SpMV. Our design builds on the NVIDIA Col-arXiv:2610.25058v1 [cs.DC] 18 Oct 2026 lective Communication Library (NCCL) [17], which provides native stream support for GPU communication. Our contributions are as follows:  

> •

We propose a three-stream overlap strategy for pipelined preconditioned CG and pipelined BiCGStab solvers that concurrently overlaps collective communication, P2P communication, and computation.  

> •

We evaluate our three-stream strategy against PETSc and a two-stream baseline [5]. Over this baseline, we achieve up to 35% (Leonardo, 256 GPUs) and 44% (MareNostrum 5, 64 GPUs) speedups for Pipe-PCG, with similar peak gains of 33% (MareNostrum5, 256 GPUs) and 42% (MareNostrum 5, 64 GPUs) for Pipe-BiCGStab compared to two-stream baseline. Beyond Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BiCGStab, the three-stream design can be generalized to any application combining high-bandwidth local data exchange with high-latency global coor-dination (e.g., convergence checks or global updates). II. B ACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION 

A. Pipelined Preconditioned CG (Pipe-PCG) 

Pipe-PCG methods [8] were developed to address the severe communication bottlenecks that limit the large-scale parallel scalability of standard PCG, where each iteration requires two separate dot products. In a distributed memory environment, each dot product forces a global synchronization event. These frequent synchronizations expose network latency and cause some processes to sit idle, creating a start-and-stop execution model that severely inhibits parallel efficiency [8]. Pipe-PCG directly targets this synchronization bottleneck by mathematically restructuring the solver to group the two dot products together, reducing the global synchronization to a single non-blocking phase per iteration. More importantly, this synchronization can be delayed until the preconditioner and SpMV are done. The Pipe-PCG implementation relies on the same fundamental compute kernels as the standard PCG method: vector additions (AXPY), dot products (DOT), and SpMV and a preconditioner. However, because Pipe-PCG restructures the algorithm, it introduces additional AXPY operations. Specifically, a single iteration of Pipe-PCG per-forms two DOT products combined, eight AXPY operations, one SpMV operation, and a preconditioning operation. The main computational difference from standard PCG is the use of eight AXPYs instead of three. Although these extra AXPYs increase the overall computational load, the benefit from reduced global synchronization amortizes this cost during large-scale executions [8]. 

B. Pipelined Stablizied Biconjugate Gradient (Pipe-BICGSTAB) 

Standard BICGSTAB suffers from even greater latency exposure than PCG, which requires three separate global reduction phases to compute four dot products per iteration. When scaled across distributed-memory architectures, these repeated synchronization points expose inter-GPU communi-cation latency and force processors into idle wait states, which inhibits the parallel performance at large scale. To overcome these communication stalls, Cools and Van-roose [18] introduced Pipe-BICGStab, which restructes the recurrences. By incorporating auxiliary vectors, the algorithm successfully fuses the dot products into just two global re-duction phases. Moreover, these reductions are formulated as non-blocking collectives that execute concurrently with the method’s two SpMVs, effectively hiding the network latency behind useful computation. While using the same core kernels (AXPY, DOT, SpMV), this reformulation increases local computational intensity. Each Pipe-BICGStab iteration executes six dot products (in two collective phases), eight AXPYs, and two SpMVs. Com-pared to standard BICGStab, it requires four extra AXPYs and expands vector storage from seven to eleven arrays. Despite increased resource demands, hiding inter-GPU communication behind computation yields substantial performance gains on massively parallel systems [18]. 

C. Beyond Traditional Computation-Communication Overlap 

Since these pipelined Krylov subspace solvers offer more overlap opportunities beyond the conventional computa-tion–communication model, this observation raises the ques-tion of whether multiple communication types can be over-lapped for greater performance gains. To answer this question, we first collected some results from nccl-tests and BabelStream microbenchmarks [19] to give an indication of relevant computation and communication costs. Specifically, on a system with 64 GPUs, nccl-test’s 

allreduce measurements show that it takes about 45.4 μs to perform an allreduce operation for two double-precision floating-point numbers, which is required to complete the global dot products during a Pipe-PCG iteration. The bench-mark also shows that a single P2P message takes approxi-mately the same amount of time when the message size is between 256 KiB and 512 KiB. Finally, based on BabelStream ,simple computations such as AXPY and DOT can process about 68.6 MB of data (corresponding to double arrays with 

∼8.6 million entries) in the same amount of time, i.e., 45 μs. This gives a rough idea of the amount of P2P communication or computation that will be needed to fully hide the collective (allreduce ) communication that occurs in Pipe-PCG. Furthermore, we developed a custom three-stream mi-crobenchmark that concurrently executes P2P communication, collective communication, and computation, to see the overlap effect. Specifically, the benchmark consists of an allreduce col-lective on two double-precision scalars (16 bytes), a send/recv 

operation forming a simple ring topology with a payload of 32,000 doubles ( 256 KiB), and a DAXPY compute kernel processing 64 MB of data. This section describes the two-stream and three-stream Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BICGSTAB implementations in detail. We use the two-stream design as the baseline because it achieves state-of-the-art performance among CG implementations [5]. We implement both the two- and three-stream designs for NVIDIA GPUs using NCCL [17] for communication, which natively supports GPU streams for asynchronous and overlap-ping GPU operations. Previous work [5], [20] showed that on supercomputers NCCL exhibits comparable performance to GPU-aware MPI. 

A. Multi-GPU Pipelined PCG and Pipelined BICGSTAB 

Executing Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BICGStab in a distributed-memory parallel setting requires partitioning the sparse matrix and its associated vectors. We utilize a row-wise partitioning strategy to divide the sparse matrix A, distributing the as-signed rows and corresponding vector segments to each rank. Following this distribution, each GPU becomes responsible for its specific partition, allowing the rest of the computations to take place locally within each GPU’s memory. 

1) Computation: In GPU implementations of CG methods, the best practice [21] is to offload the compute kernels to the GPU and handle intermediate steps such as convergence checks and scalar calculations on the CPU. For the aforemen-tioned compute kernels, we rely on vendor-optimized BLAS libraries, using cuBLAS for local dot product and cuSPARSE for SpMV, which employs merge-based SpMV kernels [22]. Although vendor libraries offer easier implementation in AXPY operations, going back and forth between host and device to launch the kernels and move scalars between host and device after their calculation may introduce an additional overhead as pointed out by Trotter et al. [5]. Therefore, we fused the eight AXPY operations in a single compute kernel together with calculations of α and β scalars on the device to mitigate this inefficiency in Pipe-PCG. A similar fusion approach is also applied in Pipe-BICGSTAB, but it is structured differently due to its algorithmic difference. The AXPY operations are grouped into three distinct fused kernels as it can be seen in Figure 1-b, while the scalar computations are performed in a separate CUDA kernel rather than being fused with the AXPYs. For the preconditioning step in Pipe-PCG, we selected the Jacobi preconditioner due to its simplicity and low computa-tional overhead. It is defined as the diagonal of the system matrix ( M = diag (A)) and its inverse M −1 is obtained by taking the reciprocals of the diagonal elements. Since it is not available in vendor libraries, we implemented it ourselves. 

2) Communication: Parallel dot product operations require collective allreduce operations to sum up the partial dot prod-uct results from every GPU into a single global value to ensure the mathematical correctness of the distributed algorithm. The second type of the communication required in pipelined CG and pipelined BICGSTAB concerns SpMV. More specif-ically, in a distributed SpMV (y = Ax ) with row-wise partitioning, each GPU computes a partial output vector from its assigned rows. Because GPUs only require elements of the vector x that correspond to the non-zero columns in their assigned matrix partition, SpMV generates an irregular point-to-point communication pattern. To manage this efficiently and hide network latency, the assigned matrix partition is conceptually divided into local and remote components, often referred to as diagonal and off-diagonal parts, respectively. B. Two-Stream Approach 

We exploit the opportunity to overlap the irregular P2P communication of distributed memory SpMV with compu-tation on the local part of the sparse matrix using GPU streams, as illustrated in Figure 1. Our implementation builds on a state-of-the-art approach [5], which combines CUDA streams with NCCL to avoid host-side synchro-nizations. Since NCCL is stream-aware, GPU kernels for packing, sending, receiving and unpacking messages can be queued asynchronously from the CPU without incurring CPU-GPU synchronization overhead. Dependencies between the P2P stream and compute stream are handled asyn-chronously through CUDA events ( cudaEventRecord and 

cudaStreamWaitEvent ), which further eliminates CPU-GPU synchronization and hides kernel launch latencies. As shown in Figure 1-a for Pipe-PCG, once the precon-ditioning step completes, synchronization is established by asynchronously recording an event on the compute stream and making the P2P stream wait on that event. Conversely, the P2P stream records an event upon completing the P2P communication, and the compute stream thus waits on this event before proceeding with the off-diagonal part of the SpMV. Similarly in Pipe-BICGSTAB as shown in Figure 1-b, once the first group of fused AXPY operations complete, compute stream signals to P2P stream and initiates the halo exchange operations. This ensures that the required vector data is ready for both the SpMV operation and the P2P transfer. Non-blocking point-to-point communication is implemented using grouped calls to ncclSend and ncclRecv , enclosed by ncclGroupStart and ncclGroupEnd .

C. Three-Stream Approach 

In the two-stream strategy, the collective communication required for the dot products remains a synchronization bot-tleneck, unable to be overlapped with other data transfers or computations. To achieve the triple overlap, we propose a novel three-stream implementation of Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BICGSTAB. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the collective operations are offloaded to a dedicated Collective stream. The core mathematical computations are maintained in the primary 

Compute stream, and the P2P data transfers remain isolated in their own dedicated P2P stream. However, placing the collective in a separate stream is not sufficient to achieve true overlap with P2P transfers. Fig. 2-a shows the profiling results for a naive three-stream design based on a single NCCL communicator. In this case, only one communication operation overlaps with computation, while the others remain queued until the active transfer finishes. As discussed in Sec. III-A2, irregular P2P communication is required to fetch remote x values, which are packed before transfer and unpacked on arrival. In the naive design, however, NCCL serializes the two communication operations and, in this case, prioritizes the collective. Consequently, in Pipe-PCG implementation, the P2P transfer is delayed until the Allreduce completes, which in turn causes the off-diagonal SpMV to execute much later than the diagonal SpMV because the required remote x values are not yet available. Similarly in Pipe-BICGSTAB, as shown in Fig. 1-b, we start overlapping halo exchange operations after the first group of AXPYs, and since NCCL serializes communications in the background, Allreduce delays until the completion of halo exchange opera-tions and we waste the opportunity of having a wider overlap window. The Pipelined-CG was introduced to reduce the cost of global synchronization in Krylov methods. Ghysels and Van-roose reformulated PCG so that a single nonblocking reduction is issued per iteration and overlapped with the matrix-vector product and preconditioner [8]. Later work extended this idea with deep pipelines that hide a reduction behind multiple future SpMVs when one iteration does not provide sufficient work [9]. More recent pipelined-PCG variants further reduce reduction frequency by overlapping one allreduce with work from two iterations [10]. However, this line of work only targets global dot product latency. Moreover, although non-blocking collectives were introduced in MPI-3, their effective-ness on CPU clusters depends on factors such as asynchronous progress and runtime support [28]. A separate line of work focuses on halo/P2P commu-nication overlap in iterative solvers and multigrid meth-ods. These approaches aim to overlap halo exchange in the SpMV or smoother/preconditioner phases with computation. For example, Nakajima et al. combine halo overlap with OpenMP dynamic loop scheduling [12], and later study pro-cess/thread allocation in hybrid MPI+OpenMP settings to optimize communication-computation overlap in a CG solver with a multigrid preconditioner [13]. Thus, these works do not address the joint scheduling of halo exchange and collective reductions. On GPU systems, Bernaschi et al. combine communication-reduced CG with overlap in the multi-GPU SpMV [14]. Ismayilov et al. use persistent kernels, device-side barriers, and device-initiated communication to remove the CPU from the critical path in multi-GPU CG [15]. Trotter et al. [5] compare CPU- and GPU-initiated communication strategies for CG and pipelined CG across GPU-aware MPI, NCCL/RCCL, and NVSHMEM. However none of these works consider triple overlap in their implementations. The closest prior work to ours is Selvitopi et al. [29], who propose a rearrangement for iterative solvers in which irregular SpMV P2P messages are embedded into a collective commu-nication phase so that P2P and collective communication can be performed in a single phase. Although this mixes the two communication classes, it is not a pipelined-CG formulation that treats collective reduction, halo exchange, and SpMV as three concurrent components. Outside CG, MPI+ULT [30] argues that user-level threads can overlap computation with both P2P and collective MPI communication, but it does not demonstrate a true three-way overlap of collective, P2P, and compute. Lina [31] studies distributed Mixture-of-Experts training, where all-to-all and allreduce coexist with computation on separate streams. It shows that naive concurrency between these communication classes can hurt performance due to network contention, and therefore prioritizes all-to-all over allreduce. Our approach differs in that we overlap collective communication with P2P exchange, yielding a different communication pattern and less pressure on the network bandwidth. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We have introduced the three-stream technique for over-lapping two different types of communication - high volume, bandwidth limited P2P messages and low volume latency limited collectives. The idea can be implemented efficiently on GPU systems using three separate streams for concurrency, making use of modern GPU communication libraries such as NCCL or RCCL which can handle communication in streams. Our experimental results on Pipe-PCG and Pipe-BICGStab indicate that the technique works as intended, yielding sub-stantial performance improvements when scaling to a large number of GPUs. In future work, we intend to explore other applications and plan to implement the three-stream technique for AMD GPUs. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

To analyze the experimental outputs, we extract the solver runtimes from the log files and calculate the average across the three trials for each configuration of matrix, solver, and GPU count. Using these average runtimes, we compute the performance speedup for N GPUs by dividing the baseline two-stream runtime by the runtime of the three-stream imple-mentation for that specific matrix and GPU allocation.

Artifact Execution 

Each experiment is a Slurm job script that runs the six solver variants, three times each, for one matrix and one number of nodes. The job scripts are located in 

> scripts/$ {system }/suitesparse/$ {matrix }/scripts/$ {system }/poisson3da/poisson3da_12/

and are named 

> ${matrix }-rtol-0-$ {nodes }-nodes-${gpus }-procs.sh

where ${nodes } and ${gpus } are zero-padded to three and four digits. For example, Queen 4147 on 4 nodes (16 GPUs) of Leonardo is run with:   

> $sbatch scripts/leonardo/suitesparse/ Queen_4147/ Queen_4147-rtol-0-004-nodes-0016-procs.sh

There are 30 SuiteSparse jobs per system (6 matrices on 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 nodes) and 3 (MareNostrum 5: 16, 32 and 64 nodes) or 4 (Leonardo: 16, 32, 64 and 128 nodes) jobs for poisson3da 12. All jobs of a system can be submitted with:       

> $for jin scripts/$ {system }/*/*/*.sh; do sbatch $j; done

Within the 8-hour evaluation budget, we suggest running the 30 SuiteSparse jobs on one system (about 30 node hours) and, if the allocation permits, the 16-node poisson3da 12 job. The output of each run is written to 

> results/acg/$ {date }/$ {variant }/$ {matrix }/

A run that exits with a non-zero status keeps the suffix .tmp ;this is expected for the PCG variants, which stop at the iteration limit, and these files are used in the analysis.

(A100 on Leonardo). From this file, the figures of the paper are produced with Python 3, pandas and matplotlib. The script figure2x3.py plots the six SuiteSparse matrices and

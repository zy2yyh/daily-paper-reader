# Abstract 

The extreme collective completion time (CCT) demands of AI workloads challenge existing packet spraying algorithms, which can have trouble efficiently load-balancing workloads that are sent at full line rates. We trace this to a structural cause: on a fat tree, once a packet picks its upward path, the downward path to its des-tination is unique, so destination-oblivious schemes cannot undo the imbalance it creates. We prove that such schemes can suffer from Θ(√m) queueing for messages of size m, thus eventually triggering rate reductions by the congestion control. Instead, we suggest O FAN , a switch-based destination-aware LB scheme that can reach O(1) queueing. We also present its 

> P

OFAN variant that fits the pipe-based architecture of current switches. Our P4 implementation shows that it consumes mod-est resources. An end-to-end FSDP2 evaluation with Llama-3 405B-parameter models shows that O FAN cuts CCT inflation by 16–39 × when compared to existing algorithms. 

# 1 Introduction 

Thirty years of load balancing. Datacenter networks offer a rich set of parallel paths, and a long line of load-balancing (LB) algorithms has been proposed to exploit them. That line was long constrained by the transport protocol’s intoler-ance for reordering, and so operated at a coarse granularity: per-flow hashing [36] and its weighted variants [60, 88], sub-flows [1, 71, 74], host-based path adaptation [31, 72, 77], and flowlet switching [41, 42, 82]. All of these pin a unit of traf-fic, whether a flow, a subflow, or a flowlet, to a single path for its lifetime. On the contrary, relying on NICs that accept out-of-order delivery [18, 62, 78], spraying algorithms load-balance at the granularity of a single packet. These include (i) random packet spraying (RPS) [26] as in Alibaba’s Stel-lar [56], (ii) host-based congestion-aware spraying algorithms like REPS and MRC [5, 8, 49] as in the Ultra Ethernet Consor-tium [18], and (iii) switch-based algorithms that steer pack-ets based on local congestion like DRILL [33] and Nvidia’s

Spectrum-X [45,63,64] adaptive routing (AR) [27,34], or also take into account congestion at neighboring switches [3, 70], like Broadcom’s GLB and ByteDance’s SGLB. 

AI training. Recent AI training workloads have new demands. They focus on minimizing collective completion time (CCT), i.e., the worst-case flow completion time among all flows in a collective, and therefore push every source to send at 100% of its line rate [21, 22, 40]. Unfortunately, as Fig. 1 shows, existing algorithms fail to meet this demand. The results display the limits of current state-of-the-art spraying algorithms. In this paper, we claim that all these issues come from a sub-optimal load balancing: as packets do not reach all queues equally, some queues become overloaded (high queue sizes) while others remain underutilized (low utilization), thus impacting the CCT. OFAN . In the paper, we introduce O FAN , a new LB algorithm that relies on three key ideas: 

(1) Per-destination LB. In fat trees, LB has freedom only on the way up, while the path down to a destination is unique. Existing switch-based LB schemes typically select outgo-ing port to optimize upward LB. This balances the north-bound links beautifully. But once a packet has picked its upward path, no downstream switch can correct a bad choice. A locally-efficient but destination-oblivious LB scheme there-fore spreads each destination’s traffic unevenly when going up, and that unevenness cannot be addressed when going down. In this paper, we consider random permutation workloads with message size m and assume that all hosts blast traffic at 100% load. We prove that randomized spraying algorithms incur an average queue size of Ω(√m) (Thm. 1). We also show that in evaluations, additional destination-oblivious al-gorithms follow a similar heuristic growth. In practice, these bounds mean that as message sizes grow, queues grow, and therefore congestion control algorithms (CCAs) will be forced by ECNs and PFCs to slow down sending rates and increase CCTs, hurting AI training performance. Instead, inspired by host-based DRB [16] and E THE -

Using N D /D/1queueing models [58, 76], we show that destination-based ro-tation can achieve optimal O (1) queue bounds even at 100% loads (Thm. 3). 

(2) Switch-based LB. We argue that the switches are a better home for DR than the hosts. First, the switch already knows the full topology and its link failures using BGP, while the host must either implement BGP in the NIC or keep re-inferring it indirectly. Second, and this is the property we find most surprising, a switch can consolidate traffic for a given desti-nation, making packets from different sources share the same rotation pointer. A host, seeing only its own flows, cannot do this.

Pipes aggregate a few ports (e.g., 4 ports at 800Gb/s each) and guarantee atomic Read-Modify-Write (RMW) accesses to local registers at line rate. Thus, we in-troduce POFAN , a more practical variant of O FAN that stores a local pointer at each pipe for each destination instead of a shared pointer accessible across the whole switch.

# 2 Background and Methodology 2.1 AI Training Workloads 

AI training alternates rounds of computation with rounds of communication, where the communication is one of a few standard collectives, such as AllReduce, AllGather, and All-toAll, drawn from a small set of libraries [21, 22, 52]. Two properties of this traffic matter for load balancing: 

Uniform. The nodes in a collective run the same commu-nication library and the same transport and NICs, and most collectives are symmetric: every participant has the same amount of data to send, with the same packet size. 

> 1This paper is a follow-up on [57], which had two parts: (1) systematically evaluating leading load-balancing designs, while decoupling them from specific congestion control and loss recovery stacks; and (2) introducing and studying O FAN . This paper focuses on the second part.

2Synchronous. All nodes begin at roughly the same time [1]. This cuts both ways. It helps load balancing, since there are no newly arriving flows to adapt to mid-collective. But it also means that every source steps through its packets in lockstep, which can cause a load balancer’s port choices to synchronize across sources and degrade catastrophically (as shown in §3). Together with the last-byte nature of CCT, these properties also mean that the traffic is long-lived and gapless: techniques that rebalance during idle periods, such as PLB or flowlet switching, are less helpful. We also assume the cluster runs the job in isolation, as is common at scale [31, 47, 71]. Throughout this paper, we will focus on three types of generic traffic matrices that reflect generic AI training work-loads [85]: (i) random permutations , (ii) all-to-all , and (iii) MoE (all-to-all-v) . In particular, we will show that all-to-all is the easier one to load-balance, and all-to-all-v suffers from a large incast that reduces the impact of load-balancing. Therefore, we will most focus on permutations, where the LB scheme can make the biggest impact. 

# 2.2 Isolating the Load-Balancing Layer 

Load balancing does not run alone: congestion control sets sending rates, and loss recovery decides what to retransmit. Both react to the queues that load balancing creates, so mea-suring a full stack conflates the three. Our methodology in the analysis is therefore to remove the other two and measure load balancing on its own (as in [57]). In evaluations (§6), we add CCAs back, and find that they do not change the main results, including the ranking among LB algorithms. 

Congestion control. A perfect load balancer makes the net-work’s entire capacity available, and an ideal congestion con-trol algorithm (CCA) would provide each host with its fair share of that capacity. Under uniform AI collectives, the fair share is the same for all hosts. Thus, we cancel the CCA and allow instead each host to send at the maximum fraction of the line rate that the network can support, which we call ρmax .In a fault-free fabric, each host can blast its traffic at full line rate. Under failures, ρmax is the highest rate at which all flows could send if traffic were balanced perfectly across all valid shortest paths. In addition, if a host has several flows, it simply performs round-robin (RR) to schedule them. Any queue that then forms, and any capacity left unused, is attributable to the load balancer and nothing else. This is what Fig. 1 measures. 

Loss recovery. Similarly, we attempt to avoid any losses: we assume large buffers and do not consider failed links (unless mentioned). This idealization is deliberate, and it is what lets us state an optimality criterion at all: a bound proved against a fixed-rate source is a property of the load balancer, not of a particular CCA’s reaction to it. It is also what makes the criterion de-manding, since there is no rate reduction available to rescue a scheme whose queues grow.

# 3.2 The Downlink Problem 

Throughout the paper, we consider three-level fat-tree topolo-gies. In such topologies, LB flexibility exists strictly in up-ward paths, while downward paths to any destination are fixed. Consequently, while existing destination-oblivious LB algo-rithms may perfectly optimize for local LB properties on the way up, they may also distribute traffic bound for the same edge or aggregation switch unevenly across upward paths, thus creating downstream bottlenecks. Fig. 2(a) illustrates how it is not sufficient for switches to simply balance traffic on the up routes. Each switch in the senders pod in the figure nicely sends half of its ingress traffic on each link, thus all up links are equally loaded. However, in this extreme example, all traffic to any destination edge switch ends up on the same aggregation switch in the senders pod. While this is fine for the up routes, this can cause collisions on the down routes, where destinations determine the route, not the LB. For example, the red and green paths meet at the first aggregation switch of the first pod, and therefore have to meet again at the first aggregation switch of the second pod (both drawn in black), and therefore conflict on the way down. In Fig. 2(b), however, switches not only balance load equally across all uplinks, they also ensure that the traffic sent to a particular destination is spread evenly. This ensures that there are no collisions on the down routes as an equal share of each flow traverses every possible path. Thus, this example shows how a destination-oblivious scheme can balance one side of the network and leave the other to chance, effectively causing statistical imbalance. Fig. 3 quantifies the imbalance. We plot the worst-case link overload beyond the ideal, uniform amount, at each of the four inter-switch stages: edge to aggregation ( E → A), aggregation to core ( A → C), core to aggregation ( C → A), and aggregation to edge ( A → E). We assume a random inter-pod permutation that forces all flows to cross pods and in which all links are ideally equally loaded. The asymmetry is stark. For example, S IMPLE RR and JSQ achieve essentially perfect balance on the two uplink stages and then collapse on the two 

downlink stages; C HOICE and DRILL also exhibit similar profiles; the other destination-oblivious schemes suffer at both

# 3.3 Queue-Size Lower Bounds 

We now want to provide a fundamental analysis of the per-formance of each LB scheme. We consider n hosts in a three-level k-ary fat tree without link failures. The hosts perform a random permutation collective, such that each host sends a single flow to a distinct random host (random derangement). All hosts send traffic at line rate, buffers are infinite, and there are no packet losses nor ACKs. We define optimality in terms of the average queue length that a scheme produces. Collectives can have a large range of message sizes [85], and we want to ensure that as they get larger, the queue size does not grow without bound. For a message size m, let q(m) be the average queue size across 

all switch queues, over the time it takes for all hosts to finish sending. An optimal load-balancing algorithm is one that achieves q(m) = O (1), independent of m. We also decompose 

q(m) = q↑(m)+ q↓(m) to obtain the contribution of the uplink and downlink queues, respectively. Queue size is the right target for several reasons. First, it is strongly correlated with CCT performance: given fixed prop-agation delay and our assumptions of uniform traffic, ideal 4sending rates, and zero loss, queueing is the dominant vari-able component of the CCT, as we validate in §3.5. Second, it is also a dominant factor in congestion control, as it incurs ECNs, PFCs and buffer overflows. Third, it directly affects host NIC reassembly buffer size and complexity. Finally, an 

## O (1) queue is valuable in itself, since it makes performance predictable. 

Square-root growth. We find that in algorithms based on random spraying, both uplink and downlink queues grow as the square root of the message size. The proof, presented in Appendix B, is based on the fact that the number of packets that land on a given uplink or downlink is a sum of inde-pendent random variables, each variable corresponding to whether a packet lands on that link. It finds that the sum has a standard deviation of √m, which translates to a queue growth of √m. For downlinks, the proof relies on an approximation that the variations in uplink queue delays are negligible. Later, we show that simulations confirm the square-root growth (Fig. 4(b)).

The cause is synchronization , and it is a direct consequence of the workload uniformity described in §2: all senders transmit the same message and packet size, paced at identical rates. This uniformity creates per-sender “stickiness,” in which succes-sive packets from the same sender are forwarded out the same port to the same aggregation switch. For example, consider again an edge switch whose w = k/2 flows all go northbound through its w ports, and assume S IMPLE RR. Since all flows are paced at the same rate, their packets arrive in the same order every period. So if the pointer sends a packet of some flow to port i, the pointer advances exactly w times before that flow’s next packet, and returns to port i: the flow ends up sticking to port i for the whole run. Given the fat tree, such sticky packets necessarily arrive at the same aggregation switch in the destination pod, then stick to the same path all the way down—where they collide with other sticky flows. Note that local salting does not help: even if each switch starts S IMPLE RR at a random port and pro-ceeds in a different periodic order, the same synchronization arises. We discuss this in detail in Appendix C. 

# 3.4 Destination-Based Rotation 

Given these results, we looked to the broader scheduling liter-ature for approaches that reach O (1). We find inspiration in the DRB scheduler of Cao et al. [16], whose key idea is that each source host load-balances packets in round-robin fashion on a per-destination basis. We generalize this to a scheduling discipline we call Destination-Based Rotation (DR). We later contribute a switch-based DR scheme in §4. 

DRB as host DR. DRB maintains round-robin pointers at each source host, one per (destination host, quantized packet size) pair, using two quanta to separate data and ACK packets. For each (host, size) pair it finds the lowest common switch layer in the topology and round-robins among the switches at that layer with a single pointer. For example, each colored source host in Fig. 2 sends its data packets to the four cores in round-robin order; this is done using encapsulation. Under failures, DRB assumes hosts have full topology knowledge and round-robins only over switches reachable by both end-points. 

Bounded queues. Between source and destination, DRB flows are load-balanced equally and periodically across all allowed paths, so any given path sees a sum of many periodic flows with random phases. We can therefore model DRB’s queues with the N D /D/1 model [58,76], which is particularly noteworthy for its bounded average queue size even when the arrival rate equals the service rate (this does not happen in M/M/1 for example). We prove the following result in Ap-pendix D. Note that the proof here is weaker than for Thms. 1 and 2, as we prove it for the DRB N D /D/1 approximation, not for the exact algorithm.

Deployment challenges. Both schemes ask for something hosts do not have today, exhibiting significant deployment challenges. DRB requires each host to know the complete set of currently reachable switches, which in practice means running the fabric’s routing protocol (e.g., BGP) on the NIC. Exposing routing and link-state updates to hosts violates the layering that separates them from the fabric’s internals, and requires every NIC to maintain global topology state and re-converge on every failure. E THEREAL requires the NIC to maintain a very large number of queue pairs (QPs) per flow (one per core, i.e., on the order of 1K with 64-port switches) in order to place each subflow on its own path. Increasing the number of QPs by 3 orders of magnitude could prove daunting at scale. Both limitations are consequences of running per-destination load balancing at the host . A host sees only its own flows and has no native view of the fabric. As we show next, a switch sees both.

# 4 OFAN 

# 4.1 Algorithm 

§3.4 left us with a discipline that is optimal but has only ever been realized at the host, where it is impractical. This section makes the case that the switch, rather than the host, is the natural home for DR, and presents O FAN , a switch-based realization of it. 

Switch benefits. We find that the switch enjoys two significant benefits when implementing DR. First, the switch already knows the topology. It knows through BGP which links are currently alive. DRB needs the host to obtain this, e.g., by implementing somehow BGP onto the NIC; E THEREAL keeps iterating to guess it from the host’s viewpoint. A switch gets it for free, and re-converges quickly on failure through the mechanisms it already runs. Second, and less obviously, the switch can see more traf-fic. This enables it to apply round-robin algorithms more efficiently, as described below. 

Overview of O FAN . OFAN 2 is a switch-based realization of DR scheduling. Its goal is to maintain per-destination point-ers at edge and aggregation switches, and use these pointers to select the outgoing ports for northbound packets. How-ever, there are many possible destination hosts, leading to an impractical number of pointers to implement. To address this scalability challenge, O FAN exploits the structured nature of a fat-tree topology to consolidate desti-nations based on their location in the tree, such that a switch only maintains a single pointer for groups of destinations that share a common edge or aggregation switch. The idea is that a switch does not need to care about the exact destination of its northbound packets, but can consolidate these packets based on their common last-mile switches. Specifically, an edge switch E will maintain one pointer for each group of destinations that share an edge switch F (F̸ =

E). Similarly, an aggregation switch A maintains one pointer for each group of destinations that share an aggregation switch 

B (B̸ = A). Hence, an edge switch on the northbound path will round-robin all traffic bound for the same destination edge switch, and an aggregation switch will round-robin traffic bound for the same destination aggregation switch. OFAN ’s consolidation of destinations significantly reduces the number of pointers, while retaining DR’s property of bal-

To see this, consider two flows that originate at source hosts under source edge switch E, and are destined to destination hosts under destination edge switch F. Then E and F are mandatory waypoints in their path. Therefore, E can maintain a single pointer that consolidates both flows. Consolidation is even more effective at the aggregation layer due to the properties of three-level fat-trees. In a fat-tree, all packets destined for a pod and come through a source aggregation switch in their uplink path can only go through a single aggregation switch in their downlink path. 3 This destination aggregation switch is again a mandatory waypoint, hence a source aggregation switch can consolidate its scheduling of all packets destined to this pod. 

Per-destination pointers. The core idea in O FAN is to have 

a pointer per destination , and each incoming packet that goes up in the fat tree accesses its specific pointer to determine its northbound port. For the consolidation explained above, we want to have at each source edge switch a different pointer per destination edge switch, and at each source aggregation switch a different pointer per destination pod. Assume that each such switch has w = k/2 northbound ports. At start up, each pointer is initialized to a random value in {0, . . . , w − 1}.Then, at each packet arrival for this pointer, it is incremented (modulo the number w of uplink ports). 

Random port order. A simple way is for each packet to be sent to the port indicated by the pointer. For example, if the pointer for the packet’s destination indicates 3, then it will be sent to port 3. However, to avoid a possible synchroniza-tion across pointers, we don’t want all of them to go RR in the same order. # 5 POFAN Pipe-Based Implementation 5.1 POFAN 

Pipes. Modern switch ASICs are organized into several par-allel packet-processing pipes (Broadcom) or slices (Cisco), each handling a fixed subset of the chip’s ports with dedi-cated stateful hardware. Because ports within the same pipe pass through identical hardware execution stages, the pipeline natively guarantees atomic Read-Modify-Write access to lo-cal registers at line rate without cross-pipe locks. Hardware pipelines historically scaled around 4-port blocks per pro-cessing engine: Broadcom’s Tomahawk 3 and 4 utilize 4 ports of 400G per pipeline (1.6 Tbps per pipe) [13, 83], while 51.2 Tbps generation ASICs such as Cisco’s Silicon One G200 and P200 explicitly implement 16 independent slices of 4 × 800G ports (3.2 Tbps per slice) [79]. Thus, we focus here on pipes of four ports. 

> P

OFAN . This pipe-based organization motivates an easier-to-implement variant, POFAN : instead of a single RR pointer shared among all bottom ports for a given destination key, as in O FAN ’s pipeline above, each pipe keeps its own indepen-dent RR pointer per key (e.g., with 4O FAN , the RR pointer in each pipe is shared by four inputs). This sacrifices some con-solidation, and hence some of O FAN ’s load-balancing quality, since we are effectively splitting each pointer into several in-dependent, uncorrelated ones, similar in spirit to DRB’s many per-flow pointers (§4), in exchange for a design that fits di-rectly within existing switch architectures, with no cross-pipe combining hardware required. By default, we assume pipes of 4 ports and use 4O FAN , matching current switch ASICs; we explore the impact of this choice on CCT performance in §6. 

Example. For our running example ( k = 64, so w = k/2 = 32 downlink ports) with 8 pipes of 4 ports each, POFAN replaces each of O FAN ’s N ≈ 4K shared pointers with 8 independent, per-pipe pointers, for a total of 8 × 4K ≈ 32 K pointers in the switch. 

Bounded queue sizes. POFAN keeps the same bounded queueing model as DRB and O FAN (proof in Appendix H): 

Theorem 7. The modeled average queue size for POFAN is bounded: q(m) = Θ (1) . (7) The intuition is that splitting a pointer into several independent ones keeps each of them balanced, so the load per destination on every link stays balanced as well. The price is a larger number of pointers, and hence a larger queue bound constant.

# 6.7 Training Scenario 

Finally, we evaluate the performance of each LB scheme on a realistic AI training scenario. Specifically, we simulate training Llama-3 [55] (8B, 70B, and 405B) parallelized us-ing Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP2) [29, 75, 87] with HSDP configuration on a 1,024-GPU cluster. The cluster is organized as 128 servers of 8 GPUs, and we assume random server placement. We evaluate the main network bottleneck in FSDP2 training by focusing on the backward pass, as it combines a ReduceScatter of gradients and an AllGather of weights. FSDP2 traffic is implemented as a hierarchical ring, where intra-server communications are performed in the high-bandwidth domain (scale-up, like NVLink), while our fat-tree network implements inter-server communications (scale-out) using 8 parallel rings: each logical GPU i is connected to log-ical GPU i + 8 (mod 1 , 024 ). Because sharding is 1 ,024-way while each ring spans only 128 GPUs, a ring step carries one 1/1,024 shard rather than 1 /128 of a layer; the intra-server AllGather then completes the remaining 7 /8. We assume FP8 precision and 4 KB packet payload, translating to 119 packets per flow per layer in Llama-3 8B (32 layers), 418 in Llama-3 70B (80 layers) and 1533 in Llama-3 405B (126 layers). We enable both the NSCC and MSwift CCAs with loss recovery, and measure the CCT of each collective phase. As shown in Fig. 14, while REPS and AR overheads re-main about constant, the relative CCT increase for O FAN 

drops as model size scales. When compared against the best out of the two existing models, at 405B parameters, O FAN 

(resp. 4O FAN ) reduces the CCT increase beyond baseline by 16 × (resp. 15 ×) with NSCC and 39 × (resp. 36 ×) with MSwift. # 7 Discussion 

Realizing O FAN ’s pointers at line rate. §5 presented POFAN ,the variant that sidesteps cross-pipe coordination by keeping an independent pointer per pipe. It is fair to ask what it would take to implement O FAN ’s shared pointers exactly, without that approximation. The difficulty is concurrent access: a switch processes many packets per cycle, and several of them may be bound for the same destination switch and therefore contend for the same round-robin register. This is a classical problem with many processors issuing fetch-and-add to a shared counter. Its classical solution, a combining network [46, 50], merges duplicate requests before they reach the counter. The general machinery is too costly and too variable in latency for a switch pipeline, but our problem is far narrower: a small, fixed set of local pointers. In Appendix I we show that a fixed-latency, four-stage pipeline suffices. It sorts the batch by pointer key, combines packets sharing a key, updates each key’s register once by the group’s count, then decombines and unsorts so each packet leaves with its own port. A back-of-envelope estimate puts this at roughly 10 5 gates and a few milliwatts, against a switch ASIC that draws hundreds of watts: negligible. We therefore regard O FAN ’s exact form as implementable, with POFAN as the pragmatic near-term option. 

# 8 Related Work 

There is a vast literature on load balancing in datacenters, and we cannot hope to summarize it in any depth. The ci-tations in Table 1 provide a large subset. Many proposed systems [31,41,72,82] do not seriously consider packet spray-ing given its potential adverse effects on the transport due to reordering. Some systems do include spraying in their de-sign [4,14,35,68], but often introduce specialized mechanisms to make spraying work (e.g., proposing an entirely new trans-port). Two exceptions are [26] and [8], where packet spraying is evaluated against ECMP and some other load balancing granularities, but one transport is assumed and the distance from optimality is unclear. We already discussed throughout the paper many LB schemes that we compare against: switch- and host-based spraying in §1 and Table 1, and the destination-based DRB and E THEREAL in §3.4. 

Fat-tree routing in HPC. Routing in fat trees has long been studied for HPC interconnects [27]. Deterministic routing sends all the traffic to a given destination along a fixed path, as in ECMP, without packet spraying. In the paper we focus more on packet spraying, which can achieve better load balancing. 

Load-balanced routers. Destination-based rotation has roots in three-stage load-balanced router algorithms such as UFS, FOFF and Padded Frames [39, 43, 44]. Load-balanced routers spread the traffic of each flow cyclically over intermediate ports to guarantee full throughput. 

SGLB. Broadcom offers a Global Load Balancing (GLB) fea-ture in its Tomahawk chips [59, 70]. However, GLB is limited to 3-stage Clos networks with at most 64 edge switches, and cannot run end-to-end on a 5-layer fat-tree topology [59]. # 9 Conclusion 

AI training pushes every source to 100% of line rate, leav-ing no slack to hide imperfect load balancing: queues grow with the message size and utilization stalls. Because a fat tree’s downward path to a destination is unique, destination-oblivious schemes cannot undo the imbalance they create on the way up, as we proved for randomized spraying using an 

Ω(√m) queueing lower bound. OFAN instead rotates per destination in the switch, con-solidating all traffic sharing a destination onto one pointer. Moreover, we showed how its 4O FAN variant fits commodity ASICs and cuts CCT inflation by 15–36 × in an end-to-end FSDP2 evaluation at 405B parameters.

# A Permutation CCT Lower-Bound 

Outline. Our goal is to provide a tight lower-bound baseline for the CCT of a permutation workload when hosts send at 100% of the link rate. This baseline is used in all of the fig-ures that study the CCT increase caused by a load-balancing scheme beyond this baseline. 

Example. With our default assumptions (§6), we get below a lower bound of 17 .05694 μs for a message size m = 256 . We want to compare that to smallest value that we can get. We only send a flow from the first host to the last and back in a small network ( k = 4), using all algorithms, iterate 1000 times, and take the minimum over all possibilities, we measure in practice a value of 17 .0587 μs (with k = 8, we get 17 .0609 μs, which is less tight). That is, the difference is 10 −4 of the CCT, less than a packet time, showing the tightness of the lower bound.

# C Proof of Thm. 2: Synchronization 

Intuition for synchronization. We prove the synchronization properties of S IMPLE RR and JSQ. The synchronization derives from three properties: 

1. Perfect match for first packets. Consider an edge switch without internal traffic. Assume that flow 1 sends the first packet, which is forwarded to uplink port i. Each of the other flows then sends one packet that is forwarded to a distinct uplink port: (i) In RR, because of the round-robin rotation; and (ii) in AR, because the queues of these ports are initially empty, then any port that is assigned a packet gets a queue size of 1 and is removed from consideration for a duration T .At the end, there is a perfect 1-to-1 match of packets to uplink ports. 

2. Sticky flows. When the second packet of flow 1 arrives after a period T : (i) in RR , it will be sent again to port i due to the round-robin periodic rotation, and (ii) in AR , the first packet of flow 1 will have just left, since it takes LPKT /C < T to service it. Thus port i is the only empty port and the second picks it. In both cases, port i is a sticky port for flow 1. Likewise, each other flow will have its own sticky port. 

3. Aggregation switch layout. Now we understand how flows can be stuck onto specific edge-aggregation links. In addition, in a fat tree, a flow stuck to aggregation switch i of a source pod will also be stuck to aggregation switch i of a destination pod (black circles in Fig. 2). Thus, two flows from different source pods but destined to the same edge switch may have picked the same aggregation switch i, leading to contention at the link from the destination aggregation switch to the destination edge switch and to linear growth of its queue. 

Proof outline. We first bound the probability that a flow is sticky (Step 1). We then show that an aggregation-to-edge downlink shared by several sticky flows sees its queue grow linearly (Step 2). Since sticky flows from distinct source edge switches pick their aggregation index uniformly and indepen-dently, a constant fraction of these downlinks receive at least two sticky flows (Step 3), which yields q(m) = Θ(m) (Step 4). Finally, we show that the uplink queues stay bounded (Step 5). For simplicity, the proof assumes that each flow starts with a random jitter uniformly distributed over a packet sending time.

# F Proofs and Details of O FAN ’s Properties F.1 Correctness of Consolidation 

Notation. Throughout this appendix, H denotes a host, E an edge switch, A an aggregation switch and C a core switch, with subscript S for the source side and D for the destination side. Thus ES and AS are a source edge switch and a source aggregation switch, and HD, ED and AD are a destination host, its edge switch, and an aggregation switch of its pod. A downlink is a southbound link, i.e., C → AD, AD → ED or 

ED → HD.

Load-balancing invariant. We start by formally formulating the goal of a switch DR in general (and O FAN in particular) in a network without link failures. It is clear that we want a perfect LB locally among uplink ports. But we also want to make sure that any source edge switch ES load-balances traffic as equally as possible across the links in the destination pods. We want to formulate a target ideal invariant that this load balancing should satisfy. However, we need to be careful in the formulation. The traffic that ES load-balances depends on the destinations of the flows that cross ES. These destinations depend on the arbitrary permutation pattern, and are therefore not uniform. For example, ES cannot send the same amount of traffic to all edge switches in the network. Instead, we condition the invariant on the destination switch. 

Invariant 1 (Load-balancing invariant). Given any source switch X in a source pod and any destination switch Y in a destination pod, X should send traffic at the same rate through all of the southbound links to Y that are reachable from X. 

The invariant implies a perfect load balancing at downlinks: 

Property 1. If Inv. 1 holds for source aggregation switches (X) and destination aggregation switches (Y ), and likewise 

22 for source edge switches and destination edge switches, then all southbound links to any switch have equal load. Proof. Partition the traffic on the downlinks into a switch by its source at the layer above: the load on each core-to-AD link is the sum of the rates of the source aggregation switches that reach it, and the load on each AD-to-ED link is the sum of the rates of the source edge switches that reach it. If each source sends at the same rate on all the links it reaches, these sums are equal for all the downlinks into a given switch. 

Consolidation. To achieve the above target invariant, we use 

consolidation into virtual flows . The key idea is that each source switch consolidates into a single shared virtual flow all of its northbound packets destined to a shared destination 

Y . Then, it load balances these packets across all of its uplink ports. However, we still need to carefully define the level at which to consolidate flows. 

Mandatory waypoints. Consider a source aggregation switch 

AS. A destination host HD that is reachable through its uplinks is necessarily in a different pod. Moreover, a property of the three-level fat tree is that when sending traffic from AS to HD,any packet must reach a given destination edge ED and a given destination aggregation switch AD in the destination pod, and cannot reach other edge or aggregation switches. For example, the third aggregation switch of the source pod can only send traffic destined to HD through the third aggregation switch AD

of the destination pod, then through the edge switch ED that is connected to HD. We can think of AD and ED as mandatory waypoints . They stand in contrast with the many possible core switch waypoints that could be used to reach HD.As a consequence, all of the traffic going through AS and destined to HD could be consolidated into a single virtual flow using several consolidation levels: (i) per destination host HD, (ii) per destination edge ED, and (iii) per destination aggregation AD. The following result shows that they can all provide a first performance guarantee. 

Theorem 8. Source aggregation switch A S satisfies Inv. 1 whether it consolidates traffic per destination host, edge or aggregation switch. Proof. Whatever the consolidation level, for any destination belonging to that level in the destination pod, AS always sends traffic at the same rate to all the cores it is connected to. Thus 

AD, the aggregation switch of the same index in the destina-tion pod, which is connected to those same cores, also receives the same rate from each of them, and therefore satisfies the invariant. In addition, the only downlinks AS can reach be-yond AD are AD → ED and ED → HD, and it can reach no other downlink into ED or into HD. Being the only ones, they trivially satisfy the invariant. Consider now a source edge switch ES. Its mandatory way-points for a given destination host HD are only HD and ED.Since it can reach HD using several aggregation switches, none is mandatory. Thus, ES can consolidate traffic (1) per destination host HD and (2) per destination edge ED. We find that both work out: 

Theorem 9. No matter the consolidation level, if both the source edge switch E S and the source aggregation switch A S

consolidate traffic, then A S satisfies Inv. 1 for the downlinks into destination aggregation switches, and E S for the down-links into destination edge switches and hosts. Hence all the downlinks into any switch have equal load. Proof. We structure the proof based on the destination type. (1) For any destination aggregation switch AD, the source edge switch ES has a single source aggregation waypoint 

AS: if the destination is in the same pod then AS = AD, and otherwise AS is the aggregation switch of the same index in the source pod. All of ES’s traffic for AD is therefore sent to 

AS. Since AS shares its pointer with the other edge switches of its pod, ES’s own share need not be spread evenly over the cores; but the aggregate that AS sends satisfies the invariant for AD by Thm. 8, which is all that Property 1 needs for the core-to-AD links. (2) For any destination edge switch ED, the source edge switch ES splits its traffic for ED equally across all the aggre-gation switches of its own pod, sending the same rate R to each. In turn, the rate leaving any source aggregation switch 

AS is carried to the aggregation switch AD of the same index in the destination pod (with AD = AS when the two pods co-incide). Hence ED receives the same rate R on each of its downlinks from the aggregation switches of its pod. (3) Finally, destination host HD is only connected through a single link to the network, so the invariant holds trivially. Since it is clear that pointers also load-balance perfectly locally among uplink ports, the above theorem means that LB achieves its dual goals both at uplinks and at downlinks. 

# F.2 Low-Cost Per-Destination Port Orders 

Requirements. Ofan keeps, at each switch, one round-robin pointer per destination (per destination edge switch at an edge switch, and per destination pod at an aggregation switch) and per packet type (e.g., data or ACK). Each pointer is an index 

i ∈ { 0, . . . , w − 1} into an order of the switch’s w uplinks, starting at a random position. Two requirements drive the choice of order. First, starting from any position, a pointer must visit every uplink exactly once per w packets, so traffic is split evenly. Second, pointers that are active at the same time at the same switch should follow different orders; otherwise they tend to select the same uplink at the same time. Storing an independent random permutation per destination meets both requirements, but costs Θ(w log w) bits per destination. 

OFAN operation. Instead, O FAN computes the order from a single global permutation P of {0, . . . , w − 1}, shared by all 23 switches, and a per-pointer identifier 

id = d + Nd · t, (16) where d is the destination (edge switch or pod), Nd is the number of possible destinations, and t ∈ { 0, 1} distinguishes data from acknowledgments. We split this identifier into its low-order and high-order parts, 

id 1 = id mod 2 b, id 2 = id /2b, (17) where b = ⌈log 2 w⌉. The i-th port of the order is then

Correctness. Each step maps {0, . . . , w − 1} onto itself one-to-one. Adding a constant modulo w clearly does. For xor w,since XOR is its own inverse, if v is kept because v ⊕ x ≥ w,no other value u < w can satisfy u ⊕ x = v, since that would require u = v ⊕ x ≥ w. Hence π is a permutation, and every pointer visits each uplink exactly once per w packets from any starting position. Under link failures, ports that no longer lead to the destination are skipped, so each remaining valid port still receives exactly one packet per round. 

Diversity from two non-collapsing operations. Using a sin-gle kind of operation yields few orders: all additions combine into one shift, giving at most w orders, and all XORs combine into one XOR, giving at most 2 b. XOR and addition, however, do not reduce to one another: addition preserves arithmetic differences and breaks bitwise ones through carries, while XOR does the opposite. Driving the XOR with id 1 (the low-order bits of id ) and the addition with id 2 (its high-order bits) therefore produces up to 2 b · w combinations. For w = 6 they yield 30 distinct cyclic orders, versus 6 with additions alone, so the few destinations served concurrently by a switch almost always follow different orders. 

Computational cost. The per-packet work is one lookup of 

P[i], one XOR, one comparison, one addition, and one con-ditional subtraction, all on ⌈log 2 w⌉-bit values. The constants 

id 1 and id 2 mod w can be derived from the destination on the fly. 

Storage cost. Beyond the pointers themselves, a switch stores nothing per destination. P is a single global constant of 

w⌈log 2 w⌉ bits that can be hard-wired and is the same for all switches. P is drawn once offline and chosen so that its w

cyclic shifts define w distinct orders.

# I OFAN ’s Hardware Implementation 

Concurrent access challenge. Implementing O FAN ’s per-destination-group pointers at line rate raises a structural chal-lenge: a switch processes many packets per cycle in paral-lel, arriving from different input ports, and several of these packets can be destined to the same destination edge or ag-gregation switch within the same cycle. Such packets must all access the same OFAN round-robin (RR) pointer, creating a read-modify-write hazard on a single shared register. 

Combining network. This concurrent-access problem is not new: it is a classic problem in parallel computing, where many concurrent processors issuing fetch-and-add requests to a shared counter can create the same kind of contention [46,50]. The classical fix, called a combining network , is to merge duplicate concurrent requests together before they reach the shared counter, so the counter is only touched once no matter how many requests asked for it. That said, these classical papers solve a more general prob-lem, where requests to a huge, dynamically-addressed mem-ory are routed hop-by-hop through a large network (e.g., an omega or butterfly network) and only combine when they hap-pen to collide en route. This general machinery is costly and has variable latency, which does not fit a switch pipeline that needs guaranteed, fixed per-cycle latency. Instead, we want to solve our narrower problem, over a small fixed set of N local pointers, using a much cheaper, fixed-latency, purpose-built circuit, described next. 

OFAN pipeline. We can implement O FAN ’s pointers with a simple, fixed-latency pipeline that processes a batch of w

packets in parallel, where w = k/2 is the number of downlink ports (only packets arriving from downlinks need a pointer decision, since uplink paths are chosen while downlink paths are unique). The pipeline has four stages: (1) sort the batch by pointer key, so packets sharing a pointer become adjacent; (2) combine the counts of packets sharing each pointer key; (3) update the shared pointer register for each key; and (4) decombine the updated pointer values back to each packet in the batch. We illustrate the pipeline in our fat-tree example of §4 with k = 64-port switches, i.e., at most w = k/2 = 32 packets arriving in parallel from downstream inputs, 32 uplink ports to cycle through, and N = 2K edge switch destinations. 

(1) Sort. First, a w-input bitonic sorting network [17] sorts the batch by pointer key, so packets sharing a pointer become adjacent. 

(2) Combine. A combining tree [25] computes, in log 2 (w)

stages, how many packets in the batch share each key; the last packet of each group becomes its master , carrying the group’s match count. 

(3) Update. A register file of N per-pointer counters is updated: each master atomically fetches its pointer’s current value and advances it, modulo the number of uplink ports, by the group’s match count. 

(4) Decombine. Finally, a reverse network decombines the fetched value back to every packet in its group (offsetting it by the packet’s position within the group), and an unsorting network—the inverse of the initial permutation—restores the original per-lane order, so each packet leaves the pipeline car-rying its own assigned uplink port. For our running example, this requires only a 32-input sorting/combining tree and 2 K

registers per edge switch, a practical hardware budget. 

Example. Suppose 3 of the w packets in a batch are all des-tined to the same edge switch, which has k/2 = 32 uplink ports, so its pointer takes values in {0, 1, . . . , 31 } and cur-rently holds the value 1. In stage (1), sorting by pointer key brings these 3 packets together, adjacent to each other. In stage (2), the combining tree counts the 3 matches and marks the last of them as the master , carrying a match count of 3. In stage (3), this master atomically fetches the pointer’s current value (1) and advances it by 3 mod 32, leaving the pointer at (1 + 3) mod 32 = 4 for the next batch. In stage (4), the 3 packets are decombined: each is assigned the fetched value offset by its position within the group, i.e., ports 1, 2, and 3 respectively, and the unsorting network routes each result back to the packet’s original lane. Thus, the pointer is touched exactly once for all 3 packets, yet each packet still receives its own distinct, correctly-ordered round-robin port, exactly as if the 3 requests had been processed one at a time. 

Cost estimate. The pipeline requires N registers for the point-ers, plus a w-input sorting network, a w-input combining tree, and a matching decombining/unsorting network of the same size. For our running example, recall each edge switch keeps one pointer per (destination edge switch, packet-size quan-tization group) pair; with 2 K destination edge switches and 2 quantization groups (data, ACKs), N ≈ 4K. Each pointer needs log 2(32 ) = 5 bits to represent its value (one of the 32 26 uplink ports), so the register file is only N × 5 ≈ 20 K bits (≈ 2.5KB). The rest of the pipeline is dominated by two con-tributions: (i) the W =32-input sorting and unsorting networks, each with 15 stages of 16 compare-and-swap cells (roughly 80 gates per cell, for a 12-bit pointer-key comparator, wide enough to address N ≈ 4K keys, plus payload-swap muxes), for ≈ 20 K gates apiece; and (ii) the register file’s read-modify-write logic, dominated by the 20 K flip-flops themselves at ≈ 4gate-equivalents each, i.e., ≈ 82 K gates. The combining tree adds only a couple thousand more gates ( ≈ 80 small adder nodes for the segmented match counts). Altogether, this is on the order of 10 5 logic gates ( ≈ 127 K). At a 1 GHz clock and a conservative 10% switching activity, this amounts to only a few milliwatts of dynamic power ( ≈ 6mW). Both figures are negligible next to the resources of the very chip we used as our pipe-architecture example: the BCM56980 (Tomahawk 3) datasheet lists a typical power draw of 300 W and 64 MB of integrated packet-buffer mem-ory [13], independently corroborated by [83]. So O FAN ’s pipeline’s ≈ 6mW would add only ≈ 0.002% to such a chip’s power budget, and its ≈ 20 K-bit register file is only 

≈ 0.0037% of that chip’s 64MB ( ≈ 537 million bits) of buffer memory—both entirely negligible.

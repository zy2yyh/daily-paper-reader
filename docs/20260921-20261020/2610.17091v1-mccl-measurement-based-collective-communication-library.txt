# MCCL: Measurement-based Collective Communication Library

MASSIMO GALLO, Independent Researcher, France Collective communication is a critical performance bottleneck in large-scale distributed training. Existing libraries and synthesis frameworks compute open-loop schedules once, assuming a static topology and peak link capacities. In production, however, persistent gray failures can silently degrade link throughput and latency for hours, causing pre-computed schedules to lose much of their efficiency during execution. In this paper, we present Measurement-based Collective Communication Library (MCCL), a collective communication library that closes the loop between runtime network measurement and dynamic schedule synthesis. MCCL passively measures GPU-to-GPU throughput, detects persistent degradation, re-synthesizes the affected communication-intensive schedules with warm-started synthesizers, and hot-swaps them without interrupting training. We validate the complete loop on a multi-node hardware prototype and use simulations to evaluate cluster-scale scenarios. In our experiments, MCCL incurs negligible overhead ( < 2%) while maintaining high measurement accuracy ( < 1.2% error) and low schedule hot-swap time ( ≈ 12 ms). Notably, under gray failures, MCCL reduces training time by up to 4.99 × over static schedules on simulated training workloads for state-of-the-art LLMs such as Llama and DeepSeek. distributed training increasingly depends on the performance of collective communications. Primi-tives such as AllGather, AllToAll, AllReduce, and ReduceScatter directly influence training time, often accounting for a substantial fraction of the overall iteration latency. Consequently, significant research has focused on optimizing these primitives, with efforts largely concentrated on communication-computation overlap, reduction algorithms, and static topology-aware scheduling. Hardware accelerator vendors provide Collective Communication Libraries (CCLs) such as Nvidia CCL (NCCL) [26], AMD ROCm CCL (RCCL) [35], Huawei CCL (HCCL) [15], and Intel OneCCL [31], with highly optimized collective communication schedules tailored to common cluster topologies and hardware configurations. These vendor libraries increasingly leverage specialized hardware, such as in-network computing [39], to accelerate Reduce-intensive collectives. In parallel, schedule synthesizers [3, 5, 23, 34, 36, 43, 48] have demonstrated substantial speedups on communication-intensive primitives such as AllGather and AllToAll. However, existing synthesis tools share a critical limitation: they operate in an open-loop , relying on static topology assumptions and peak link capacities that rarely hold in production environments. Datacenter networks typically exhibit complex and dynamic behavior. A critical yet pervasive challenge is the prevalence of gray failures [14, 17], where components remain functionally opera-tional but experience severe performance degradation due to thermal throttling, PCIe or Network Interface Card (NIC) symbol errors, and optical link decay. Unlike hard failures , which typically trig-ger alarms, gray failures can silently reduce link performance, sometimes substantially, for several minutes or even hours [14, 46]. Consequently, optimal collective communication schedules at the beginning of training can become increasingly inefficient as system conditions evolve, significantly slowing training processes and leading to suboptimal resource utilization across the distributed infrastructure. This mismatch is particularly pronounced in iterative training because the exact same collectives repeat for thousands of iterations; any localized performance degradation becomes persistent and cumulative. Our key observation is that the repetitive nature of distributed training turns every collective into a free measurement opportunity. Because identical GPU-to-GPU transfers repeat across thousands of iterations, their progress can be passively monitored at path granularity, without injecting any traffic. This insight enables a fundamentally different operating model for collective communication. Instead of synthesizing a schedule once under static topology assumptions and peak capacities (open-loop ), the library can continuously measure the paths it uses, decide when the deployed schedule diverges from actual network conditions, re-synthesize it for observed capacities, and 

deploy the new schedule dynamically during training ( closed-loop ). Realizing this closed-loop architecture introduces three fundamental challenges. First, the system requires path-level visibility without perturbing training. Existing monitoring approaches either rely on synthetic probes, which compete with training traffic, or on aggregate collective completion time, which can detect slowdown but cannot localize the degraded paths. Second, to avoid triggering spurious re-synthesis cycles, the system must reliably distinguish persistent degradations from short-lived fluctuations and pinpoint the affected collectives. Finally, re-synthesis time is the primary barrier to rapid runtime adaptation. Tree-packing synthesizers such as ForestColl [48] can quickly generate AllGather and ReduceScatter schedules, yet they lack support for warm-starting; even after an isolated, single-link degradation, they discard structurally valid subtrees and recompute schedules from scratch. The barrier is even higher for AllToAll: existing synthesizers [23] solve heavyweight Linear Program (LP) formulations that take hours to converge, too slow for online adaptation. While recent approaches accelerate search times by exploiting network symmetry [5, 37], gray failures inherently break topological symmetry, invalidating their core performance assumptions.

In this paper, we present Measurement-based Collective Communication Library (MCCL) 1,a collective communication library that integrates a closed optimization loop directly into the communication stack. MCCL employs a controller-executor architecture: distributed executors run collectives and passively collect GPU-to-GPU telemetry on the fast path, while a centralized controller detects persistent performance drift, selectively re-synthesizes the affected collectives, and deploys the resulting schedules through a hot-swap mechanism adapted from MCCS [47]. Each new component of MCCL is designed around the requirements of the loop rather than as a standalone optimization. MCCL targets communication-intensive collectives, namely AllGather and AllToAll. Reduction-centric collectives such as ReduceScatter keep their existing highly optimized implementations, avoiding interference with in-network computing. AllReduce remains compatible with MCCL through its standard decomposition into ReduceScatter and AllGather, optimizing its AllGather phase while leaving the reduction phase unchanged. In summary, this work makes the following contributions: 

• We design and implement MCCL, the first collective communication library that closes the loop between path-level measurements and schedule synthesis, continuously adapting communication-intensive schedules to live network conditions without interrupting ongoing training processes (Section 3). 

• To fill the gap of existing synthesizers, we present a warm-started version of ForestColl that reuses the structure of deployed AllGather schedules, and a column-generation formulation for AllToAll whose rounding gap stays within 2.13 % across tested topologies (Section 4). 

• To enable reliable path-level visibility, we design a passive telemetry framework that captures GPU-to-GPU bandwidth metrics at the thread-block level, with < 2 % overhead and < 1.2 %measurement error (Section 5). 

• We validate the complete loop (telemetry, failure detection, re-optimization, and hot-swapping) on an 8-GPU, 2-node prototype, where a schedule swap completes in ≈ 12 ms without im-pacting training-related collective communication scheduling (Section 6). 

• We evaluate MCCL at cluster-scale simulating state-of-the-art LLM trainings, demonstrating up to 4.99 × faster training under gray failures compared to static schedules (Section 6). The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We provide background in Section 2, Sections 3–5 detail the system architecture, optimization formulation, and measurement framework. Finally, we evaluate MCCL in Section 6, discuss related work in Section 7, and conclude in Section 8. 

2 Background and motivation 2.1 Collective Communication Synthesis 

The collective communication research landscape [45] is rapidly evolving, with hand-tuned vendor libraries increasingly complemented by automated schedule-synthesis frameworks. Libraries pro-vided by hardware accelerator vendors, like NCCL [26], RCCL [35], HCCL [15], and OneCCL [31], rely on static, heuristic algorithms (e.g., Ring, Tree) mapped to specific hardware topologies. While these libraries execute reduction-heavy collectives (e.g., AllReduce) with high efficiency by leveraging specialized hardware such as SHARP in-network computing [39], the benefits of such accelerators are largely confined to collectives that involve reduction operations. Communication-intensive collectives, such as AllGather and AllToAll, remain primarily constrained by data movement and often cannot fully exploit heterogeneous or non-standard interconnects. Optimizing these collectives is therefore critical to reducing communication overhead and, ultimately, train-ing time. To enable this flexibility, platforms such as MSCCL [25] were developed, enabling the execution of custom collective algorithms on top of standard libraries such as NCCL.

Building on this foundation, several collective communication synthesizers have emerged to automate schedule generation. Early approaches such as SCCL [3] use Satisfiability Modulo The-ories (SMT) solvers to find Pareto-optimal algorithms, while TACCL [36] uses user-provided “communication sketches” to guide synthesis. Subsequent frameworks adopted traffic-engineering principles: TECCL [23] formulates AllGather as an 𝐴 ∗-scaled Mixed Integer Linear Program (MILP) and AllToAll as an LP, while ForestColl [48] constructs spanning tree packs using strongly polynomial-time algorithms to generate throughput-optimal schedules. More recently, frameworks have focused on scaling these optimizations for massive topologies. Concurrent work OptCCL [37] and SyCCL [5] both leverage network and collective symmetry to decompose the scheduling problem, with OptCCL explicitly separating routing from chunk scheduling via a multi-commodity flow LP. However, a critical common denominator across these approaches (cf. Table 1) is the “static world” assumption: they optimize for a topology snapshot taken at initialization time that is assumed to remain constant throughout training. 

2.2 Challenges: Scale and Reliability 

Training foundation models like LLama3 [1], Gemini 2.5 [12], DeepSeek [21] involves big clusters with over 10k GPUs/TPUs running continuously for weeks or months. Furthermore, multiple major AI labs, including OpenAI, Microsoft, xAI, and Meta, are racing to deploy individual training clusters exceeding 100k accelerators, pushing datacenters scale to unprecedented levels. At this scale, network-level component failures (e.g., links, NICs, and optical transceivers), which might be considered rare exceptions in isolation, become a near certainty and pose a critical operational challenge [33]. For instance, estimates suggest that even if each NIC-to-switch link had a mean time to failure of 5 years, the first training job failure would likely occur in less than half an hour within a 100k GPU cluster [10]. Such high hardware failure rates are particularly problematic in distributed training, where a single component failure can cause the entire training job to halt, leaving expensive accelerators idle. While catastrophic fail-stop events are relatively easy to detect, a more challenging issue is the presence of gray failures (or fail-slow hardware) [14, 17], where components remain functional but operate at degraded performance levels due to specific recovery mechanisms [2, 32, 42]. These performance degradations are rarely brief anomalies; empirical evidence shows that congestion-induced gray failures persist for an average of 72 minutes (e.g., Greyhound [46]), while hardware-related faults can last significantly longer (e.g., FailSlow [14]). Because these degraded states persist across thousands of training iterations, they become severe long-term bottlenecks. 

2.3 The case for dynamic CCL schedules 

Given these reliability challenges, we argue for a shift from static planning to dynamic adaptation, as pre-optimized schedules become highly inefficient when communication performance drifts.

2.4 Limitations of Current Approaches 

Realizing the theoretical gains of dynamic scheduling requires accurate, real-time detection of performance drifts. Static collective schedule synthesizers rely on active probing i.e., injecting dummy traffic, which is effective offline but slows live training by consuming valuable network resources. Furthermore, relying solely on bandwidth measurements taken from network links or switches is insufficient. While these metrics capture the state of the network fabric, they fail to account for the end-to-end complexity of the communication path. Indeed, traditional network-level measurements [9, 24, 46] overlook critical intra-node factors, such as PCIe bottlenecks and protocol overheads. Consequently, MCCL must explicitly monitor the full GPU-to-GPU data paths. While recent frameworks like MCCS [47] attempt runtime adaptability, their scope is limited to re-ordering ranks within a fixed ring algorithm; they lack the mechanisms to pinpoint path-specific degradations or the capability to synthesize new schedules online. Moreover, accurate detection alone is not sufficient to enable dynamic schedule adaptation, as existing synthesizers fundamentally lack runtime architectural support. While synthesis tools like ForestColl [48] are faster than traditional solver-based ones, they still operate entirely statelessly. When a localized gray failure occurs, they do not preserve unaffected portions of a previously optimal schedule. Instead, they discard the entire communication structure and rebuild from scratch, forcing redundant exploration and unnecessarily delaying runtime adaptation. For complex patterns like AllToAll, where every node sends unique data to every other node, the optimization challenge becomes intractable for time-discretized flow-based methods. While recent approaches like SyCCL [5] attempt to mitigate this scaling issue by exploiting topology symmetry, they are rendered ineffective by gray failures, which inherently break symmetry and create irregular network states. Concurrent work OptCCL [37] faces the same limitation: although its LP is polynomial, its scalability relies on structural symmetry [38, Appendix A.11]. Consequently, finding an optimal, asymmetry-aware AllToAll schedule for a large cluster remains computationally prohibitive for online adaptation.

3 System Design 

The key idea of MCCL is to shift from a static “optimize-once” model to a dynamic “always-optimizing” framework. To achieve this without delaying the active training job, MCCL utilizes an asynchronous controller-executor architecture, depicted in Figure 2. The Controller runs as a background service (e.g., on a dedicated CPU or separate node) and manages the slow-path :processing telemetry, diagnosing network health, and executing the schedule synthesis algorithms. The Executor runs on the GPUs participating in distributed training and manages the fast-path :executing the collectives, passively measuring network throughput, and hot-swapping schedules. By design MCCL targets communication-intensive operations (AllGather, AllToAll). However, distributed training paradigms such as Fully Sharded Data Parallelism (FSDP) rely on operations like AllReduce. MCCL natively supports these workloads through collective decomposition. When a training job triggers an AllReduce, MCCL splits it into its constituent phases. The initial arithmetic-intensive ReduceScatter phase is executed using static schedules to leverage specialized in-network aggregation hardware [39]. The subsequent data-intensive AllGather phase is routed through MCCL’s Executor for dynamic optimization. This split preserves hardware-accelerated reduction performance while adapting the data-transfer phase to network conditions. 

3.1 The Dynamic Optimization Loop 

MCCL operates as a continuous feedback loop (Figure 2), with a startup phase for baseline optimiza-tion and a runtime phase for dynamic adaptation. At startup 0 ○, a profile of the training’s collective calls (e.g., list and frequency) and an initial overlay topology are fed to the Schedule Manager . Such topology models communication as overlay links: each GPU connects to an intra-node switch (e.g., NVSwitch), and GPUs in different nodes are connected by direct inter-node links. Initial link capacities are obtained via probing, as for other static synthesizers [6, 23, 36, 48]. In this phase, without online measurements, the Schedule Manager 1○ acts as a static orchestrator, passing the topology to the Schedule Optimizer to generate a set of optimized baseline schedules. These are sent to the Executor’s Online Rescheduler 2○ to ensure that training starts with high-performance primitives. Optionally, MCCL can bypass this step and fall back to standard NCCL rings, deferring all optimization to the runtime phase. Once training begins, the system enters the runtime phase and transitions to its dynamic loop. While the optimized schedules are executed, the Executor’s Measure-ment module passively monitors overlay links throughput 3 ○. This real-time data is continuously fed back to the Controller, fundamentally changing the Schedule Manager’s role.

3.2 Controller Components 

The Controller operates on the “slow-path” and is composed of two modules: 

Schedule Manager. This component acts as the “brain” of the MCCL framework; its primary role is to select which collective(s) to re-optimize based on network conditions. It takes three inputs: the application profile with its graph 𝐺 (𝑉 , 𝐴 ) (step 0 ○), the set of collectives S with the details of their active execution schedules, and throughput measurements M (step 3 ○). Both Γ and the EWMA 𝛼 are tuned to the cluster profile: a small 𝛼 filters out longer bursts at the cost of slower detection, while a large Γ ignores milder degradations. Both reduce the number of re-optimizations and thus the risk of schedule thrashing , i.e., swapping schedules when degradations clear faster than a synthesis cycle. If a degradation clears during re-synthesis, the Schedule Manager aborts it and keeps the current schedule; if it clears right after, the installed schedule may be suboptimal until the next re-optimization. In practice, thrashing is unlikely, as gray failures typically persist for minutes to hours (Section 2.2), far longer than a re-synthesis cycle. Additional thrashing prevention mechanisms and Γ and 𝛼 tuning from the observed failure dynamics are left for future work. 

Schedule Optimizer. The optimizer serves as a unified wrapper that delegates collective opti-mization to specialized synthesizers. To ensure broad applicability while maintaining tractability, it targets communication-intensive primitives from which other collectives are derived: AllToAll and AllGather. For AllToAll, where asymmetric gray failures render traditional symmetry-based synthesizers ineffective, the optimizer invokes a Column Generation (CG)-based synthesizer. This engine enables fast re-synthesis via warm starts from previous solutions, supports time-bounded execution, and optimizes throughput against network irregularities. For AllGather, it interfaces with an extended ForestColl tree-packing synthesizer [48], which integrates an 𝐴 ∗-guided search to warm-start re-synthesis, preserve previous schedule structures, and minimize recomputation overhead. 3.3 Executor Components 

The Executor handles “fast-path” operations on the GPUs. Built on top of the MSCCL [6, 25] backend to inject custom schedules, it consists of two elements: 

Online Rescheduler. This module is responsible for the dynamic deployment of new schedules leveraging a runtime “hot-swap” mechanism adapted from MCCS [47]. When the Controller provides a new Optimized Schedule (step 2 ○), the Online Rescheduler stages it. At that point, GPUs belonging to the target communicator execute an out-of-band AllGather to exchange two state variables: the version number of their most recently staged schedule, and the sequence number of their last-launched collective (or collective group). Here, the minimum version number identifies the newest schedule staged across all GPUs, while the maximum sequence number defines the collective selected for the transition. Collectives with previous sequence numbers complete on the old schedule; subsequent ones complete using the re-optimized schedule. Because each swap is scoped to a single communicator, collectives on other communicators are unaffected. 

Measurement. This module provides real-time telemetry feeding the optimization loop (step 3 ○). To ensure that measurements capture only actual network communications, telemetry collection is restricted to operations without reduction phases, isolating link throughput from the compu-tational phases of reduction arithmetic. To capture the distinct performance characteristics of different interconnects, the measurement module employs a hybrid metric strategy. For intra-node communication, it measures the cumulative per-GPU throughput, aggregating bandwidth across all concurrent transfers thus capturing the total device injection capacity. For inter-node commu-nication, it captures point-to-point throughput between GPU pairs. These metrics are collected passively during training and asynchronously flushed to the Controller with RDMA 3, which records them in a raw measurement matrix later used to derive M (Appendix B, Figure 13 for reference). The design of this passive measurement system is detailed in Section 5. 

4 Collectives Optimization 4.1 Overview 

Optimizing collective communication centers on scheduling data-block (i.e., tensor) transfers to minimize completion time. For MCCL, this requires balancing theoretical throughput optimality with the computational scalability required for online deployment. Existing synthesis tools fail to reconcile these conflicting goals because they cannot efficiently manage the optimization state during dynamic network performance drifts. Approaches like TECCL [23] optimize throughput-latency trade-offs but rely on computationally heavy formulations, such as time-expanded graphs or rigid MILP. These methods scale poorly with cluster size and may require substantial solving times, rendering them impractical for online schedule re-computation. Conversely, synthesizers like ForestColl [48] are significantly faster at generating throughput-optimal schedules, but they operate statelessly. When a localized topology change occurs, these algorithms discard the entire communication structure and rebuild the schedule from scratch (i.e., cold start) wasting critical computational cycles and delaying runtime adaptation. The core principle underlying MCCL’s optimization framework is that network anomalies typically disrupt only a localized fraction of the topology. Rather than re-evaluating the entire search space, MCCL utilizes the previously optimal solution as a structural heuristic to constrain the new search. The shift from a naive cold-start to an efficient warm-start paradigm drastically reduces redundant exploration. As our evaluations demonstrate, using historical schedules to anchor the search reduces convergence times, rendering real-time schedule adaptation practical.

To achieve this, the Schedule Optimizer integrates two specialized synthesis engines tailored to the communication structures of AllGather and AllToAll: 

AllToAll via Column Generation (CG). We propose an LP-throughput-optimal, decomposition-based formulation for AllToAll collectives that eliminates solving time overhead associated with traditional time-expanded graphs. By decoupling the problem into a Restricted Master Problem (RMP) and a pricing subproblem, we avoid enumerating the exponentially many tree variables while preserving LP-optimality. Building on this scalable foundation, we also introduce a warm-start mechanism that retains valid routing structures from previous iterations, drastically reducing the time required to adapt to network changes. 

AllGather via ForestColl-warm. We introduce ForestColl-warm, which extends the throughput-optimal tree-packing algorithm by incorporating an 𝐴 ∗-guided exploration strategy tailored for online re-computation. By leveraging the solution history to direct the underlying Breadth-First Search (BFS), this mechanism strategically reorders the sequence of candidate links to align with the previous optimal arborescence. This guided approach aggressively prunes the exploration space, minimizing redundant computational overhead and enabling rapid adaptation to network changes. Additionally, in Appendix C we propose a ForestColl-like latency-sensitive formulation which reduces maximum tree depths with the objective of reducing schedules’ latency.

4.3 AllGather optimization 

In this section, we present our ForestColl extension, whose baseline algorithm comprises three phases that must be executed sequentially: 

Phase 1: Compute maximum throughput — This phase calculates the maximum flow for each tensor from a super-source 𝑆 to the source node of each tensor 𝑐 within the reversed graph. The overall maximum throughput is determined by the minimum of these computed flows. 

Phase 2: Switch removal — This phase abstracts switches by replacing physical paths with direct logical links. The procedure greedily assigns capacity to each logical link (Fig. 14, Appendix C). For each node, the total capacity assigned to incoming and outgoing logical links must not exceed the underlying physical link capacity and must satisfy the calculated maximum throughput. 

Phase 3: Tree generation — For every tensor in the logical topology, this procedure allocates link capacity based on the maximum throughput. The allocation uses a Breadth-First Search (BFS) strategy initiated at the tensor’s source node, which guarantees an arborescence structure. 

History-Guided Tree Generation (ForestColl-warm). To guarantee the rapid schedule re-synthesis required for online adaptation, we enhance the tree generation phase by making it history-aware. The original ForestColl algorithm employs a stateless BFS to construct the arborescence. This approach is computationally wasteful during a localized network anomaly, as it discards the entire communication structure and rebuilds from a cold start. To improve the re-optimization strategy, our enhanced BFS is guided by the previously computed arborescence through a mechanism analogous to the 𝐴 ∗ algorithm. The primary objective is to preserve its structure as much as possible. This approach aggressively reduces redundant explorations within the BFS by reordering the sequence of candidate links. Given that a gray failure typically impacts a limited number of links, there is a high probability that links from prior optimal trees remain valid in subsequent iterations, allowing the solver to rapidly converge on a new throughput-optimal schedule.

5 Measurements framework 

Before detailing our lightweight passive measurement architecture, we analyze the execution model of the underlying communication library. Without loss of generality, we focus on NCCL, though other software stacks for GPU-to-GPU communication share fundamentally similar architectures. As detailed in recent literature [16], NCCL parallelizes collective operations by splitting them into virtual pipelines known as channels . Each channel manages a fraction of the total payload via a producer-consumer model, transferring data from a source to a destination. At the hardware level, each channel is driven by a single Thread Block (TB) executing on a dedicated Streaming Multiprocessor (SM). For throughput-intensive protocols (e.g., Simple), NCCL partitions the payload across these channels. This enables fine-grained pipelining where computation, transmission, and synchronization occur concurrently. The underlying data movement relies on two distinct mechanisms: intra- and inter-node as depicted in Figure 3. For intra-node communication, NCCL utilizes the P2P_DIRECT transport. By mapping peer memory into a unified address space, TB issue direct Load/Store instructions to remote GPU memory, bypassing intermediate staging buffers. Inter-node communication instead relies on a Proxy-based protocol run by the Host CPU. The GPU posts work requests to a shared FIFO in host memory, which the Proxy polls to trigger network operations (e.g., RDMA or Socket transfers). With GPU-Direct, chunks are transferred immediately from the GPU to the NIC, bypassing the CPU for the payload. This decoupled, multichannel execution model presents unique challenges for telemetry: because traffic is split across independent hardware and channels, traditional single-point monitoring is insufficient. This motivates our specialized measurement architecture, which is designed to capture effective bandwidth natively across these distributed execution paths. Currently, our measurement framework targets collective operations without a reduction phase (e.g., AllGather, AllToAll) to ensure that the measured throughput is not affected by the computational latency.

5.2 Inter-node measurements 

For inter-node communication, we leverage the CPU Proxy Thread to capture performance metrics. Unlike the highly parallelized execution model of GPU TBs, the Proxy centralizes the management of network operations for a given connection. This significantly simplifies the measurement logic and effectively eliminates the need for atomic operations. Despite these architectural differences, we reuse the same Measurements Accumulator and Measurements Buffer data structures, as well as the same multi-channel measurement algorithm. The primary distinction is that, in practice, a single communication channel is often sufficient to saturate the available network bandwidth. The measurement logic is embedded within the Proxy’s Finite State Machine (FSM), which manages the life-cycle of network chunks in three stages: Posted –a transfer slot is available, 

Transmitted –the network operation, e.g., isend , has been triggered, and Done –transmission is confirmed as complete. To calculate the effective bandwidth, we isolate network injection and transit time by measuring the interval between Transmitted and Done states. To ensure accuracy, MCCL models this effective inter-node throughput as the minimum of the local buffer write rate and the network travel rate (i.e., local-to-remote buffer transfer), thereby accounting for potential bottlenecks across the entire data path, i.e., High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), PCIe bus, or network.

We evaluate a prototype implementation of MCCL that builds on MSCCL [25]. The prototype runs on two hosts, each featuring four Nvidia H200 GPUs connected in a rail-optimized topology with Nvidia ConnectX-7 NICs (200 Gbps) using GPU-Direct (details in Table 3).

Overhead. We evaluate throughput overhead by comparing MCCL direct point-to-point throughput with and without our module, varying the reduceCopy chunk size. 6.2.2 Hot-Swap Mechanism. We implement the hot-swap mechanism building on top of MSCCL for on-demand connection and dynamic algorithm loading [25]. When the controller issues a new schedule version, the system delegates the transition to a dedicated background thread. This is responsible for asynchronously loading the new algorithmic routing and establishing the necessary peer-to-peer connections without interrupting ongoing communications. If any, existing connections are reused, matching NCCL’s default behavior [29]. Once setup completes, the background thread briefly pauses the issuance of new collectives on the communicator and executes an out-of-band (OOB) AllGather, adapted from MCCS [47]. Each GPU reports the latest version among its successfully loaded schedules and the sequence number of its last-issued collective operation. The minimum schedule version ensures that the selected schedule is available on every GPU, while the maximum sequence number 𝐾 defines the schedule transition point: all operations up to 𝐾 use the old schedule, and subsequent collective operations use the new one. Since NCCL preserves operation order within a communicator and serializes their device execution [28, 30], the two schedules never execute concurrently, and no draining of in-flight operations is required. Swaps are scoped independently to each communicator and do not interfere with one another. Figure 10a illustrates the reaction of the system during an inter-node link failure, which halves the available bandwidth between GPU ranks 0 and 4 (the first GPU of each node) to 100 Gbps. We evaluate a 16 MB per-GPU AllGather, matching the per-GPU input tensor size of the dominant col-lective in Llama 405B. Under the stale schedule, the degradation reduces algorithm bandwidth [27] from 74 .02 GB /s to 44 .76 GB /s. The controller detects the failure in 53 .1 ms; this window is primarily spent gathering sufficient degraded telemetry samples to prevent false positives from transient anomalies (we use a conservative smoothing factor 𝛼 = 0.012). The controller then runs the syn-thesizer and dispatches the new schedule to the executors, whose Online Rescheduler activates it 24 .7 ms after detection. Meanwhile, collectives continue running on the stale schedule: all in-flight operations (here, a single AllGather) complete on it, while the next submitted collective switches to the new schedule, starting execution 0.4 ms later for a total reaction time of 78 .2 ms. Once executed, algorithm bandwidth recovers to 59 .72 GB /s without interrupting training execution. Figure 10b breaks down the adaptation time of 24 .7 ms: 13 .1 ms is spent in the synthesizer invoked by the Schedule Optimizer, while the hot-swap takes 11 .6 ms, mainly for OOB synchronization (5.74 ms) and algorithm loading ( 4.48 ms). 6.3 End-to-End Validation 

To validate the end-to-end benefits of our approach, we run large-scale simulations in SimAI. We use an HPN topology with segments of 128 GPUs each (16 hosts) and scale the cluster from 128 to 2048 GPUs; we apply the same three failure scenarios as in Section 6.1. Because detection parameters (Γ, 𝛼 ) are inherently cluster-specific, calibrating them to our simulation offers little generalizable insight. We therefore decouple condition detection from schedule adaptation, evaluating MCCL’s re-synthesis and failover in isolation under prompt failure notifications, in regimes where adaptation proceeds without schedule thrashing. As a baseline, we compare against plain NCCL. We exclude TECCL due to impractical runtime solving time, and ForestColl as it cannot optimize AllToAll collectives, while it delivers the same schedule quality as MCCL for AllGather collectives. We design two sets of workloads that mimic the training of Llama3.1 with 405B parameters and DeepSeek-v3 with 671B parameters and 256 experts [1, 21]. We configure a pipeline parallelism of 16 and only simulate one pipeline stage as traffic between stages is point-to-point. Therefore, our simulations with 128–2048 GPUs correspond to training the full model on 2048–32768 GPUs. We execute a forward and backward pass on a batch of 16K tokens. 7 Related Work 

Offline schedule synthesis. A large body of work synthesizes collective schedules offline. TACCL [36], TE-CCL [23], and ForestColl [48] build topology-aware schedules from commu-nication sketches, traffic-engineering formulations, and spanning trees, respectively, outperforming vendor-provided collectives. However, they rely on static, offline profiling of the topology at peak capacity and cannot promptly react to link degradation, multi-tenant interference, or gray failures. The concurrent work OptCCL [37] synthesizes provably optimal schedules from a multi-commodity flow LP solved via Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition, with an AllToAll formulation close to ours (Section 4.2). OptCCL scales the decomposition by exploiting topology symmetry, which gray failures inherently break, forcing a fallback to a cold start. MCCL instead exploits the locality of re-optimization, reducing re-synthesis time without relying on symmetry. The two approaches are complementary; moreover, OptCCL neither detects degradations nor redeploys schedules at runtime. 

Runtime adaptation. A second line of work adapts at runtime, but along axes orthogonal to ours. FAST [20] schedules MoE’s skewed, dynamic AllToAllv online, rebalancing over the fast intra-server fabric so that every NIC sends equal volume across the slow inter-server links. Its adaptation tracks workload skew, not network state: it models the cluster as two uniform, equal-capacity bandwidth tiers, so its balancing breaks under link degradation, asymmetric capacity, or gray failures. At the inter-job level, Crux [4] and MCCS [47] arbitrate the network shared by co-located jobs: Crux prioritizes flows by GPU intensity (compute-to-communication ratio), routing GPU-heavy jobs away from contended links, while MCCS, a provider-managed service, reorders ring ranks and pins flows to paths to preserve fairness and reduce congestion. Both re-optimize only at job arrival/exit or on external triggers, leaving collective schedules unchanged and thus exposed to gray failures. The concurrent work Theseus [8] hot-swaps schedules at runtime according to user policies, but neither synthesizes schedules nor measures the network: it relies on slow MILP-based synthesizers or precomputed candidates, and on NIC statistics that miss intra-host degradations (e.g., NVLink, PCIe). MCCL instead measures each GPU-to-GPU path and re-synthesizes within tens of seconds, only when the bottleneck cut loses saturation. 

Execution backends. A final line of work provides the execution backends. NCCL and RCCL ship static, built-in algorithms, while MSCCL [6], MSCCL++ [19], and ResCCL [22] add DSL-defined schedules, portable low-level primitives, and resource-efficient thread-block scheduling, respectively. They improve expressiveness, portability, and efficiency, but still execute static, offline-generated schedules and cannot adapt to link variability, congestion, or gray failures. 

8 Conclusion 

This paper introduced a Measurement-based Collective Communication Library (MCCL) that shifts the paradigm from “optimize-once” to “continuously-adaptive”. By leveraging a controller-executor architecture, MCCL integrates lightweight, passive, high-fidelity GPU telemetry directly into the optimization loop without disrupting the training workload. Our evaluation demonstrates that MCCL re-optimization algorithms rapidly synthesize schedules adapted to detected bottlenecks, improving solving times by up to 2.80 × for AllGather and by up to 17 .8× for AllToAll compared to static synthesizers. The MCCL prototype demonstrates the feasibility of the complete closed loop, with low measurement overhead ( < 2 %), high accuracy ( < 1.2 % error), and fast schedule swaps (≈ 12 ms). Finally, large-scale simulations of LLM training workloads show that MCCL reduces training time by up to 4.99 × under gray failures compared to static schedules. 

This work does not raise any ethical issues.

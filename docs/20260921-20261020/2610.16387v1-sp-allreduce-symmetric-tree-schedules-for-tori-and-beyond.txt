# SP-Allreduce: Symmetric Tree Schedules for Tori and Beyond 

Mikhail Cherniavskii, Andrey Kupavskii, Georgy Mironov, Stanislav Moiseev 

Abstract 

Allreduce is a fundamental communication primitive in large-scale distributed training, and torus interconnects remain widely deployed in high-performance systems. We introduce SP-allreduce, a new allreduce algorithm for d-dimensional tori that exploits the symmetry of the topology and routes only along shortest paths. We call an allreduce algorithm fully bandwidth-optimal if it incurs no bandwidth overhead, no multihop-induced traffic inflation, and achieves perfect per-round load balance. SP-allreduce is based on a family of carefully symmetrized shortest-path trees that guarantees full bandwidth optimality in the α–β model. We prove that it uses the minimum possible number of rounds among all fully bandwidth-optimal allreduce algorithms on d-dimensional tori, and therefore the minimum running time in this class. In this sense, SP-allreduce refines Bucket allreduce: it preserves Bucket’s key strengths while reducing the number of rounds by a factor of two. We further extend our approach to broader symmetric topologies and to communication trees of prescribed depths. Experimentally, the resulting algorithms improve over the best compared state-of-the-art algorithms for tori by up to 1 .19 × on square tori and 1 .65 × on rectangular tori across the tested configurations. On HyperX and spine-leaf networks, we demonstrate gains of up to 14 .82 × and 1 .72 ×, respectively, over the best evaluated baselines. 

# 1 Introduction 

The allreduce operation is a fundamental building block for both high-performance computing (HPC) and distributed deep learning. It is used to aggregate and redistribute model gradients and other global states, and is a major factor in the end-to-end runtime: prior work reports that collective operations, and allreduce in particular, may account for up to 40% of training time in large distributed ML workloads and a substantial fraction of core-hours in production HPC systems [6, 9, 12, 27, 32, 36]. Modern deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch expose allreduce as the main primitive for data-parallel training and heavily rely on optimized communication backends [28]. The importance of allreduce has motivated a long line of work on performance models, algorithmic variants, and implementations of allreduce and related collective operations. Torus and torus-like networks are widely deployed in systems targeting both traditional HPC and machine-learning workloads. Multi-dimensional tori are used in IBM BlueGene/L and its suc-cessors [1]; Fujitsu’s 6D Tofu-D interconnect is used in the Fugaku supercomputer, Wisteria/BDEC-01 (Odyssey), TOKI-SORA, several PRIMEHPC FX1000-based systems, and Nagoya University’s Flow supercomputer [3, 14, 20, 21, 24]. For ML workloads, vendors increasingly favor torus or torus-like fabrics: Google’s TPU v5, v6 and Ironwood systems use 2D/3D tori and optical reconfig-uration to scale to thousands of accelerators [15, 16, 25, 34], AWS Trainium (Trn1, Trn2) uses a 2D torus for chip-to-chip connections [4], Graphcore IPU-PODs implement a dense IPU interconnect with torus structure [18]. The allreduce collective operation combines values from all computing nodes and returns the result to every node. On general interconnects, its cost is typically modeled in the α–β message-cost model [30]. The latency term charges a fixed initiation cost α for each communication round; the 1arXiv:2610.16387v1 [cs.DC] 12 Oct 2026 bandwidth term is proportional to the amount of data transmitted, with β denoting the time to transmit one bit along a link. For small messages, the data-transfer time is short, so the initiation costs of successive rounds can account for a large part of the total time. Reducing the number of rounds is therefore particularly important in this regime. Ring allreduce [29] transmits the minimum total volume, but requires 2( p−1) rounds for p nodes. Classical recursive doubling and halving–doubling [30] require O(log p) communication rounds, but their communication partners may be separated by several physical links on a torus, increasing total link traffic. Different transmissions may also load the same physical links, making the distribution of traffic another factor in the bandwidth term. Section 2 introduces parameters in the α–β model that account for communication volume, physical route lengths, and link-load imbalance. On the one hand, the torus’s limited degree and low bisection bandwidth constrain collective performance; on the other hand, its regular structure favors allreduce algorithms that are explicitly topology-aware. Indeed, there are several allreduce algorithms that were specifically designed for torus networks. Bucket allreduce [5, 22, 31] is a canonical example: it preserves nearest-neighbor communication and is bandwidth-optimal on tori, but it requires a number of rounds that scales linearly with the torus diameter, which can hurt performance for small and medium message sizes. Torus-aware algorithms such as Swing [12] and Trivance [26] retain the logarithmic-round struc-ture while reducing routing overhead through their choice of communication partners. Swing uses pairwise exchanges, whereas Trivance exchanges data with two peers at each step. We introduce SP-allreduce, which on tori can be viewed as an improved version of Bucket. More generally, its tree-symmetrization approach applies to a broader class of topologies with sufficient symmetry. We divide the input vector into chunks and assign them to copies of a base tree obtained using network symmetries. On equal-sided tori, translations, global reflection, and cyclic coordinate permutations form a subgroup of the topology’s automorphism group that can map any directed physical link to any other. Applying these transformations to both the tree and its communication rounds distributes traffic equally across links in every round. We also adapt the construction to HyperX and spine-leaf networks.

# 2 Model and Background 

2.1 Allreduce and its representation 

Allreduce combines values from all computing nodes and returns the combined value to every node. Let p be the number of nodes, each holding a vector of N bits. The reduction combines correspond-ing vector elements using an associative and commutative operator. A common implementation consists of two stages: reduce-scatter, during which the values are collected and combined, and allgather, during which the reduced values are communicated back to every node. 2.3 Torus notation 

The equal-sided torus T dn has vertex set Zdn, with neighbors differing by +1 or −1 in one coordinate. For n ≥ 3, its degree is D = 2 d and its diameter is H = d⌊n/ 2⌋. For the two-dimensional rectangular torus Tm,n , the diameter is H = ⌊m/ 2⌋ + ⌊n/ 2⌋.

2.4 Related work 

We briefly review representative allreduce baselines for torus and torus-like interconnects, compar-ing their communication rounds, transmitted volume, physical routes, and link utilization. Ap-pendix C gives the quantitative comparison in our deficiency model and its derivations. 

Hamiltonian ring allreduce. Ring allreduce [29] performs reduce-scatter followed by allgather along Hamiltonian cycles in 2( p − 1) rounds. When the cycles use physical links, it achieves Ψ = Θ = 1. A balanced multiport implementation runs several rings on separate chunks and distributes traffic equally across physical links in every round, giving Ω = 1. Such an implementation is fully bandwidth-optimal . Its main drawback is the linear number of rounds. 

Bucket algorithm. Bucket [22, 31] performs reduce-scatter and allgather along the coordinate rings of a torus, using only nearest-neighbor exchanges. On T dn , its balanced multiport schedule is fully bandwidth-optimal , with Ψ = Θ = Ω = 1, and requires 2 d(n − 1) rounds. SP-allreduce preserves these bandwidth properties while approximately halving the number of rounds. 

Swing. Swing [12] refines doubling-like allreduce algorithms by choosing partners with shorter physical routes on tori, thereby reducing the multi-hop path deficiency Θ. For n = 2 m, SwingL re-quires log 2 p rounds, while SwingB performs reduce-scatter and allgather in 2 log 2 p rounds. SwingB achieves minimum abstract communication volume, with Ψ = 1, whereas SwingL incurs additional abstract traffic. 5Trivance. Trivance [26] extends logarithmic-round allreduce schemes by using simultaneous ex-changes with two peers to combine contributions from three groups per step. For n = 3 t, TrivanceL requires log 3 p rounds, while TrivanceB performs reduce-scatter and allgather in 2 log 3 p rounds. TrivanceB achieves minimum abstract communication volume, with Ψ = 1, whereas TrivanceL incurs additional abstract traffic. 

Other approaches. A complementary line of work aims to synthesize collective schedules auto-matically for a given accelerator fabric, rather than relying on a fixed closed-form algorithm. Most such systems generate step schedules that explicitly track data dependencies across synchronized rounds and optimize link usage within each round [7, 8, 33]. To keep the search tractable, these ap-proaches use communication sketches in TACCL or symmetry-driven reductions in SyCCL [8, 33]. Greedy synthesizers such as TACOS and MultiTree, as well as tree-based constructions such as Blink, provide further approaches to topology-aware collective design [19, 35, 37]. ForestColl [38] constructs tree-flow schedules via spanning-tree packing, aiming to maximize throughput by balancing aggregate link load over the entire collective rather than enforcing perfectly balanced utilization in each synchronized round. Our analysis instead tracks the round structure and per-round physical-link loads. Finally, a separate line of work co-designs the interconnect itself to improve collective perfor-mance [39]. This direction is orthogonal to our setting, where the topology is fixed. SP-allreduce uses an explicit construction with formal bandwidth and round-count guarantees on equal-sided tori, and adapts the same tree-symmetrization approach to other topologies.

A base construction consists of a rooted spanning tree T over the nodes, a physical path Ruv for every tree edge, and a round assignment σ. The tree is logical: its adjacent vertices need not be adjacent in G. In reduce-scatter, edges point toward the root; in allgather, the aggregate follows the same tree and physical paths in reverse. For a tree of edge depth h, reduce-scatter takes h rounds. In round s, vertices at depth h − s + 1 send to their parents. Let Γ ≤ Aut( G) be a subgroup of the automorphism group of G. For each γ ∈ Γ, apply γ to the tree and its physical routes, retaining the round assigned to each transmission. In particular, a route ( v0, v 1, . . . , v m) becomes ( γ(v0), γ (v1), . . . , γ (vm)). The resulting construction is denoted by (γT, γR, γσ ). Split each input vector into |Γ| equal chunks, one for each γ ∈ Γ, and run the corresponding tree schedules in parallel. Chunks with the same sender, receiver, round, and physical route can be packed into a single message.

# 5 k-SP-allreduce on Tori 

In SP-allreduce, every transmission connects physically adjacent nodes. We now allow nonadjacent nodes to communicate within a single round by routing their transmissions along shortest physical paths. This permits communication trees of smaller depth and therefore fewer rounds. The tree construction retains minimum abstract communication volume, with Ψ = 1, and the same sym-metrization on equal-sided tori gives Ω = 1. Using paths longer than one physical link introduces Θ > 1. The experiments show that this trade-off extends the range of message sizes for which the tree schedules outperform the compared algorithms. A rooted spanning tree is a k-hop tree if every root-to-leaf path contains at most k tree edges. A tree of actual depth h(T ) ≤ k requires h(T ) reduce-scatter rounds followed by h(T ) allgather rounds. # 6 Extensions to Other Topologies 

We adapt the SP-allreduce approach to other topologies by choosing a base communication tree, shortest physical routes for its edges, and a subgroup of network automorphisms. We briefly describe the resulting constructions for rectangular tori, HyperX, and spine-leaf networks. 

6.1 Rectangular tori 

For a two-dimensional rectangular torus Tm,n , use translations and global reflection: Γ = {(x, y ) 7 → (a + εx, b + εy ) : ( a, b ) ∈ Zm × Zn, ε ∈ { +1 , −1}} .

This subgroup of Aut( Tm,n ) has 2 mn elements and two directed-edge orbits: horizontal and vertical links. One base tree need not distribute its traffic equally between these orbits. Two suitably chosen BFS trees suffice. 

Theorem 6.1 (Two-tree balance on rectangular tori) . For every m, n ≥ 3, there exist two BFS trees rooted at (0 , 0) whose families under Γ, with 4mn equal chunks in total, give a fully bandwidth-optimal allreduce schedule in 2H rounds, where H = ⌊m/ 2⌋ + ⌊n/ 2⌋.

The proof is given in Appendix B. The experiments in Section 7.5 use this two-tree construction on all three rectangular tori.

For even n, the orbit with difference n/ 2 has half as many directed links and receives half as many tree edges. Symmetrization therefore gives equal load on all directed links in every round.

# 7 Experimental Evaluation 

We evaluate SP-allreduce on square and rectangular tori and k-SP-allreduce on square tori. Both are compared with state-of-the-art allreduce algorithms for tori. HyperX and spine-leaf experi-ments demonstrate the extension of the method to other topologies, using the torus algorithms as references. We examine how closely the shortest-path schedules approach the bandwidth upper bound, how reducing the tree depth changes the range of message sizes at which they outperform the compared algorithms, and how this range shifts with topology shape, link bandwidth, and hop latency. 

7.1 Methodology 

Simulator and setup. All runs use the Structural Simulation Toolkit (SST) [23], following the setup of [12]. The simulator models routers, links, and queues at packet level. Unless stated otherwise, links carry 400 Gb/s and a hop costs 400 ns (100 ns of link latency and 300 ns of router processing); the reduction itself costs nothing. Each run is one allreduce: every node holds an input vector and starts at the same time. The simulator is deterministic. The square-torus experiments cover 8 × 8, 16 × 16, 32 × 32, and 64 × 64. Figure 3 shows absolute goodput on 8 × 8 and 64 × 64; Figure 2(a) and Table 1 report results for all four sizes. The rectangular tori have 1024 nodes and shapes 64 × 16, 128 × 8, and 256 × 4. We also evaluate HyperX 32 × 32 and a spine-leaf fabric with 16 spines, 32 leaves, and 16 hosts per leaf. On 8 × 8, the bandwidth sweep covers 100, 200, 400, and 800 Gb/s, and 1 .6 and 3 .2 Tb/s; the hop-latency sweep covers 200, 400, 800, and 1600 ns.

7.6 HyperX and spine-leaf 

We follow Swing [12] by conducting experiments on HyperX. We also run Trivance on HyperX using the same approach as for Swing. Figure 5 shows the results on HyperX 32 × 32 and on a spine-leaf fabric with 16 spines and 32 leaves. The evaluated baselines are Swing, Trivance, and Bucket, with Ring and recursive doubling also included on spine-leaf. On HyperX, the 62 links per node give a goodput upper bound of 12 .4 Tb/s at the default link bandwidth. SP-allreduce reaches approximately 11 .76 Tb/s at 256 MiB, or 94 .9% of this bound. Swing, the fastest compared implementation at this size, reaches 0 .794 Tb/s, or 6 .4% of the same bound. The measured goodput ratio between SP-allreduce and Swing is 14 .82 ×.A limitation of the evaluated Swing, Trivance, and Bucket implementations is that they are poorly suited to exploiting HyperX’s connectivity: they use only 4 of the 62 links per node in each round, whereas SP-allreduce uses all 62 links. This difference explains the large goodput gap. In our experiments, Swing reaches approximately 0 .794 Tb/s, close to its four-link goodput limit of 4B/ 2 = 0 .800 Tb/s at the default link bandwidth B.On spine-leaf, each host has one 400 Gb/s link, giving a 200 Gb/s goodput upper bound. SP-allreduce reaches approximately 199 .7 Gb/s at 256 MiB, or 99 .8% of the bound. At large message sizes, Bucket, Swing, and recursive doubling also approach this limit. SP-allreduce’s maximum improvement over the best compared baseline is 1 .72 × at 32 KiB, with approximately 28 .4 Gb/s versus 16 .5 Gb/s for Trivance. 

# 8 Conclusion 

On equal-sided tori, symmetrizing a BFS tree under a group acting transitively on directed phys-ical edges yields minimum abstract communication volume, one-hop transmissions, and perfect per-round load balance. The schedule takes 2 H = 2 d⌊n/ 2⌋ rounds. The minimum-transmission argument gives a sharp lower bound of 2 H for odd side lengths and 2 H − 1 for even side lengths. The described construction is optimal in the first case and uses exactly one additional round in the second. Experimentally, the resulting algorithms improve over the best compared state-of-the-art al-gorithms for tori by up to 1 .19 × on square tori and 1 .65 × on rectangular tori across the tested configurations. On HyperX and spine-leaf, the approach nearly attains the bandwidth upper bound and improves over the best evaluated baselines by up to 14 .82 × and 1 .72 ×, respectively. A.1 A one-chunk information-flow model 

Fix one elementary chunk, corresponding to a single position i of the input vector. Let G be the communication graph and let V = V (G), |V | = p. For a vertex v, let 

Kt(v) ⊆ V

denote the set of original inputs at position i whose aggregate is known at v after round t. Initially 

K0(v) = {v}. In round t + 1, a transmission u → v sends the aggregate over Kt(u), where Kt(v)denotes the state at vertex v at the beginning of the round. At the end of the round, v forms 

Kt+1 (v) by taking the union of Kt(v) and all sets received in that round. The goal of allreduce for this chunk is to reach 

KT (v) = V for every v ∈ V. 

For a state K = {K(v) : v ∈ V }, define its covering number by 

c(K) := min {r : there exist v1, . . . , v r such that K(v1) ∪ · · · ∪ K(vr) = V }.

Thus initially c(K0) = p, while if some vertex knows the full aggregate then c = 1. We shall use the following elementary observation. If one individual transmission is added to a state, then the covering number can decrease by at most one. Indeed, if after the transmission a cover of size c′ uses the newly formed set A ∪ B, then before the transmission one could replace it by the two sets A and B, obtaining a cover of size at most c′ + 1. 

A.2 Transmission lower bound 

Proposition A.1 (Transmission lower bound) . In the above model, any allreduce schedule for one elementary chunk uses at least 2p − 2 transmissions. Proof. Let τ be the first round after which at least one vertex knows the full aggregate V . Put 

q := c(Kτ −1)and let 

r := |{ v ∈ V : Kτ (v) = V }| 

be the number of vertices that first know the full aggregate after round τ . Since no vertex is full before round τ , we have q ≥ 2. Before round τ , the covering number has decreased from p to q. Since each individual trans-mission decreases it by at most one, there must have been at least p − q transmissions before round 

τ .Now consider round τ . Fix a vertex v with Kτ (v) = V . If v receives sv messages in this round, then the sv received sets together with the old set Kτ −1(v) cover V . Therefore sv + 1 ≥ q, and hence sv ≥ q − 1. Summing over the r vertices that become full in round τ , the round contains at least r(q − 1) transmissions. After round τ , there are p − r vertices which do not yet know the full aggregate. Each of them must receive at least one later transmission in order ever to become full. Hence there are at least 

p − r transmissions after round τ .23 Altogether, the total number of transmissions is at least (p − q) + r(q − 1) + ( p − r) = 2 p − q + r(q − 2) = 2 p − 2 + ( r − 1)( q − 2) .

Since r ≥ 1 and q ≥ 2, this is at least 2 p − 2. 

A.3 Structure in the equality case 

Lemma A.2 (Structure in the equality case) . Suppose an allreduce schedule for one elementary chunk uses exactly 2p−2 transmissions. Let τ , q, and r be as in the proof above. Then, immediately before round τ , the inclusion-maximal sets among 

{Kτ −1(v) : v ∈ V }

are pairwise disjoint, form a partition of V , and each maximal set has a unique holder. Moreover, if r > 1, then q = 2 . In this case exactly two vertices can become full in round τ ,and this can only happen by a bidirectional exchange of two complementary maximal aggregates. If transmissions are restricted to physical neighbors, the two holders are adjacent in the topology. Proof. Since the schedule uses exactly 2 p − 2 transmissions, all inequalities in the proof of the proposition must be tight. In particular, before round τ there are exactly p − q transmissions, and these transmissions reduce the covering number from p to q. Hence every one of these transmissions reduces the covering number by exactly one. We now prove by induction over the individual transmissions before round τ that the maximal aggregates are pairwise disjoint and have unique holders. To avoid any ambiguity about simulta-neous transmissions, fix an arbitrary ordering of the transmissions inside each round, but keep the payload of every transmission equal to the aggregate known by the sender at the beginning of that round. Processing the transmissions one by one with these fixed payloads gives the same state at the end of the round, since all updates are unions. Initially the maximal sets are the singletons {v}, and each has a unique holder. Suppose that before some individual transmission the maximal sets are 

P1, . . . , P h,

pairwise disjoint, covering V , and each Pi has a unique holder. Then c = h. Consider a transmission whose payload S is contained in Pi, and whose recipient currently holds a set contained in Pj .We claim that this transmission can decrease c only if 

S = Pi, K(v) = Pj , i̸ = j, 

where v is the recipient. Indeed, if i = j, the transmission only combines information already contained in the same maximal component and cannot reduce the number of components needed to cover V . If S ⊊ Pi, then the part Pi \ S still has to be covered by the unique maximal aggregate 

Pi; the new set does not replace Pi. Similarly, if the recipient holds a proper subset of Pj , then the old maximal aggregate Pj is still needed. Thus the covering number cannot drop unless the whole component Pi is sent to the unique holder of a different whole component Pj .In that case the transmission replaces the two maximal components Pi and Pj by their union 

Pi ∪ Pj . The new maximal components are 

Pi ∪ Pj , Pk (k̸ = i, j ),

24 which are again pairwise disjoint and cover V . The holder of Pi ∪Pj is unique, namely the recipient. The old holder of Pi may still possess Pi, but Pi is now a proper subset of the maximal set Pi ∪ Pj ,so it is no longer a maximal aggregate. Thus the induction continues. Since every pre-τ transmission must decrease the covering number, the induction applies to all transmissions before round τ . Hence, just before round τ , the maximal aggregates are pairwise disjoint, form a partition of V , and have unique holders. It remains to analyze the first-full round. Exact optimality also gives 2p − 2 = 2 p − 2 + ( r − 1)( q − 2) ,

and therefore (r − 1)( q − 2) = 0 .

Thus either r = 1, or q = 2. Assume r > 1. Then q = 2, so immediately before round τ there are exactly two maximal aggregates, say 

A ⊔ B = V, 

with unique holders a and b. Each vertex that becomes full in round τ must receive at least 

q − 1 = 1 message, and by tightness it receives exactly one relevant message. For one received aggregate together with its old aggregate to cover V = A ⊔ B, the vertex must already hold one of A, B and receive the other. Since A and B have unique holders, only a and b can become full in this round. Thus r ≤ 2. If r = 2, then a receives B from b, and b receives A from a, so the two holders exchange their complementary aggregates; under one-hop routing, this exchange uses a physical edge. This proves the final claim.

Lemma A.4 (Dissemination after the first complete aggregate) . Consider the one-chunk model with one-hop transmissions. Suppose an allreduce schedule uses exactly 2p − 2 transmissions. Let 

τ be the first round after which some vertex knows the full aggregate V , and let 

Fτ := {v ∈ V : Kτ (v) = V }.

Then every transmission after round τ is sent by a vertex that is already full at the beginning of that round, and its recipient becomes full at the end of that round. Consequently, every vertex y that is not full at time τ receives the full aggregate along a causal path starting from Fτ . In particular, if the schedule finishes after T rounds, then 

dist G(y, F τ ) ≤ T − τ for every y ∈ V.

Thus fullness propagates after round τ only by transmissions from already full vertices to not-yet-full vertices. Proposition A.6 (Attaining the even-side bound) . For even n ≥ 4, there is a fully bandwidth-optimal allreduce schedule on T dn taking 2H − 1 rounds, where H = dn/ 2.Proof. Let n be even and H = dn/ 2. Choose adjacent nodes a, b of T dn . Their joint eccentricity is H − 1, as established in Corollary A.5. A BFS forest rooted at {a, b } assigns every other node a parent whose distance to this root set is one smaller. It has two components, p − 2 edges, and depth H − 1. Reduce each component to its root in H − 1 rounds, using p − 2 transmissions. The roots then exchange their complementary aggregates in one round, using two transmissions. Both now know the complete result. Disseminating it along the forest takes another H − 1 rounds and p − 2transmissions. The total is 2 H − 1 rounds and 2 p − 2 transmissions, all between physical neighbors. Apply the torus subgroup from Section 3 to this entire schedule, including the forest, the central exchange, and all round assignments, and assign equal chunks to its copies. Thus the even-side lower bound is attained within the fully bandwidth-optimal class.

## Abstract 

As Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models continue to scale, serv-ing them increasingly relies on expert parallelism (EP) across a growing number of devices. Yet skewed expert workloads create imbalance across computation, communication, and memory, making load balancing a central optimization objec-tive in distributed MoE serving. We observe that balance is not free : operations introduced to balance one dimension can themselves be expensive or imbalanced. This motivates us to rethink balance as a con-straint rather than an optimization objective. We present Zepp ,which directly optimizes the bottleneck communication in dis-tributed MoE serving subject to simplified balance constraints on physical resources, i.e., GPUs and NICs. Zepp progres-sively optimizes inter-node communication across placement, routing, and execution. It first places expert replicas to reduce token communication under GPU constraints, then reshapes communication flows through split and merge primitives un-der NIC constraints, and finally partitions and schedules ex-pert computation to overlap the resulting communication. To adapt to dynamic workloads, Zepp jointly coordinates compu-tation, token communication, and expert-weight movement at each iteration. Together, these designs allow Zepp to pursue the most efficient execution rather than a single-dimension balanced one. We implement Zepp and evaluate it against 7

state-of-the-art MoE serving systems, achieving up to 6.68 ×

MoE layer speedup and a geometric mean speedup of 1.86 ×

over the fastest competing baseline.

Serving large MoE models typically relies on expert parallelism (EP), which distributes experts across multi-ple GPUs and routes each token to the GPUs hosting its selected experts [23, 46]. More importantly, dynamic token-to-expert routing creates highly skewed and time-varying work-loads [24, 28, 42]: as shown in Figure 1, different experts receive different numbers of tokens, leading to uneven ex-1arXiv:2610.11158v2 [cs.DC] 9 Oct 2026 pert computation and communication traffic across GPUs and network links. Such imbalance can leave some resources overloaded while others remain idle, making efficient EP execution a central challenge for high-performance MoE serv-ing [19, 24, 43]. Existing systems follow a common principle: better bal-ance leads to better performance. Early approaches balance communication by padding redundant tokens to enforce uni-form communication shapes, at the cost of unnecessary data transfer [19, 23, 44]. More recent systems eliminate such re-dundancy and instead balance traffic across bottleneck re-sources, e.g., by routing tokens across multiple NICs, but introduce explicit routing overheads [22, 38]. To further bal-ance computation and communication, systems replicate hot experts [12,43,45] and may periodically remap experts across devices in response to changing loads [2, 8, 28]. However, expert remapping, in turn, introduces expensive and inher-ently imbalanced communication. Reducing the remapping frequency mitigates this overhead, but evenly dispatching to-kens across expert replicas to maintain runtime balance can still introduce additional inter-node communication. These approaches reveal a fundamental balancing dilemma: eliminating one form of imbalance often introduces another source of imbalance or overhead, making balance itself in-creasingly costly to pursue. Orthogonal techniques, such as communication-computation overlap [2, 4, 5, 7, 44] and attention-FFN disaggregation [40,49], can mitigate the perfor-mance impact of imbalance, but do not fundamentally resolve this dilemma. More importantly, we observe that better bal-ance does not necessarily translate into better performance, particularly when achieving balance requires additional inter-node communication. This motivates us to rethink the role of balance in distributed MoE serving: rather than treating balance as the optimization objective, we enforce it only as a constraint on physical resources, i.e., GPUs and NICs, and directly minimize the bottleneck inter-node communication. We present Zepp , a distributed MoE serving system that di-rectly optimizes bottleneck inter-node communication while treating GPU and NIC balance as resource constraints. ## 2 Background 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) has become an increasingly promi-nent architecture in modern large language models. Unlike dense models that activate the same parameters for every token, MoE models sparsely activate only a subset of parame-ters organized into experts . Consequently, increasing model capacity does not necessarily require a proportional increase in per-token computation, enabling substantially larger param-eter counts [11, 15, 39]. Recent models illustrate this: Kimi K3 scales to 2.8 trillion total parameters while activating only 104 billion parameters per token [21]. To determine the set of activated experts, a learned router dynamically selects the top-k experts to process each token ,making token-to-expert routing an intrinsic part of MoE ex-ecution [23, 39]. To scale large MoE layers across multiple GPUs, expert parallelism (EP) distributes experts across dif-ferent GPUs [37]. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting selection and movement of tokens: during dispatch , tokens travel to GPUs hosting their selected experts, where computation is performed. The outputs are then returned during combine and aggregated at each token’s source. When EP strategies are deployed to multiple nodes, their to-ken traffic is mapped onto a heterogeneous hierarchy of GPU interconnects. Intra-node GPUs communicate through high-bandwidth, low-latency scale-up fabrics such as NVLink and NVSwitch [31]. Inter-node GPUs rely on NICs and scale-out networks with much lower bandwidth and higher communi-cation overhead. These communication paths utilize distinct data-movement resources: CUDA peer-to-peer copies can uti-lize dedicated copy engines for intra-node data transfers [35], while NVSHMEM provides fine-grained data transfer op-erations that utilize NICs to perform inter-node data trans-fers [32]. By utilizing resources separate from the GPU’s compute units, these mechanisms create opportunities to over-lap communication with computation. Dynamic routing decisions therefore naturally produce im-balance, whose effects are shaped by the underlying hetero-geneous interconnects. Uneven token assignments across ex-perts create both varying communication volumes between GPUs and uneven computation loads on GPUs hosting those experts [10,15,24]. Figure 2 shows both effects in play: Expert 4 receives four tokens and depends on tokens delivered across nodes from remote GPUs, whilst Expert 3 is not activated in this layer at all. As routing layer decisions are generated inde-pendently at each MoE layer, these imbalances shift across tokens and layers throughout model execution. 

## 3 Motivation 

Compared with dense distributed serving, MoE serving intro-duces two unique challenges: load imbalance from dynamic expert activation and communication planning under irregular traffic. 

## 3.1 Imbalance is Shifted, Not Eliminated 

Existing MoE serving systems increasingly optimize imbal-ance across different resources. However, we observe that balancing one dimension often shifts the bottleneck to an-other rather than eliminating it. • Communication overlap. As shown in Figure 3(a), vanilla EP suffers from both expensive inter-node token communi-cation and imbalanced expert computation. Existing over-lap techniques partition communication and computation to pipeline their execution, effectively hiding part of the com-munication latency. However, the skewed expert workload remains, leaving the critical path dominated by straggling expert computation (Figure 3(b)). Thus, reducing the im-pact of communication imbalance can expose computation imbalance as the next bottleneck. • Computation balancing. Expert replication and remap-ping further balance expert computation by redistributing hot experts across GPUs. However, moving expert weights introduces additional inter-node communication, which can become the new bottleneck even after computation is well balanced (Figure 3(c)). ## 3.2 Split, or Merge? 

Dense-model overlap typically splits large communication operations to enable pipelining. However, MoE’s sparse and irregular communication makes finer granularity not always beneficial. • Redundant Tokens. In dense communication, different chunks typically carry distinct data, so splitting primarily changes granularity without duplicating payloads. In MoE, however, multiple experts selected by the same token may reside on the same remote node. Sending their requests separately repeatedly transfers the same token. As shown in Figure 4(a–b), merging such transfers allows the shared token to cross the network only once. • Imbalanced NIC Traffic. Dense collectives typically dis-tribute regular traffic across participating ranks, allowing fine-grained splitting to expose overlap without substan-tially changing traffic balance. MoE routing instead pro-duces highly skewed traffic across GPUs and NICs. As shown in Figure 4(c–d), splitting and locally redistribut-ing traffic can alleviate bottleneck NICs, while subsequent merging removes redundant transfers. • Message Granularity. As EP scales to more devices, each GPU serves fewer tokens and individual MoE transfers be-come smaller, making per-message latency increasingly sig-nificant. Unlike dense communication, where large transfers can amortize startup latency, aggressively splitting these already-small MoE messages further increases the number of latency-dominated transfers. ## 4 System Design 

Zepp directly optimizes the bottleneck inter-node communi-cation of MoE layers while treating GPU compute and NIC traffic balance as resource constraints. Figure 5 summarizes the design. First, under GPU compute load constraints, Zepp 

jointly coordinates expert replication, placement, and token routing to avoid inter-node transfers whenever local replica capacity permits (§4.1). For the remaining transfers, Zepp 

uses split and merge to restructure and reduce communication under NIC traffic constraints, and reschedules expert compu-tation around the resulting communication flows to overlap communication with computation (§4.2). Finally, as dynamic expert demand shifts GPU compute loads, Zepp uses intra-node expert swaps to restore the load constraints without introducing inter-node weight transfers (§4.3). ## 4.1 Expert Placement and Token Routing 

We first optimize expert placement and token routing, as they reduce inter-node communication without introducing ad-ditional communication operations. By changing the num-ber of replicated experts, where expert replicas reside at the beginning, and which replica serves each token at each forward round, Zepp reduces remote token transfers using only the communication already required by MoE execution. We therefore prioritize this optimization before introducing more active communication transformations. These decisions jointly determine GPU compute load and token traffic. Zepp 

preserves the model’s logical expert selections and routing weights while choosing their physical execution locations, without changing the original algorithm. Considering expert placement and token routing indepen-dently can lead to expensive token assignments even when replica loads are balanced. A placement may balance expected compute load across GPUs while clustering popular experts within a few nodes, providing limited node-level expert cover-age for local token dispatch. Conversely, evenly distributing each source rank’s tokens across expert replicas can introduce unnecessary remote transfers even when local assignments can satisfy the replica load targets. We therefore treat expert-replica load balance as a constraint rather than the routing objective: among assignments satisfying the load constraints, we prefer those with lower communication cost. 

Expert replication and placement. Zepp replicates experts based on historical demand while prioritizing expert cover-age across nodes. In addition to the original slots for evenly distributed expert weights, we reserve R extra slots on each GPU for expert replicas. We greedily assign these slots to the expert with the highest historical token demand per replica, updating its demand per replica after each replication, until all available slots are filled. After determining the number of replicas, Zepp places them hierarchically. ## 4.2 Overlapping Scheduling 

Although expert placement and locality-preferred token rout-ing reduce inter-node communication volume, the remaining communication can still bottleneck MoE execution. Its per-formance depends not only on the total traffic volume, but also on how traffic is distributed across the multiple NICs of each node. An overloaded NIC can dominate communica-tion latency even when the aggregate traffic volume is small. We therefore introduce split and merge operations to restruc-ture communication flows under NIC balance constraints, and schedule expert computation around the resulting commu-nication dependencies to overlap inter-node transfers with computation. 

Split . Conventional EP expresses token dispatch as an all-to-all collective, coupling many token transfers into a single communication operation. Zepp first splits the all-to-all into independently schedulable send-receive flows according to their source and destination nodes. This decomposition ex-poses the underlying traffic and allows Zepp to explicitly control which NIC carries each inter-node transfer. However, the resulting flows can remain highly imbalanced across NICs because dynamic expert routing generates different amounts of outgoing traffic on different GPUs. Zepp therefore fur-ther splits and redistributes these flows across local GPUs to satisfy NIC load constraints. We first analyze the traffic between a pair of nodes to de-termine how its outgoing load should be distributed across the available NICs. Let Vi denote the original outgoing traffic from GPU i toward a destination node, and xi j the amount of this traffic eventually transmitted through the NIC attached to GPU j. When i = j, the traffic directly uses its original NIC; when i̸ = j, the corresponding payload is first transferred from GPU i to GPU j over NVLink and then sent through GPU j’s NIC. Merge . NIC-aware splitting exposes flexible physical paths, but can also produce fine-grained transfers and preserve re-dundant payloads across independent flows. Zepp therefore 

merges compatible flows after redistribution, without chang-ing the NIC assignment determined above. During dispatch, multiple experts selected by the same to-ken may reside on the same destination node. Sending their requests independently would transfer the same token acti-vation multiple times across the network. Zepp merges such requests into a shared inter-node transfer: the token crosses the network once and reaches an initial receiver side GPU. It is then responsible for forwarding tokens over NVLink to the GPUs hosting experts that require them (Algorithm 1, lines 8–11). As illustrated in Figure 4(b), this replaces re-peated inter-node transfers with a single remote transfer and inexpensive node-local forwarding. In addition, compatible small flows sharing the same communication path can be coalesced into a larger transfer, reducing the number of com-munication operations and amortizing per-transfer latency. 

Merge is applied differently during combine. Expert out-puts are distinct and therefore cannot be deduplicated. Instead, 

Zepp locally accumulates the weighted expert outputs belong-ing to the same token into a node-local partial sum. Once all local contributions are available, only the partial sum is transferred back to the source node. This replaces multiple expert-level return transfers with one node-level transfer while preserving every expert’s contribution. 

Split and merge restructure inter-node communication into independently schedulable flows, but also introduce explicit dependencies between communication and expert computa-tion. Zepp jointly schedules these flows and their dependent computation to minimize communication exposed on the criti-cal path. The key is that dispatch and combine exhibit opposite dependencies: during dispatch, communication makes expert inputs ready for computation; during combine, computation makes node-local partial sums ready for communication. Zepp 

therefore makes computation follow communication during dispatch, but drive communication during combine. Figure 7 illustrates the resulting schedule, and Algorithm 1 summa-rizes the procedure; its after ready clauses express data dependencies, allowing communication and computation in separate loops to overlap. 

Dispatch follows communication. During dispatch, source tokens are immediately available, so Zepp first initiates the communication flows determined by the split /merge plan P .These flows progress in rounds across node pairs, while intra-node redistribution and forwarding realize their assigned phys-ical paths, with local fan-out ordered cyclically across GPUs (Algorithm 1, lines 8–11). As soon as all inputs of a computa-tion group q arrive, the group becomes eligible for execution (lines 12–13); it does not wait for unrelated inputs of the same expert. The computation order follows the planned delivery or-der rather than expert identity. Split allows different input groups to become ready independently, while merge makes a shared group ready after its inter-node transfer and subse-quent local forwarding complete. Zepp prioritizes computa-tion groups according to this communication order, allowing already-delivered groups to execute while later groups remain in transit. In Figure 7, Node 0 receives inputs from Node 1 in Round 1 and processes them on experts E0–E3 in Round 2. These experts thus execute a delivered input group while Node 0 receives the next group from Node 2.

## 4.3 Three Stream Scheduling 

The preceding optimizations exploit a relatively stable expert placement to reduce inter-node communication. However, as token demand changes across batches, the historical place-ment may no longer satisfy the GPU compute-load constraint. Globally remapping experts could restore balance, but would introduce expensive inter-node weight transfers and disrupt the locality established by placement and routing. Zepp in-stead performs lightweight intra-node expert swaps , which adjust GPU-level compute load while preserving each expert’s node-level coverage. Figure 8 illustrates this process. 

Rebalancing under fixed node-level coverage. The initial placement spreads replicas of each expert across nodes (§4.1), providing multiple node-local opportunities for subsequent adjustment. When the estimated GPU load exceeds the al-lowed imbalance, Zepp searches for swaps between experts on overloaded and underloaded GPUs within the same node. ## 5 Implementation 

Zepp is implemented in C++ and CUDA [35] with Python bindings and uses CUTLASS [29] grouped GEMM kernels for expert computation. NVSHMEM [32] provides inter-node RDMA, while CUDA streams and NVSHMEM synchroniza-tion primitives coordinate transfers with expert computation. 

Routing. CUDA kernels translate expert demand and place-ment into locality-preferred token assignments that satisfy integer-rounded per-replica load bounds. A greedy water-filling procedure constructs these assignments by considering replicas on the source GPU first, followed by other GPUs within the same node and then remote nodes. Each allocation is limited by the receiving replica’s remaining capacity while reserving sufficient unassigned demand to satisfy the other replicas’ lower bounds. Starting from these feasible assign-ments, a greedy refinement consolidates each token’s remote expert requests onto fewer nodes while preserving the same load bounds. 

Expert Swapping. Expert swapping uses a greedy intra-node search that pairs heavily and lightly loaded GPUs, accept-ing exchanges only when they reduce the pair’s maximum estimated load. Together, routing, swap planning, and associ-ated metadata exchange and construction account for approx-imately 5% of iteration latency (§6.6). 

Transport. Dispatch implements union forwarding by having each receiving node GPU forward complete received tokens to all GPUs within the destination node. Expert GEMMs select their required rows from these tokens through gather indices, eliminating recipient-specific forwarding-index con-struction and repacking. These transfers use NVSHMEM symmetric staging buffers to hold intermediate payloads for dispatch redistribution and combine aggregation. The buffers are allocated during initialization and reused across iterations.

## 6.6 Case Study 

Figure 13 shows how Zepp overlaps token communication and intra-node expert swaps with computation under different demand conditions. We contrast a Prof. law iteration from the rotating workload, whose placement history excludes Prof. law, with LiveCodeBench served using history from the same workload. Each profiled iteration starts from the static placement to expose expert swaps. Under Prof. law, Zepp shows intra-node expert swaps on NVLink and overlaps these transfers with expert computation. The selected ranks accumulate 24.2 and 46.7 ms of expert GEMM execution, illustrating the uneven execution asso-ciated with this demand shift. To help alleviate the skew, intra-node swaps can be seen on the timeline occupying the NVLink resource, keeping expert-weight traffic off inter-node links. The resulting weight-copy bursts overlap both GEMMs, while inter-node dispatch overlaps the first GEMM: schedul-ing ready expert work ahead of swapped-expert work allows token delivery, weight movement, and computation to pro-ceed concurrently. Expert outputs then enter combine as they become ready, although residual imbalance remains visible in late completion, consistent with the intra-node scope of rebalancing. Under LiveCodeBench, Zepp combines more balanced ex-pert execution with overlap across dispatch, expert move-ment, and combine. The selected ranks accumulate 23.8 and 32.8 ms of expert GEMM execution, consistent with place-ment and routing matching current demand. These compu-tation windows accommodate dispatch alongside the first GEMM, allowing ready token groups to enter computation while remaining transfers proceed. Alongside token delivery, the much smaller intra-node expert swaps overlap their corre-sponding GEMMs and inter-node transfers. As expert outputs become ready, Zepp overlaps their pre-reduction for merge and inter-node return transfers with the second GEMM; late-arriving contributions and their final top-k reduction form the remaining tail. 

## 7 Related Work 

Communication optimizations. Communication optimiza-tions improve data movement and network utilization. FAST [22] rebalances skewed all-to-all traffic over fast intra-node links and schedules inter-node transfers to avoid incast. FuseLink [38] redirects traffic through intra-node connections to exploit otherwise idle NICs. SwiftEP [25] reduces MoE communication overhead through buffer fusion and TMA offloading. We introduce split and merge as graph transfor-mations that jointly restructure MoE token transfers and their synchronization dependencies across intra- and inter-node links. 

Computation load balancing. Expert replication and place-ment distribute computation under skewed routing. EPLB [12] 12 replicates popular experts and packs them onto GPUs ac-cording to estimated loads; its hierarchical policy also co-locates expert groups to reduce inter-node traffic. EPIC [2] uses communication-aware expert placement and replica-tion, pipelines communication and computation across expert groups, and eliminates duplicate transfers. CRAFT [45] allo-cates replicas across layers according to their expected benefit under a memory budget. MoonEP [8] uses current routing decisions to plan redundant experts and prefetch their weights for balanced execution. Our placement spreads hot-expert replicas across nodes and couples them with locality-preferred token routing under per-GPU load constraints. 

Computation–communication overlap. Overlap exposes computation before all communication. NVSHMEM [32] pro-vides fine-grained, GPU-initiated one-sided communication and synchronization within CUDA kernels, enabling persis-tent kernels to interleave communication with computation. COMET [44] resolves fine-grained producer–consumer de-pendencies to overlap communication with tiled GEMMs. FlashMoE [1] fuses dispatch, expert computation, and com-bine into a persistent GPU kernel with tile-level scheduling and device-initiated transfers. StreamEP [26] removes collec-tive barriers during MoE decoding through per-layer queues and asynchronous rebatching. Our scheduler coordinates ex-pert computation with the dependencies produced by split and 

merge .

Collective communication libraries. Collective libraries provide reusable communication interfaces and execution mechanisms. NCCL [30] provides topology-aware collective and point-to-point communication across GPUs and nodes. MSCCL++ [18] exposes low-level communication primitives, a DSL, and a collective API for portable GPU communi-cation. DeepEP [46] specializes dispatch and combine ker-nels for expert parallelism. Related scheduling work includes SyCCL [3], which exploits collective and topology symmetry, and HeteCCL [16], which synthesizes schedules for heteroge-neous clusters. ForestColl [47] constructs throughput-optimal broadcast and aggregation schedules, while ResCCL [27] optimizes primitive execution and GPU resource allocation. PReCCL [9] reallocates collective work using network teleme-try, and Theseus [14] supports runtime replacement of collec-tive schedules. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We presented Zepp , a distributed MoE serving system that directly optimizes bottleneck inter-node communication. By treating GPU and NIC balance as resource constraints, Zepp 

addresses the communication overhead introduced by pursu-ing balance independently. Under these constraints, it com-bines expert replication, placement, and routing with commu-nication reduction and communication–computation overlap, while adapting to changing demand. Evaluation against 7 baselines on 4–16 nodes shows up a geometric mean speedup of 1 .86 × over the fastest competing baseline, while weak-scaling experiments demonstrate sustained gains on up to 32 nodes.

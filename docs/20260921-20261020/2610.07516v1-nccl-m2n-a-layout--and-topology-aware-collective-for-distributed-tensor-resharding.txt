# NCCL M2N: A Layout- and Topology-Aware Collective for Distributed Tensor Resharding 

Kaushik Kandadi, Youngeun Kwon, Sreeram Potluri, Ching-Hsiang Chu, Ke Wen, Pouya Kousha, Sangkug Lym, Nitin Nitin, Manjunath Gorentla Venkata 

NVIDIA Corporation

Abstract —Distributed deep learning training partitions tensors across a process mesh according to a parallelism configuration, producing a corresponding sharding and replication layout .Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) often uses different layouts for policy training and rollout generation; crossing this boundary requires resharding from one layout to another: the M-to-N layout redistribution problem, in which M

source ranks collectively hold a global tensor in one layout and 

N destination ranks need it in another. This pattern is absent from today’s collective libraries: flat direct sends duplicate every source contribution across destination replicas, while gather-then-broadcast concentrates traffic at one root and over-delivers the full tensor. These workarounds are costly: with legacy all-gather plus broadcast, RL weight sync accounts for 29.4% of the reported RL step time for DeepSeek-V3 at 256 GPUs. We present NCCL M2N, a collective primitive implemented in NCCL Extensions for cross-layout sharded-tensor transfer. It automatically derives the transfer schedule from the layout endpoints . Compared with flat direct sends and gather-then-broadcast, the collective provides: (1) layout-derived traffic — only bytes required by the destination layout are transferred; (2) load-balanced NIC usage — source contributions are distributed across eligible destination leaders; and (3) topology-aware routing — replicated fan-out stays within an NVLink domain while a pipelined ring carries one copy between domains. We evaluate NCCL M2N on up to 256 GB200 GPUs in an NVL72 cluster with NDR InfiniBand. On a single FFN-MoE layer transfer, NCCL M2N achieves up to 7.9× speedup over flat direct sends (9.8 ms vs. 77.3 ms), closely tracking the aggregate data-movement bound. In a non-colocated DeepSeek-V3 NeMo-RL run, NCCL M2N reduces the reported weight-sync time from 5.78 s to 2.77 s ( 2.09 ×), reducing step time by 12.7% over legacy all-gather plus broadcast.

Modern large language model (LLM) workflows routinely require resharding tensors from one layout to another as parallelism changes across phases. In such an M-to-N layout reshard operation, M source ranks collectively hold a tensor in one layout and N destination ranks require it in another. The runtime must derive the overlapping tensor regions, deliver each region to every required replica, and coordinate ranks that do not belong to the same process group. RLVR-style training exposes this operation directly: it alternates rollout generation and policy updates, often using different parallel layouts for the generator and trainer. After each update, policy weights must be resharded and transferred across the process-group boundary before the next rollout begins. We refer to this synchronization phase as weight refit . Recent frameworks (NeMo-RL [7], OpenRLHF [8], and HybridFlow/verl [9]) expose such a phase. The RL weight refit step, which transfers updated policy weights from trainer to generator after each training step, is the most costly instance of this pattern. It becomes one of the major performance bottlenecks as it sits directly on the critical path, where generators cannot begin the next rollout batch until new weights arrive. Our profiling of DeepSeek-V3 at 256 GPUs showed that legacy all-gather plus broadcast weight sync accounted for 29.4% of the reported RL step time (Table I), a substantial share of the end-to-end step. Despite this cost, today’s communication stack does not express this cross-group resharding operation natively. (2) A topology-aware routing optimization that removes inter-node replica redundancy, balances NIC injection under shard imbalance, and performs local fan-out over NVLink while forwarding one copy across domains (Sec-tion §V). (3) An evaluation of the hierarchical route on an NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 cluster. The measured route achieved up to a 7.9× speedup for a single FFN-MoE layer transfer (9.8 ms versus 77.3 ms). II. B ACKGROUND 

A. RL Training Loop and Refit 

Reinforcement-learning fine-tuning alternates between roll-out generation, policy training, and weight synchronization. The policy uses a parallel configuration optimized for training throughput and a separate generation configuration optimized for rollout throughput. At each synchronization point, updated weights move from the training layout to the generation layout. We call this in-memory transfer phase refit ; it is an M-to-N layout redistribution [10]. Refit combines different tensor layouts with communica-tion between potentially disjoint process groups and with destination-side replication. Because it runs between training and the next rollout, its transfer schedule affects the iteration critical path. The following subsections describe how frame-works express these layouts and why existing communication abstractions do not directly realize the transfer. 

B. Distributed Tensor Layouts 

Modern distributed-tensor frameworks can describe a global tensor with a process mesh and a per-tensor placement . A placement maps each mesh axis either to a tensor dimen-sion (sharding) or to replication. PyTorch’s DT ENSOR [4], GSPMD [5], and Alpa [6] follow this model. For the ma-terialized parameter tensors considered here, we use S HARD 

and R EPLICATE placements. Given a global shape S, a mesh 

M , and a placement list p whose entries are S HARD (d) or REPLICATE , each rank’s local shape and offset are determined deterministically. We call the tuple (S, M, p ) a layout .A reshard converts a tensor from a source layout 

(S, M src , p src ) to a destination layout (S, M dst , p dst ). When the source and destination groups contain M and N ranks, respectively, we call this an M-to-N layout redistribution . The two groups can use different shard dimensions, shard counts, and replication factors, and can be disjoint. The runtime must therefore derive which source regions overlap each destination region instead of relying on a fixed participant pattern. Tensor Parallelism (TP) and Expert Parallelism (EP) shard tensor and expert dimensions, respectively. Pipeline Paral-lelism (PP) assigns successive layers to different stages, while Data Parallelism (DP) replicates a model or tensor. Combining these strategies gives training and generation layouts with different ownership and replication patterns. In particular, TP and EP may shard different dimensions, so a reshard can require intersecting multi-dimensional regions rather than copying one rank’s contiguous slice.

M OTIVATION AND DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS 

A. Limitations of Existing Approaches 

Cross-layout transfers are often assembled from existing lower-level communication operations, with two primary lim-itations: (i) flat direct sends duplicate every source contribution across destination replicas; and (ii) gather-then-broadcast avoids source-side duplication but concentrates traffic at one root and over-delivers the full tensor, wasting network bandwidth. The missing capability is therefore not another send operation. The runtime needs one collective that jointly plans the M-to-N redistribution from both endpoint layouts, identifies the overlapping tensor slices, maps each slice to source and destination byte offsets, and accounts for the communication topology. 

B. Layout-Derived Schedule and Hierarchical Route 

We define a layout-derived schedule as a transfer plan computed at call time from the source and destination layout descriptors together with the runtime topology. The descriptors determine which tensor regions overlap and which destination replicas require each region. The topology determines which leaders and links carry those regions. For the layout class formalized in Section IV, the hi-erarchical route provides three properties. First, it removes replica-multiplied source egress by injecting each required contribution once and forwarding one copy through each destination domain. Second, it balances the number of source contributions assigned to each eligible leader rather than concentrating them on one receiver. Third, it keeps replica forwarding within an NVLink domain and uses inter-domain links only for the copies that must cross them. These properties eliminate replica-multiplied network traffic and single-ingress over-delivery while enabling global topology-aware load bal-ancing and preserving the semantics of the supported M-to-N layouts (Section IV). B. Hierarchical Topology Route 

The schedule above maps each source rank to overlapping destination shards and, conversely, each destination shard to its unique source contributions. Flat direct sends transmit every contribution separately to all replicas of its destination shard. The hierarchical route instead balances contributions across selected replica leaders: each contribution enters through one leader, is ring-forwarded across domains, and is replicated locally. Among K destination shards, let j identify one, qj its number of unique source contributions, and ρ its local replicas per domain. Ordered by source shard, each replica receives base load aj = ⌊qj /ρ ⌋; the first bj = qj mod ρ receive one extra. Replicas with nonzero loads become leaders and forward their contributions to the other ρ − 1 replicas, so loads differ by at most one. D. Implementation and Availability 

The initial release (v0.2) supports one- and two-dimensional process meshes, one- to three-dimensional tensors, and at most one sharded mesh axis per layout. It realizes the derived sched-ule through two hierarchical transports. PACK gathers strided contributions into contiguous staging buffers, executes the hierarchical transfer, and scatters them into destination buffers. PIPE, currently beta, pipelines bounded chunks through the same route using device-side or host-driven remote-memory-access (RMA) progress. Both transports preserve caller-stream ordering. NCCL M2N is released as the nccl_m2n compo-nent of the open-source NVIDIA NCCL Extensions reposi-tory. 1 It provides C and packaged Python interfaces for layout-derived resharding over ordinary CUDA buffers; the repository documents the current API, build, configuration, and transport details. A. Experimental Setup 

Experiments use a GB200 NVL72 cluster with NDR InfiniBand (50 GB/s per NIC per direction). The sin-gle FFN-MoE layer experiment uses NCCL Extensions’ 

reshard_model_bench to isolate a 7168 MB BF16 ex-pert gate-projection transfer. A 16-GPU EP trainer group occupies one NVLink domain, while a TP8 generator group with DP ∈ { 2, 4, 8} (16–64 GPUs) occupies a separate do-main. This expert-sharded-to-tensor-parallel transfer represents a dominant MoE weight-movement pattern. The end-to-end experiment uses DeepSeek-V3 in BF16 on 256 GPUs across 64 nodes of the same cluster. The 128 trainer GPUs span two NVL72 domains, and the 128 non-colocated generator GPUs span two additional domains, with 64 active GPUs per domain. Trainer parallelism is TP=1, PP=8, EP=16; gen-erator parallelism is TP=16, DP=8. Single-layer latency is the maximum across ranks of each rank’s average over 10 timed iterations following three warmups; every point also passes three correctness-validation iterations. The flat direct-send baseline sends from each active source rank to every final destination replica independently. 

Measurement provenance.: The single FFN-MoE layer measurements were rerun with NCCL 2.31.0-1 and the NCCL Extensions model benchmark. They compare the flat direct-send baseline with the hierarchical route. The end-to-end experiment compares NCCL M2N against NeMo-RL’s legacy all-gather-plus-broadcast path under the same non-colocated asynchronous RL configuration. It uses one-step off-policy execution and sequence length 1536. End-to-end metrics are reported for non-warmup execution and are labeled as step time and weight-sync time.

Distributed-tensor abstractions and RL training systems.: 

PyTorch DTensor [4], GSPMD [5], and Alpa [6] make layout a first-class abstraction but provide no cross-mesh collective. torchstore [14] and PyTorch Monarch [15] both bridge meshes through DTensor-aware transfer interfaces. torchstore additionally provides topology-based transport se-lection through a put/get interface. NCCL M2N addresses a different layer by deriving one global schedule from both end-point layouts and coordinating all shard groups jointly. Ten-sorHub [16] layers a storage abstraction (Reference-Oriented Storage) above pair-wise transport, tracking where weights live and serving topology-optimized reads on demand; NCCL M2N shares this goal of topology-awareness but operates at a different layer — a single collective call with a global, layout-derived schedule rather than a directory of per-replica fetches. NeMo-RL [7], OpenRLHF [8], HybridFlow/veRL [9], and LlamaRL [17] each implement their own refit phase using a mix of NCCL broadcast, CUDA IPC, and one-sided DMA — efficient for their target setup, but treating refit as opaque pair-wise sends rather than as a planned topology-aware collective. NCCL M2N provides the missing primitive in this stack: an NCCL-native collective for cross-layout sharded-tensor transfer that derives a single global schedule from two (mesh, placement) endpoints at call time and plans jointly across the interconnect hierarchy — a drop-in replacement for refit and other cross-layout transfers that preserves each framework’s process-group structure. 

Programmable communication abstractions.: 

MSCCL++ [18] exposes channel and signal abstractions over NVLink and RDMA; NVSHMEM [19] provides OpenSHMEM-style one-sided primitives. The GPU-Initiated Networking interface for NCCL [12] unifies NVLink and RDMA paths in NCCL’s device API and is the foundation our data plane builds on. NCCLX [20] scales standard collectives to 100K+ GPUs without introducing layout-aware primitives. NCCL M2N contributes the layer above these abstractions: a layout-derived schedule that tells GIN/LSA/multimem what 

to move and which path to use. 

Offline checkpoint resharding.: Universal Checkpoint-ing [21] saves a parallelism-agnostic checkpoint and recon-structs any target layout on load; ByteCheckpoint [22] dis-aggregates data and metadata for load-time resharding with 

∼3.6 × speedup over manual scripts. Both target the file-system path across runs, while NCCL M2N targets in-memory transfer between two live process groups within a single run — the two approaches are complementary. VIII. D ISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK 

NCCL M2N establishes a deployable API and several transport choices, but the following directions remain open. 

Multi-dimensional and higher-rank sharding.: The cur-rent implementation permits at most one sharded mesh axis per layout. Extending schedule derivation to support simul-taneous sharding across multiple dimensions — and meshes with more than two axes — would cover arbitrary GSPMD-style placements and three-way parallelism combinations (e.g., TP ×EP ×DP) natively, without composing multiple two-dimensional meshes. 

Elastic communicator APIs.: Production RL systems in-creasingly resize their rollout clusters dynamically. Emerging NCCL extensions for shrink and grow communicators allow process group membership to change without a full teardown and reinitialize cycle. Integrating these APIs into NCCL M2N would allow the reshard schedule to be updated in-place as the source or destination mesh changes size, making NCCL M2N a natural weight-transfer primitive for elastic RL training loops. 

Adaptive transport selection.: Contiguous staging and device- or host-driven remote-memory access expose different storage and startup costs. A policy can choose among them per transfer using contiguity, tensor size, peer count, staging pressure, and whether several compatible calls can be grouped. 8Such a policy should retain the current explicit environment override for reproducible evaluation. 

Overlapping meshes and recovery.: Multi-rank source and destination meshes are currently disjoint, and protocol errors after host-RMA entry require communicator-level shut-down. Supporting overlapping membership would broaden in-place layout transitions; distributed failure agreement would allow safe recovery instead of the current fail-stop contract. Both require protocol changes beyond descriptor validation. 

On-the-fly quantization.: Quantization is commonly ap-plied to model weights before transfer to reduce bandwidth consumption. Rather than quantizing weights as a separate pass before resharding, NCCL M2N’s staging kernel can be extended to apply quantization transformations in-flight as chunks move through the staging ring — fusing the two opera-tions into a single kernel pass and eliminating the intermediate quantized buffer. IX. C ONCLUSION 

We presented NCCL M2N, a collective primitive for trans-ferring distributed tensors between different source and desti-nation layouts. By deriving the transfer schedule from both layouts, it sends only the tensor regions required by each destination and balances network traffic across destination replicas. Its hierarchical route avoids the repeated network transfers of flat direct sends and the full-tensor gathering of gather-then-broadcast, using NVLink to complete replication within each destination domain. On a GB200 NVL72 cluster, NCCL M2N achieved up to 7.9× speedup over flat direct sends for a single FFN-MoE layer transfer. In a separate 256-GPU DeepSeek-V3 NeMo-RL experiment, it reduced weight-sync time from 5.78 s to 2.77 s—a 2.09 × speedup over legacy all-gather plus broadcast—and reduced step time by 12.7%.
